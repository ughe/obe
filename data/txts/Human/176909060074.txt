The young gentleman won the ten guineas; he called right all the three times. Upon that the prisoner said, Gentlemen, you seem to have very bad luck, be in spirits, you will win all his money before he has done. With a great deal of persuasion, I went two guineas more; and the farmer he put in two guineas. We lost that time. Then I went a moidore. The prisoner said, So you seem to be rather out of spirits; why don't you go all at once? You will either get a great deal, or lose. You are sure of winning. He wanted me to put in all my crown pieces, and all my silver I had. After I lost my moidore, I seemed to be rather vexed, and out of spirits. I thought these must be a set of false fellows, that knew one another. The prisoner said, I have a friend, a woollen-draper, that lives in the Poultry; I can have seven or eight guineas there, and I will try my luck with him, and you shall see what we can do with him. Then the prisoner, as well as me, gave the farmer a strict charge to keep the young gentleman in the room till we came back, as he had won so much money. Then we went to go to the Poultry, but the prisoner said, We will go in here; at the King's Head tavern, by the Mansion-house. We will have half a pint of wine, and I will pay for the wine: it is but half a dozen doors higher. You need not go there, I will go myself. There he left me. I did not stay to drink the wine. I went back to the King's Arms in Cornhill again, but the young gentleman and the farmer were gone, and had taken my cane; there came the waiter, who said, I hope them gamblers have not taken you in? I thought they had been breaking you in as one of their gang. When I told him the circumstances, said he, I knew them to be a set of rogues in many places where I have lived. I applied to my Lord Mayor, and got a warrant, made special for Whitechapel way and for the Borough, and employed Justice Fielding's men. It has cost me above fourteen guineas to take these people. Also I walked up and down myself to see for them. I took the prisoner myself half a year after in the Strand. I was with the chariot coming to town with my master. I knew him as soon as I saw him. I carried him to my master's house in Lombard-street. When he came there, he desired to go to the necessary: he looked round him when there, and said, There is no place to get out; you need not stand by me. I said, I will not leave you. He took this purse out of his pocket, and dropped it down the water closet. I thought I heard some noise, so as soon as he got off the necessary, I took it out. (Produced a net-work purse with a large quantity of yellow counters in it, fastened in with net-work above, so that if a good guinea was above, those would not come out with it.) Then master desired I would take care of him. Master went with me to the Mansion-House. There were six or seven such gilt pieces taken out of each of the prisoner's shoes: there was also a powder-horn found upon him. He made interest to get bail, and neither he nor his bail appeared to have his trial, so they have forfeited their recognizance. I took him since at the end of Tooley-street, with two other people with him. He made the greatest resistance, and said I might take the bail, he would not go with me. I insisted on his coming with me, and brought him the second time to my master's house, where Sir Charles Asgill was. Sir Charles ordered him to be searched, and another such bag was found upon him.

 Cross-Examination.
 Q. How long have you lived in London?
 Bristow. I have lived in London about eight years.
 Q. Did you never hear of this sort of business before?
 Bristow. No, I never did.
 Q. Did the prisoner play with you at all?
 Bristow. No, he only desired me to play, and appeared to be of their party.
   Richard Wiltshire. I am a waiter at the King's Arms tavern in Cornhill. I have seen the prisoner several times.
