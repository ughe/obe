step in"—we went into the parlour, and when we entered Nathan bowed to the prisoner, and introduced me as Mr. Berlyn, and then he said to me, "This is Captain Cornish"—the prisoner asked us to sit down, and when we had sat a minute or two, the prisoner laid, "Well, sir, I understand you have a quantity of this linen?"—he asked me the price—I said I had 4,000 yards of the Hessian linen, at 4 d ., and 4,000, at 3 1/4 d .—he offered me 3 d . a yard, and to take the whole 8000 yards—I told him I could not make any abate ment from the price, and he said as that was the case he could only take 1000 yards at 4 d .—when that bargain was made he said, "Have you brought a pattern of that flock that I wanted, Mr. Nathan?"—Nathan said, "Yes, Mr. Berlyn has got it"—I produced it, and agreed to let the captain have it at 30 l .; a ton—I told him there was about a ton and a half—he said he wanted it for shipping—he did not mention what vessel he was captain of, but said he was going to set sail in a day or two—when I asked him the price for the flock he said I was much cheaper with the flock than I was with the linen, and he agreed to take that at once—he said I ought to make some little allowance, as all his dealings were for cash—I said I did business on no other terms—he then said, "I want these goods to-morrow; they must be here between twelve and one"—I said very well, he should have them, and they were to be paid for on delivery—that was the agreement between us at the time—I was then about to take my departure, and the captain said, "Won't you take a glass of wine before you go?"—I said I was not in the habit of drinking wine—he said I was not half a sailor, and would I have some grog—I at last had a drop of clear gin—I then went away, and Nathan with me—I laid to him, "Where is this flock?"—he told me to come with him, and took me to a man named Isaacs, of whom I bought the flock for 35 l .;—the next day I took the flock and linen in a van and a cart—I paid a sovereign for the use of them—I went, and Nathan with me, to the house in Baker-street—I knocked at the door, and the captain opened it himself—he said, "Good morning, Mr. Berlyn; you have brought these goods; you are very exact; if you will be kind enough to take them round to my warehouse, in Spring-street, my man will be there and receive them of you, and he will assist you in unloading; then come back, and I will pay you"—I was going to leave the house with the goods, and he said to me, "Have you got the invoice?"—I said, "Yes"—he said, "Let me peruse it, to see that it is all right"—I gave it him and went round to Spring-street, where I met his man, and he assisted me to unload the goods into a coach-house—I then gave the men a sovereign for the van, and 1 s . to get some bread and cheese—I went back to the house, and knocked—I saw a woman, but I could not find the captain," and could not get my money—the next day I went to the police-office, and, by the advice of the Magistrate, I was accompanied to the prisoner's house by the police-inspector—I went to the warehouse with a sledge-hammer, and gave two or three men 1 s . a-piece to assist me—I broke open the door—I found the flock, and got all that back again—I went across the yard, and got into the house—I saw a lad, who came with a crow-bar, and said, "What do you want here?" and made a blow at me—I did not see the prisoner there at first, but I saw him afterwards at the back parlour window—he said nothing—I said, "I want my property"—a woman who was there held up the invoice, in the prisoner's presence, and said, "What do you want? it is only a mere debt; you can do nothing but sue for it"—I had broken the window to get into the house, followed by two or three persons—I got severely handled by a number of persons in the house where the pri soner was—one struck me with a poker in the ribs, and I did not recover it for three weeks, and I got nearly blinded, as I had an umbrella shoved  into my eye—I found about half the linen in the house, and succeeded in gaining that—the persons who went with me were frightened, and decamped—the value of what I lost was about 58 l .;—I saw Nathan after that, and taxed him with being a party concerned—he said what a fool I must have been to be gulled  by him out of so much money, and he only wanted such fools as me to enable him to pay for his wife's confinement—he mentioned something about sharing some money, but I forget it almost; it was, that two or three of them had a share, or a swag , or something, out of it—I once saw the captain afterwards, but I could not secure him; I had broken both my legs and arms, and could not chase him—I found him here in Newgate, and recognized him at once.

WILLIAM PENNY. I am an inspector of the G division of police. Mr. Berlyn complained of having been swindled out of his property, and in conse quence of directions, I accompanied him to No. 15, Baker-street—I stood at the front door while he went round to the warehouse—I did not see any body—I did not go into the yard—I have known the prisoner many years, efer since I have been in the police—I cannot say that I knew him by the name of Cornish—that name was on the door of the house—I knew him by the name of Broad—he lived within a few yards of the station—I have also known him by the name of Brown.
