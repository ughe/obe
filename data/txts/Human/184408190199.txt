   MR. ROBINSON . Q.  Did you see the prisoner afterwards? A.  Yes—he went off for the doctor himself.
   COURT . Q.  Did you see any mark on the deceased's face? A.  I saw blood trickling on the left side of her neck as I went down on the landing—after she was dead I saw several marks on her forehead—Mrs. Bray came to the door and looked—Carroll opened it, and Mrs. Bray said, "Don't kill the woman"—Mr. Carroll said, "I have sent for a policeman, and the policeman won't come, what am I to do with her?"—this was after I had passed her on the landing. WILLIAM HOBBS. I am the husband of the deceased. On Saturday, the 2nd of March, I got up about half-past five o'clock, and went out with the deceased to go to work—we went into a public-house, and took half a quartern of rum—I drank the biggest part of it, and my wife the rest—she left me about five minutes before six—she was then in a very good state of health, and was sober—her name was Elizabeth Hobbs—I went to my work, and knew nothing more till I came home at one o'clock. SARAH SUMMERS. I am the wife of William Summers, and live in Bird cage-alley, the next house to the prisoner, in the first floor room. Between nine and ten o'clock in the morning of the 2nd of March I heard a terrible crying in the prisoner's house—I went down stairs, and then up stairs into the two-pair, and looked through a hole—I saw Mr. and Mrs. Carroll in the room, and Mrs. Hobbs—I heard her call him a transported (using a bad word)—directly she did so, Mrs. Carroll struck her with her hand—I could not say whether it was shut or open—she repeated the same word over again, and then Carroll struck her with his fist or hand, I cannot say which—he had nothing in his hand—I saw no more—I weir down stairs, and called Mrs. Bass. MARY BASS. I live in Birdcage-alley. In consequence of what Mrs. Summers told me I went to the house, and saw Mrs. Hobbs lying on the bed very much swollen—I saw nothing happen between her and the prisoner. MARY BURN. I was living in Birdcage-walk. On the 2nd of March, be tween nine and ten o'clock, I was at Carroll's house—I saw the deceased come up stairs—Mrs. Carroll was coming up, and got quarrelling with her—I heard nothing more till after Mr. and Mrs. Carroll went up stairs into her room—I heard a disturbance over head, but 1 was not up there at the time—it was like falling—I heard the deceased say if her husband had been at home she would not have been ill-used so—I went up stairs after that, and she was bleeding behind the ear—Carroll was in the room at the time—I saw no blow struck.
   Cross-exarnined. Q.  You were a friend of Mrs. Hobbs? A.  Yes—she and I had not been drinking that morning—I had not seen her that morning—I saw her the night before, and parted with her in the Borough, about one o'clock—I will swear it was not three—we had nothing to drink. WILLIAM RENDLE. I am parish surgeon of St. George's, and live in Blackman-street, Borough.
