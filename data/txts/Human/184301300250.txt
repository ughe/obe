   COURT  to MISS CONSTABLE . Q.  Take that paper into your hand, and carefully read the memorandum at the bottom, above the signature? A.  I read it before—the signature is decidedly not my handwriting—if any one has sworn that he saw me write it at Camberwell, he has sworn falsely—the words beginning" I have carefully compared," are not my handwriting, not any part of it—I never saw the paper before this evening.
   Q.  Look at the very beginning, and explain what those items mean? A. (Reading )—"Mount Pleasant House, Lewisharn.—Richard Constable, Dr. to Joseph Stanley. 1838, Feb. 1st, paid for carpets, as per annexed, 30 l ."—I suppose that item was obtained from Stanley's memory, or perhaps a bill delivered to him—I should think it was taken from an account that Stanley kept—he and I bought the carpets together—we went together to choose them, and he had 30 l . given to him to pay for them, by my father—there was a trifling balance, which he never accounted for—the next item is, "Paid acceptance, 25 l ., for furniture"—that was paid by a bill, and I have the acceptance, but not with me—it was to a man named Rushton, in the Kent-road, accepted by my father—"Mr. Buffin, 20 l ., stoves as per bill 30 l ."—they did not cost anything like 30 l .—he had the money to pay for them.
   Q.  Had he any funds of his own, by which he could have paid for these several things? A.  I have ascertained since, that he had not 1 s .—he had nothing—"Radburn, for painting house, 115 l ."—I paid that man myself in the house, and Stanley was not present—he brought the gentleman down, and gave him a promissory note for the amount, and I paid him in his presence—I do not know the gentleman's name—he lived somewhere at Rotherhithe, and this gentleman was his landlord—"Collet, 40 l ."—he was Mr. Abraham Constable's coachman, and received it from my father—"Hind's bill of costs, 22 l . 19 s ."—Mr. Hind was the coroner of Sitting bourne, and these were the costs accruing from Mr. C. Pratt signing the deed of reversion, in conjunction with the Thompsons—my father paid that—I was not present—he supplied Stanley with the money to go—he could not have gone without it—"Smith's bill, for bell-hanging, 44 l ."—that was paid by an acceptance of Mr. Davies, the wax-chandler, in St. Martin's-lane—"Paid an account of furniture bought at Shooter's-hill, 7 l . 12 s . 3 d ."—Mr. Langdon bought the furniture, I believe, with a larger sum than this—we bought a cow and calf, but the sum waa 9 l .—he has doubled it—" Ditto, dtto, 19 l ."—it was rather an aged cow, and we had it for 9 l .—'"A red cow, 13 l . 5 s ."—I think we gave about 10 l . or 11 l . for it, but Mr. Fluker, I be lieve, has the items—"By cash, 20 l ., by ditto, 30 l .," but then it was cash given to Mr. Stanley not as given my father—"Expenses to Wales, on ac count of Richard Constable's affairs, 0.5 l ."—I have the numbers of all the notes—he received a sum of 300 l ., and I gave it him myself—"Sept. 23, paid an acceptance, on account of Foakes, 31 l . 7 s . 6 d ."—that was an accept ance that I went and paid Foakes myself, at the Greyhound, at Croydon, on Saturday, the day he always calls to collect his rents—he is the land-steward of Captain Cater, of Tulse-hill—"Mr. Gibbons, carpenter, balance of ac count, 20 l ."—that I paid Gibbons, and I have, no doubt, his voucher for it—"To Mr. Gray, plumbing, 7 l . 16 s . 3 d ."—it was something more than 7 l ., but not "7 l . 16 s . 3 d ."—"For a sow and ten pigs, 8 l ."—I paid it myself—Stanley was not on the premises at the time, and the gilding was the gilding
