Mr. Attenborough contended that the Court had no power to make a discretionary
order as to the repayment of the pawobrokers where the pledges are orer £16.
The whole purview of the Pawnbrokers' Act was with reference to small loans
Pawnbrokers could charge what interest they liked over £10. It was not beres
question of imposing terms. There was no power to make an order for the simple
reason that his clients had a valid right of property in these goods. The general
proposition was that no Court had power to take away goods the property in which
was legally vested in one man and to bestow them upon another. It was only when
the Court was satisfied that a prosecutor was legal owner of the goods that it had
power to make an order under the Larceny Act or any other Act.
Judge Lumley Smith: Do you say that no order can ever be made on a paw
broker? Supposing a man steals my watch and pawns it?
Mr. Attenborough: If he steals your watch the property in the wateh still
remains rested in you and the Court would order the watch to be returned to you
on proof of ownership. This is a matter of the civil rights of the parties. If the
prosecutor here has any property in these articles, it is subjeet to the rights of
property which my clients have quite innocently acquired. That these goods
belonged to Mr. Royne at one time is not disputed. He delivered the goods to the
prisoner under certain contracts which are known as approbation contracts. We
do not have, fortunately, to depend upon Mr. Royne's verbal statements, but we
have the transaction reduced into writing. When one looks at these approbation
notos, and at the letters which Mr. Tremayne writes, it is perfectly clear that
Tremayne had these goods, not as agent, but as a buyer, to deal with them as he
hked, and to sell them for any sums he liked, and to make any profit he liked.
Counsel referred to the sale of Goods Act (Sec. 18) dealing with the various orents
upon which property passed upon contract of sale, and cited Kirkham . Gill
(1897, 1, Q.B., 201), where it was held that the original vendor could not recover
goods from the person with whom they had been pledged, though he had been the
victim of dishonesty. Vendors might protect themselves by adopting particular
form of approbation noto. As to the suggestion that conviction revested the
property in the prosecutor, that was no doubt so before the sale of Goods Act of
1893, but section 24 of that Act drew a clear distinction between a prosecution for
larceny and a prosecution for false pretences, and since the passing of that Act it
had never been the practice in these courts to make orders of restitution where
goods have been obtained by false pretences and had got into the hands of innocent
persons for value
