ELIZABETH HEDGES. I am the wife of Williams Hedges—the prisoner and his wife lodged with us for about seven months—they lived on very bad terms together—I have heard the prisoner make use of very bad expressions to her—he often said, "You d—little b—, madam, I'll do for you, I cannot do as I like here, because I am in Mrs. Hedge's house"—he said, "D—you, I will do for you, before long: as I am in Mrs. Hedges's house I cannot do as I like:" and then he said again, "Madam, d—you, I will do for you"—on Saturday, the 16th of April, Mrs. Hag gerstone went out at a quarter past one o'clock in the day—I was in the White Hart, with my husband, about eleven o'clock at night, and she came and sent in for me, and she then came in with me—I did not see her go out-we staid at the bar till about half-past eleven o'clock—I did not see Claridge go out—I saw him there—my husband and I left the White Hart at half-past eleven o'clock—Mrs. Haggerstone and Claridge were gone when we went away—I was sober—my husband was not—I called Mrs. Haggerstone for some time, when I came out of the house, but got no answer—I afterwards saw her at a little gate, about twenty yards from the White-Hart—nobody was with her—it was between the White Jart and Saunders's beer-shop—she went with me down Merton-rise—my husband and I did not go into Saunders's beer-shop together—Mrs. Haggerstone was with me, but she would not come into the beer-shop—I asked her several times, and left her at the second little post, close to Wise's—that was on her road home—there is a turning to the left, just before you get to Saunders's beer-shop, which would take her home—it does not pass by the well—when we went into Saunders's it struck twelve o'clock, and when we came out it was half-past twelve o'clock—we went direct home—I found the prisoner at home—he was not undressed, but lying on the bed—I said to him, "Davy, is your wife at home?"—he said, "No, Mrs. Hedges, is not she along with you?"—when I went in again, I said, "Davy, my lad, do get up, it is a very dark night, I would get up and go across the fields and look for her"—he said he would see her d—d first: he supposed she was at some of her wh—tricks"—I said to him. "Stop Davy, don't judge her, for it is a dark night"—he said he would see her d—d before he would go outside the doors—I then said to him, "Oh, Davy, don't say so; come my lad, come get up and go and look for her"—he said, "No, I won't; and God send I may hear of her corpse before long"—and then, between sleeping and waking, I did not go to bed for half an hour after—I did not go to sleep when I went to bed—he got up after a bit, and I thought he was going after his wife—I spoke to him, hoping he was going after her—he said, "No; I will be d—d if I do," and said he was going to undress himself to go into bed to his children—he said, "She neither shall by aside of me, nor yet of my children no more"—I said to him, " Davy , come my lad, come"—he said, "No: I would see her d—d before I go out to her, nor shall she enter my house any more"—this was while I was up—I went to bed after that, but could not sleep—I was aroused some times after that, and heard persons come to the side of the bedge—I rose up momestarily and heard what they said—I hallooed to the prisoner two or three times while the persons were outside, "Davy," but got no answer—I said to his eldest daughter.
