   Q.  Could you tell what would be the difference between a person dying from apoplexy and from concussion of the brain? A.  I think, if I attended from an early period, I could, no doubt, form an opinion—the appearances of the deceased's head did not indicate sufficient symptoms of apoplexy—the great mischief here was in the spine, and my previous knowledge of the case made me suppose so the more—I saw nothing in the head to produce apoplexy—when I observed the wound on the spine I did not attribute it to the effect of disease—not at any time. ELIZABETH LEE. I am a nurse—I attended Mrs. Brenton from the 19th of Aug. up to the time of her death—I remember the night of the 3rd of Oct.—I left Mrs. Brenton at eleven o'clock that night—I left Mr. Brenton in the bedroom with her—I went into the front parlour—they were in the back parlour—I slept in the front parlour that night—after I had been there ten minutes I heard Mrs. Brenton say, "You may pinch me, or take pieces out of me, but I will die before I will give in to you"—I heard a scuffling first of all, and that expression directly, at the tame time—the scuffling continued about an hour, from eleven till twelve—I did not hear Mr. Brenton say anything—I heard no other remark from Mrs. Brenton in that hour—they were quiet for a little while, and then I heard them begin again—that was about a quarter after twelve—I heard her call him, "You wretch," and "a beast"—she said, if she had had Cobley, he would not hare used her in that way—I heard nothing else—that was the last I heard—I heard no sounds or any groaning, that I swear—I have never stated so—the baby, six weeks old, was in the room—it was crying in the course of the night, two or three times, and I heard Mr. Brenton speak to it, and hushing it—he was crying "Hush"—about half-past six or seven in the morning I knocked at their door—Mr. Brenton told me to come in—I leant over and took the baby out of the bed—Mr. Brenton was in bed at the time—I saw Mrs. Brenton—I asked her what was the matter, but received no answer—she was in an insensible state, but I had seen her so several times—after this I left the room, and went into the front room with the, child—I prepared the breakfast at eight o'clock, and took it in—Mrs. Brenton did not get up to eat any—she was not well enough to take any—Mr. Brenton got up about a quarter to eight, and went into the front parlour, and took breakfast at the same table with me—I did not put any question to him about Mrs. Brenton—I did not say a word to him about what I had heard—I am married—I attended Mrs. Brenton all this time—she was in a very weak, debilitated state, and had been so during the eighteen months I had at tended her—Mr. Brenton went out about nine that morning—I told Mrs. Baker, the landlady, what had taken place, as soon as he had gone out—it might be twenty minutes after he was gone, I cannot say exactly—Mrs. Baker did not send for a medical man on seeing her—she did not do any thing—we had a medical man, who always came, every day, about twelve o'clock.
