   MR. METCALFE . Q.  Were there two passenger trains which passed just at that time? A.  Yes; they were signaled in the ordinary way—I have not got the prisoner's book—the law department have had the charge of it since the accident—I went to the prisoner's box about the time of the col lision, as I understood he wanted to speak to me—he said, "I understand the ballast train has divided in the tunnel"—I saw he was agitated, and he said, "I am afraid I have given the clear signal"—I immediately telegraphed to Charlton station, but at that time the collision had taken place.
   Cross-examined. Q.  How old is the prisoner? A.  Twenty-four—I have given him a very good character, and I believe him to be anxiously careful to do his duty—he had not received signal-man's wages, as his first week had not elapsed—he would be entitled to them—his wages as a porter were 16 s . a week—he has married since he has been at Blackheath-station—his wages as a signal-man would be one guinea—I treat this entirely as a mistake; I cannot account for it—the signal-man in charge has four columns to make entries in for each train between its being received and having passed the station—I do not know whether he would have to make 150 entries in three quarters of an hour—in addition to the four entries to each signal, he has to hold out a flag for each train that passes, and to put up the semaphore arms—there were trains at 3.30 and 3.50 from Charing-cross; 4.5 and 4.20—the 4.30 would not arrive till after the collision—only one empty train went previous to the coal train. EDWARD WINGFIELD. I am a time-keeper in the service of the South eastern Company—I knew John Cumming, who was injured in the accident—he died in the Royal Marine hospital at Woolwich from the injuries he received.
   The prisoners statement before the Magistrate:  —"I entered the company's service two years last August as porter, which situation I held up to a week previous to the accident, when I was placed in the signal-box to do duty for the signal-man who was ill. I don't believe I had done more than twenty duties in the signal-box, that is to say, when there was any signalling to do, before I took charge of it, so that my previous experience was but little. However, when I took the duty I hoped I should have been able to have performed it satisfactorily. My duties were to attend in the signal-box from 7 in the morning until 6 in the evening, with the exception of the hour allowed for dinner. After coming out of the box at 6, I had to wait the arrival and to attend the 6.17 down train. Whilst in the box I had to receive and give telegraphic signals to and from Lewisham-station, and to and from Angerstein-junction. I had beside to enter signals in five different places for each train in the signal-book in addition. I had for each train or engine to attend to the semaphore and distinct signal, and upon each train passing to show my flag or hand-signal that all was right. I will now state briefly the circumstances connected with the lamentable acci dent so far as I am able, and the traffic that passed through the station within three-quarters of an hour of it. The following trains passed through the Blackheath-station:—Down from London 3.30, 3.50 (expressed), 4.5, 420 (expressed). Due at Blackheath, 3 51, 4.7, 4.25, 431. The next down train, the 4.30, due at 4.47, was fortunately stopped at Blackheath. Up from Woolwich, 3 30. Due at Blackheath 3.44. Angerstein coal-train, 4.5. Blackheath empties'-train, 4.10.
