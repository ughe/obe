CHARLOTTE STANLEY. I am the wife of John Stanley, a surgeon, and live in Commercial-road, Lambeth—he is the son of the late Joseph Stanley—I was acquainted with Joseph Stanley—he was the prisoner's uncle—I knew him in the years 1836, 1837, and 1838—he was very badly off at that time—I frequently lent him money—he was in a state of poverty during the years 1835, 1836, 1837, and 1838—I know that by his coming to me, and borrowing 1 s . and 6 d .—he wan in the habit of doing to—I know the pri soner—I never saw him and Stanley together in the Fleet—I have been frequently in the Fleet, but I never saw Stanley there—he went to the coffee-room—I never saw him and the prisoner together—I do not know whether the prisoner was in the habit of associating with Stanley—I saw the prisoner's sister, Phœbe, at the time her father lay dead—I am not aware that the prisoner was in the habit of associating with Phœbe.
   DR. LEVER re-examined.  I did not see Stanley at any time on the 31st of Dec., until he called at my house—I did not see him at Mr. Con stable's house on the 30th—I never saw the prisoner at Mr. Constable's.
   MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  About how long altogether, putting both your visits together, do you think you were in the house that day? A.  Half or three quarters of an hour, including both visits—his daughter nursed him. CHARLOTTE STANLEY re-examined.  I belive the prisoner was aware that Joseph Stanley was in great poverty, from different conversations that I have heard when I have been with some part of the family—I never learnt it from the prisoner himself—I was very little with him—I have seen very little of him—I have heard that Stanley was in the habit of going to the Fleet Prison, but I did not see him—William Blackwell, the prisoner's brother, was in the Fleet—I do not know from the prisoner, or from anybody in his presence, whether he knew of Stanley's going there—I have heard him speak ill of Stanley. MARY WAGNOR. I did live at No. 19, Liverpool-street, Camden-town, but now live at No. 16, Stanmore-street, St. Paneras-road. I knew Joseph Stanley—he was in poverty—he came to me the year the Queen was crowned, in the summer time, and was there about seven or eight months—I cannot say exactly to a month, but he left me about Easter, and I was very glad to get rid of him—I never saw the prisoner. ELIZABETH JENKINSON. I live at No. 15, Swinton-street, Gray's-inn-road. The late Joseph Stanley lodged at my father's house for about ten weeks, in the year 1838—during that time his circumstances were very poor indeed—his wife and three children lived with him—they appeared very pool in their circumstances, and their clothing was poor—the children were con stantly coming into our kitchen complaining of hunger, and their clothing exhibited poverty in the extreme—this was about the middle of April, 1838—he was to pay 12 s . a week for his lodging, which he did for the first three weeks, and afterwards we had it by instalments—I know nothing of the pri soner—I never saw him before—I remember Stanley's wife being confined—she had no doctor until afterwards, and no nurse was spoken to—she had her sister to nurse her afterwards—there were very few clothes for the child.
