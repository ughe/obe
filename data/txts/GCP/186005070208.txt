MICHAEL LEWIS. I am an inmate and wardsman of Lambeth Poorhouse
-on Monday, the 19th, about 5 o'clock, the deceased was brought there-
he appeared rather lightheaded, and complained of his head-he was left
in my charge - he could walk and stand, but he went to bed half an
hour after he came, and complained of a violent pain in his head-I looked
at the back of his head; there was a cut or wound about an inch long, with
a piece of sticking plaister on it-I took the old plaister off and put new on
about an hour after he was brought in the medical officer was sent for, who
ordered him to go to bed and have all nourishment, wine, bread and
butter and tea, and whatever he could take, which I gave him ; but he
could not take much-he was put into a bath and cleaned, he came to me
-he complained next morning of a violent pain across his head, in front,
and seemed much worse-he had taken the medicine that I gave him-he
bad constant fits till Saturday the 4th, when he died about a quarter past 4
in the afternoon-the first fit he had was on Wednesday night-he was a
little sensible when he came out of the fits, but he had no time before the
next fit took him.
HENRY ST. JOHN BUTLER. I am house surgeon and medical officer of
Lambeth Workhouse on Monday, 19th March, I was sent for to attend
upon Thomas Faye, about 5 o'clock in the afternoon-I found him in the
bath room washing himself-I accosted him-his replies were confused and
incoherent-I asked him what was the matter with him; he said, “Nothing"
-my attention was directed by the bath man to his head-I examined it,
and found a slight wound about three-quarters of an inch long; it appeared
to be incised, and such as might have been inflicted with a flint or a sharp
stone, or any sharp instrument-I removed the plaister, and found it was in
process of healing-in my opinion a violent fall on the back of the head on
a stone would probably produce it.
Court. l. Could you tell whether the wound had bled much ? A. I
should presume not; there was a little clotted blood on the hair—it was &
sort of wound more likely to be caused by a fall than a blow; the small
quantity of blood would lead me to that conclusion.
MR. SHARPE. Q. Was he a heavy person A. A remarkably heavy
man, a very fine man-his being heavy would be more likely to cause a
wound of this kind-after I had examined him in the bath room he was
received into a ward by my orders- I saw him again in about an hour; he
was then leaning his head in his hand-I spoke to him, and he complained
of his head-I ordered his head to be shaved in order that I might make a
more complete examination—that was done-I ordered him a mustard
plaister-I did not at that time detect any injury to the brain, though I sus-
pected it—the wound had only penetrated the scalp—the pupils of his eyes
were somewhat dilated; that, and other symptoms, led me to the conclusion
that he was suffering from concussion of the brain-he said he had lost his
appetite, and complained of great oppression in breathing--I had him put to
bed, and saw him twice next day-I had received no information as to the
particulars of this fight, but from the information I received, and from the
symptoms, I judged he was suffering from compression of the brain, and
treated him for that
