THOMAS WATKINS( police-constable M  174.) About eleven o'clock at night, on the 9th of October, Coker came and gave me information—I saw the prisoners come up from Sweeps-alley about two minutes before, and there found the prosecutor drunk—Coker gave me two pieces of a torn bag—I took the prosecutor to the station-house, and apprehended the prisoners about three quarters of an hour afterwards—Istead resisted being searched—I said, "Well, give me the money out of your mouth," and she pulled out what she said was 5 s . 6 d ., but I found it was 6 s . 6 d .—I found 2 s . 11 1/2 d . on Garman, in copper.
   Garman.  The money found on me was my own. GEORGE CURTIS I was very drunk that night—I found myself lying down somewhere—I knew Garman well, and thought I could trust my life with him—we had been fellow-servants—I recollect seeing the woman that night, also, at the Crown, but after I came out of there, I know nothing—Coker came to me and asked if I had lost any thing—I said I had lost my bag, but did not know how much money there was—there was six or seven shillings in it—I could swear to the bag as soon as I saw it, as had sewn it myself.
   Istead. Q.  Were you in another public house besides the Crown? A.  I cannot say—I was not with another woman to my knowledge—I cannot say whether I was at Mrs. Burridge's.
   Istead.  You were with another female, and she went to see you home, and you went up into a concert-room. Witness.  I saw the female prisoner at the concert-room—both the prisoners were together—whether it was the Crown, or where, I cannot recollect—I recollect Garman telling me be would see me safe home, as I told him I was quite forward in liquor.
   Garman.  I found him in there drinking, and said, "What cheer, Curtis?"—he said, "What cheer, Sergeant?
