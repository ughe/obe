SARAH CLARK. I am the wife of Henry Clark, a chair-maker, of No.
48. Belvidere-place, Borough-road. I knew the deceased on the Monday
after the Saturday on which he died, I saw Mrs. Bowler, when I came back
from the inquest-room, after the inquest had been adjourned-I think it
was after the third meeting there were three Mondays and one Fri-
day-I attended at three meetings I did not go on the first Mon-
day-I was at the house all the week after he died the first time I
went there was on the Monday night I had no conversation with
her then-on the next Monday I had no conversation with her I had
only one conversation with her that was on the Monday before the
Friday on which the inquest closed-I do not know the day of the
month-as I returned from the Jury-room I called at Mrs. Bowler's
Mrs. Parfitt was there, and she said in the prisoner's presence,"Well.
Mrs. Clark, how do you get on to night?"--I said it was postponed till
another night-Mrs. Bowler burst out laughing--I said, "Do not laugh,
Jane? it is no laughing matter"-she laughed, and said, “Well, if I laugh
it is not right, and if I cry it is not right, and what am I to do?"-Mr.
Dunster was present at the time--be turned round, and said, "Well, Jane,
you ought to know better"-she immediately turned round, and said,
“Now, Dunster, you had better hold your tongue, or by G- I will open,
and say something that you will not like "-he said, “Well, Jane, I stand
manfully before you, and if you have anything to say openly, say it; I
will most happily hear you, with the greatest of pleasure-she said, "I
bave trusted to one, and no doubt I shall get over it; I am in it, and
must get out of it in the best manner I can; there is one thing, they can-
not hang me, on account of my children"-she said that partly in a serious
and partly in a jocular way-nothing more particular passed that night-
we had supper, and went to bed-I remained there all night-next morn-
ing we were at breakfast, and the prisoner said, “Well, if he has been
poisoned, as it seems he has, he must have done it himself”-she laughed
about it--she was partly jocular, and partly serious, middling.
Cross-examined. Q. Did she not at this time say, "I am quite innocent,
but they are all against me, and wicked enough to hang me if they can?"
A. I never heard anything of the sort-I can repeat what passed from
beginning to end, from the time I was there first-I was asked from the
Monday at the inquest-I have not been asked as to the evidence I gave
first.
MR. PAYNE. Q. I asked you what passed ? A. You asked me the
last part instead of the first-I heard Mr. Bowler was bad on Saturday
night-I did not go till the first Monday after his death, when I came
from my work--there was not much conversation then-I remained there
till the doctor came- I was at the opening of the body I heard that he
died by poison-I remained there all night, during the week, and I think
on the Wednesday, Mrs. Bowler said it was a shocking thing, but thank
God she had not done it, that nothing had passed through his body except
what she had given him, but poison she had not-I remained there till
the Saturday, and she still said that nothing had passed through his body
except what she had given him herself.

body had been opened? A. Not to me--she said it in my hearing-1
