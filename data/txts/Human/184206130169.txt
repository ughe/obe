I sorted the whole of the books, which were brought by Gorman—there were a great many valuable works—after that was done, I prepared a catalogue, and sent it to the prosecutor—I know Ritson's Scotch Poems very well—the two volumes would produce 14  s . or 15 s . in a sale—the Byron, in the condition this is, would be worth about 30 s .—it is six volumes, bound in green morocco; and Butler would be worth about 5 s .
   Cross-examined. Q  I suppose the value of this early edition of Byron is the binding? A.  Yes—because it has been superseded by a late edi tion.
   COURT . Q.  Occasionally a book of value is to be picked up at a stall, at a small price? A.  Yes—a stall keeper would be more likely to judge from the binding, than the value of the book. RICHARD LEWIS. I am porter to Mr. Golden, in White chapel-road. I know the prisoner as keeping a stall there—on Saturday the 14th of May, I saw all these books except the Byron, at the prisoner's stall—I purchased the Ritson in two volumes, and Butler in two volumes of him for 5 s .—I afterwards saw a volume of Campbell's Pleasures of Hope, there, with the prosecutor's name in it—I held it up to the prisoner, and remarked that the Rev. Mr. Valentine's name was in it—I knew Mr. Valentine—he merely nodded—seeing the prosecutor's name in it, I went and mentioned it to my master—I went back to the stall in about ten minutes, and asked the prisoner for Campbell's Pleasures of Hope—be said it was sold—it was a 12mo. book, in good condition—I went back to Mr. Gladdin, and in consequence of what he said I went and told Mr. Valentine—when I came back, I told the prisoner he was not to sell any more books till Mr. Valentine came down.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did not he say, "Very wail?" A.  Yes, and then I left him—I have bought books of him before—I attend sales, and am por ter to Mr. Gladdin who lives about a minute's walk from the prisoner's house—Mr. Gladdin's is a shop—he sells all kinds of books. REV. WILLIAM VALENTINE re-examined.  Some of these books bear my name—the autograph of Lord Byron was in this volume, where this bit of wafer is—I received information of this about eleven o'clock in the morn ing—I was exceedingly unwell, and did not go about till about half-past five. HENRY TONGMAN. I bought five volumes of Byron's Works of the prisoner, about the middle of April—I gave him 6 s . for them.
   Cross-examined. Q.  I believe he first asked you 9 s . for them? A.  Yes, which I refused—I went three days afterwards, and got them for 6 s .—I found them openly lying on the stall.
   COURT . Q.  Is it common for persons in the book trade to sell books for less than they give for them? A.  They frequently sell very cheap—the marking of books varies. THOMAS CHEVELEY. I live in Jubilee-place, Stepney. On the 14th of May, I saw a little volume of Hudibras on the prisoner's stall—I bought that, and another book of poems of small value for two shillings—I saw Littleton's Works there—I had frequently seen the prisoner's stall before, but I had not noticed books of the value of these there—he had generally books of an inferior description—I asked if he had any other books of a similar description—he said he thought he had, and looked about—I said I was in the habit of passing, and would look in again.
