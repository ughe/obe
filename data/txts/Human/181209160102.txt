The credit side, goods sold to a person of the name of Moranda, was made up; about the time they were made the greater part were sold by my brother, James, to Signora Moranda. She is an Opera lady, and lives in the Haymarket. I believe her to be thirty years of age and upwards. The last articles were sold by myself. The entry, Edward James, was entered as a real transactions; and much about the same time they bear date, Edward James. I do not think there are any regular entries made. He is about thirty years of age. The articles, seventy-three pounds, were taken away in an hackney coach, in day time. My brother, James, assisted in packing them up. They consisted of various articles of jewellery. Baroness Menkwitz lives in Downing-street. She actually owes me what appears in the books. When did you see the Baroness. In the early part of February I saw her at the West end of the town. She took the suit of pearls herself. All the goods in the said account were delivered to her in my shop. John Shehey was a wine and brandy merchant in Smithfield. He came to my shop for the goods. He is now in the Fleet. The account of Count Holmar is a true and correct account. The goods sold in November last. I delivered them to him myself. The bullion consisted of doubloons, some India pieces, and some coin. The bullion was sold at different times. Halliday became a bankrupt in the Fleet. The goods were sold to Halliday when he lived in Bell Savage-yard, in the city. The eleventh leaf is written by the boy. The baroness is written by my brother, James.
 Q. to Mr. Powis. You have examined the books carefully - A. I have.
 Q. Did you ask him any questions about certain goods that he had charged, which you thought he had not rightly charged - A. I have asked him questions upon all these accompts which I suspected. I pointed out two accounts to him, and particular; Halliday was one, the other Shehey. I pointed out the absurdity, to shew him, that he could have credited such a man as Halliday; he must know his real situation in life. I remonstrated with him how he could have trusted such a man as that. At that time he trusted him he had been recently liberated from the Rules of the Fleet. At the time the bankrupt credited him, Halliday had no means of living. Speaking to him one time upon that, he said, you are the only man who knows any thing respecting Halliday or Shehey; if you remain quiet you shall have nothing to complain of. I told him, it would be better for him to tell the truth. After that he said, he believed the account of Halliday and Shehey to be fabricated, but said he, it was all his, William and Nugent's doing: he was ill at the time. He then told me, if I would remain his friend, and would not oppose his certificate, that he should have at command, in a short time, five or six thousand pounds. I said, why, you have not done the trick to deep as that, and asked him to explain himself. He said, he had it in his power to marry a woman of that property.
 Q. With respect to the enquiry of Bond; you have examined the books - A. I have.
 Q. Did you find any articles charged to these persons, which by these books you find to have come in afterwards - A. I do.
 Q. State the articles - A. It is in the account of James. The articles are two trunks booked to him in November last charged three pounds eight shillings. He said, he had them of Mr. Briant, Ludgate-hill. I pointed out to him the absurdity that he had not them in his possession until the 26th of that month. He said, it might be a mistake in the date. He was conscious the account of James was correct, although it was not his own making. He was ill a bed at the time.
 Q. Now, I ask you, sir, what is the deficiency upon the estate - A. The deficiency, admitting these debts to be just, amounts to between two and three thousand pounds, and if they are not just they amount to five.
 Mr. Andrews. What was the amount of your debt against  John Folkard- A. Ninety-nine pounds thirteen shillings.
 Q. Do you know what business James Folkard was - A. He was shopman to his brother,  John Folkard.
 Q. Do you know whether James was present when Bond proved his debt - A. No, I am sure he was not.  William Folkard was there, but not John.
 Q. On the 25th of November here is an entry in the book of James. Do you know the hand-writing of  James Folkard- A. I believe it to be his. I have seen him write. In addition, he admitted it to be his. I asked James Folkard, whose hand-writing was this. He said, his; and that the Baroness Minkwitz had called in November 1811.
   JOHN ROBINSON. Q. At the time of this commission you were a creditor - A. Yes, to John Folkard to about one hundred and thirty pounds, me and my partner.
 BARONESS MINKWITZ. Q. Where did you reside in the month of February last - A.In Fleet-lane, at Mr. Bond's house. I had lodged there between four and five months. I came there in October last.
 Q. Were you acquainted at that time with the prisoner, Nugent - A. Yes. I have known him many years.
 Q. Do you know Jacob Grick - A. Yes.
 Q. On the 13th of February do you recollect any of the five persons being at your lodgings, either Nugent or the Folkards - A. I cannot be sure to the day. On the Thursday, Nugent, and the other gentleman. He said, his name was  John Folkard. When they came I was below stairs.
 Q. Was you landlord at home at that time - A. I cannot be sure that Mr. Bond was at home.
 Q. Did you afterwards go up stairs - A. Yes, the parlour and street door was open. I saw them come in. They said, how do you do, Baroness. I followed them up stairs.  John Folkard was a stranger to me. Nugent first spoke to me. He set himself down. He said, how do you do: he had introduced a friend of his. He told me his name.
