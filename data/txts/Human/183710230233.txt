   Redshaw's Defence.  The first time of my meeting the prosecutor was on Thursday evening, about half-past nine o'clock, in Blackfriars-road, he and Denton were together—Denton said, "How do you do; will you turn back, and walk with us?—I walked as far as the Elephant and Castle—the prosecutor and Denton were talking about the 3 l . 10 s . which he owed to Denton, and when we got to the Elephant and Castle, the prosecutor said, "Will you go in and take any thing to drink?"—I said, "No"—Denton said, "I want the money you owe me, for I am going out of town on Saturday—and he said to me, "I dare say you want your money that I owe you"—he owed me 2 l  —I had seen him the day before about the money—he said to the prosecutor, "Would you pay him; it would do as well as paying me?"—he said, "I will ask mistress for the money"—I left them, and told him before I left that I would come down on Saturday afternoon, and asked him what time—he said, "Half-past three o'clock in the afternoon; and ring the bell at the gate"—I went ac cording to my appointment, and left the prosecutor and Denton together—I went about half-past three o'clock—the prosecutor answered the bell, and said, "How do you do? you have come for the money, I have not got it; mistress is not within"—I said, "You told me and Denton you would let us have it, or make away with your watch"—he said, "I have not got it, mistress will be in; you had better call in the evening"—accordingly, I called about half-past six o'clock, and that was the second time I ever saw him in my life—I went to Denton, and he was not at home—Page was there—I was going to be married in a short time to Hayes—I went down to tell Denton, and while we sat at tea, he said, I will make you a watch, John has not done yours, which he had to repair"—he said, "I will soon make you a watch"—we both stopped there to tea—it quite slipped my memory—I went out with the watch, as it was still in my pocket—I asked Page to go with me—I was going to receive the money—I saw the servant, and said, "Is the footman within?"—she said not—I said, "What time?"—she said, "I don't know exactly, but I think he will be in in a minute"—on turning from the gate, I saw the prosecutor—he said, "Here you are—I said, "Yes, am I in time?"—she said, "Yes, you are in time, I have not got the money; I can let you have part of it"—I said, "Part of it will be of no use; you had better wait till Denton comes from the coun try"—he said, "You had better take it"—Page came and said, "William, you had better come on; what are you waiting for?"—and the policeman came and took us—there was nobody with us at all—Page went with me from Mrs. Denton's, as I asked him—the prosecutor had something in his hand in the square, and said, "You had better take it"—the policeman, when he came up, said, "Have they taken it?—he said, "No, but I will give them in charge"—I said, "It is not me, it is the other man you want," meaning Denton, as I had not been with the prosecutor before.
   REDSHAW  — GUILTY.
   PAGE  — NOT GUILTY.
