moored by stakes at each end across the punt—we were sitting on chairs—there were two other gentlemen, friends, in a skiff close by—the skiff was on the Surrey side of the punt—while we were there in the morning I remember two steamers passing up towards Richmond, on the Surrey side of the punt—that is the side on which the current is—where we were is comparatively shallow—when the steamers passed up to Twickenham in the morning there was a little swell—it did not interrupt our fishing—about half-past five o'clock I was sitting with my face towards Richmond-bridge—we were all in that position, and fishing in the same place—I looked round, and saw a steamboat coming from Twickenham—it was not more than thirty yards from the punt when I first saw it—I cannot say at what rate it was coming, but it appeared to be coming rapidly—the tide had turned about an hour, and was running down—I am unable to say if it was a strong tide—I observed somebody in some part of the boat, but I cannot say where, waving his hand as if to the man at the wheel—he was in an ele  vated position, or I should not have seen him—I have seen steamboats navigating, and a person on the paddle-box making a motion—it appeared to be done in that way—I was standing up—I could not see the person at the helm—the steamer continued to approach towards the Surrey side of the river—I cannot say whether it altered its course after the waving with the hand, for I sat down, thinking I should be thrown down, as I thought it would either strike the punt, or we should be upset by the swell—I had scarcely sat down a moment when we were struck by the steamboat—I had not even time to call out—I was about the middle of the punt—I know I was not on the Surrey side of it—the deceased was near the extreme end, on the Surrey side—there was a little boy sitting beyond, or nearest the Surrey side—I did not observe whether Mr. Lewis was standing or sitting at that moment—the end of the punt towards the Surrey side was struck by the steamboat—I cannot say what part of the steamer struck us—we were all knocked down into the punt, and the punt, particularly the end which was struck, went under water—that caused the punt to ship a deal of water—it did not right itself—that end remained slightly under water—directly I got up I looked round, and saw Mr. Lewis struggling in the water—the punt was driven from both its moorings, and carried down with the tide—I threw out two chairs to Mr. Lewis, and other efforts were made to save him but he sunk—I did not then observe the steamer—I afterwards saw her below the bridge—I think I saw her about ten minutes or a quarter of an hour after then, between us and the Surrey shore—I afterwards saw her below bridge, stopping—I rowed about for some time to endeavour to find the deceased, but did not succeed—I afterwards went with a policeman on board the steamboat—it is called the Richmond  —the prisoner Undey was pointed out as the captain—I cannot say whether he was the person I had seen in an elevated position making signals—the policeman who was with me told him he was come to take him to the station, or something to that effect, I did not hear
