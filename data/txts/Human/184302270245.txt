 1094. ELIZABETH BROWN and ANN CAMPLIN were indicted for stealing, on the   11th of February , 24lbs. weight of bread, value 3 s  . 9 d  ., the goods of Edward Thies .
   MR. BALLANTINE conducted the Prosecution. EDWARD THIES. I am a baker, and live in Camden-street, Newington. On the morning of the 11th of Feb., about seven o'clock, my journeyman left me—he had given me no notice—I went with the officer, on the 11th of Feb., to Camplin's house, which is next to mine—the officer knocked at the door—Camplin refused to let him in—he said, if she did not open it, he would break it open—she then opened the door, and said, "O my God, what shall I do?" and she offered me a sovereign to say nothing further about it—I found there twelve 2lb. loaves on a bed, partly covered over—I have every reason to believe they were mine—I had directed some one to watch my premises.
   Cross-examined by MR. PAYNE . Q.  What do you mean by partly co vered? A.  They were partly covered over on the bed, in the room on the ground floor—she seemed very much frightened.
   Cross-examined by MR. HORRY . Q.  What is Camplin? A.  A shoemaker—my journeyman's name is John Radford—Brown had been in my service occasionally, and used to come for the waste for the pigs, and generally at the time the bread was hot in the morning—it is the custom to allow journeymen bread. DONALD FRAZER( police-constable C  181.) On the morning of the 11th of Feb., between six and seven o'clock, I was watching the prosecutor's pre mises—about seven I was at the back of the premises, and I saw Brown opening the bakehouse door—she had a pail in her band, which she placed outside the door—she then stood in the doorway—I saw Radford bring a quantity of bread, and give it to Brown—the yards of the prosecutor's house and Camplin's join each other—I did not see any thing of Complin at the time—as soon as Brown received the loaves from Radford she ran into Camplin's back-door immediately—I went to the front directly—I there saw Camplin standing in front, talking to a servant girl, who was taking down the shutters—Camplin then went in—I stopped a few minutes, and saw Camplin come out the prosecutor's shop-door in front—(I know the premises, she could get from the bakehouse out that way)—she then began to take down some of the pro secutor's shutters—I then called the prosecutor, and went with him to Camp lin's house—I knocked several times—Camplin would not let me in—she said at first there was a person in the house who was undressed—I knocked again, and she said she would not let me in—I began forcing the door, and then she opened it—I found twelve loaves on the bed, covered over, with the exception of three or four—they were quite hot, and some of them partly destroyed—I did not find any person undressed, but there was a girl there—I asked her whose bread that was—she said it was hers—I called in the prosecutor—Camplin was then greatly confused, and said, "O God, what shall I do?"
