I have known the prisoner eighteen or twenty years—we were in the habit of visiting each other—her husband holds a respectable situation in Doctor's-commons—on Monday, the 14th of January, I changed a cheque at Barclay's bank—my landlady was with me, and she took the money, all but 5  s ., which she gave me—the money was a £10 note, three £5 notes, and a sovereign—after I got home, I paid my landlady the £10 note, and two of the £5 notes—I put the sovereign into the other £5 note, and put them together into my purse, which I put into my pocket—I afterwards put it into my table-drawer in my front room—the drawer was not locked—that was between four and five o'clock in the afternoon—the prisoner was in my room at the time—she came about four o'clock, and drank tea with me—I gave her 2 s . To fetch a loaf from below, and we had a little gin and water together about seven o'clock I said to her, "I am very tired, and if you will excuse me, I will go to bed"—I was fatigued with the walk in the morning—she said, "I will go up stairs with you"—she did so, and assisted me to bed she wished me good night, stooped over the bed and kissed me, and went away—I heard the private door slam—I did not go to sleep, but about ten minutes after, I got out of bed, put on my shoes and cloak, and went down stairs to get my purse out of the table-drawer, but it was gone—I am quite sure I had put it into the drawer.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLABKSON . Q.  Had you and the prisoner met together a good deal lately? A.  No; she came whenever she found it con venient—we were not in the habit of writing to each other—I never intrusted her with my money to take care of—we were intimate—I lodged with her mother three years—I took nothing after tea on this night—we had sixpenny-worth of gin and water between us before tea, just after the prisoner came, which was four o'clock—it was two glasses, I believe, it was not nearly half a pint—I frequently have a little gin and water after dinner, not always—I had taken a little porter at my dinner—I took nothing that did me any harm, I was perfectly myself—I do not usually go to bed so early as seven o'clock, except from fatigue or illness—I had no punch-bowl in the house that night—I did not ask the prisoner to go up stairs with me, she offered to do so—I did not put the £5 note into any punch-bowl, or any basin—I put it into my purse, and in the table-drawer—I did not put it into the slop-basin at tea—the prisoner went away about half-past seven o'clock, and I got out of bed about ten minutes after—I went down, as I fancied I had mislaid my money, and wished to be sure—I searched in the closet, under the sofa, and in the boxes, and every where I could think of, I was full an hour searching—I then went to bed again—I did not call up my landlady, but she came up to me. WILLIAM VICKRESS. I keep the White Hart, in the Walworth-road, about half or three quarters of a mile from the prosecutrix. On the 14th of January, between seven and ten o'clock in the evening, I gave the pri soner change for this note—I knew her before, and have not a doubt of her being the person—I knew her name, and put it on it myself—I have written "Sagger, Manor-place, 14 January, 39"—I afterwards paid the note to the Excise.
