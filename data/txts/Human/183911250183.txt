Before Mr. Common Serjeant.
 267. WILLIAM BROWN and JAMES SMITH were indicted for a conspiracy.
   MESSRS. BODKIN and CHAMBERS conducted the Prosecution. RICHARD CLARKE. I am a coach-maker in Stanhope-place, Southwark-bridge-road. A few days before the 2nd of September Brown came to my shop—he asked if I had a headed phaeton to let on hire—I said, "Yes," and showed him one—he did not approve of it—I showed him another one, for which we agreed—he wished to hire it for three months—he told me he was a ship and insurance broker—that his offices were at No. 18, East India-chambers, and he resided at Blackheath—I saw him again on the 2nd of September, when he brought a memorandum of agree-ment with him—this is it—he inquired if the phaeton was ready for him—I told him it was, and he produced this memorandum, which he signed in my presence, and he brought another, which I signed—a man from a livery-stable brought a horse, which, was harnessed to the phaeton and he drove away—I saw him again repeatedly—in about a week after, he said he would buy the phaeton for a friend of his who liked it, if I would take 30 l . instead of 35 l .—I said I could not take that, and then he inquired for a new britska—he looked at one and made me an offer for it—I think I told him 110 l . as the price—he offered 90 l . which I refused—I never agreed to take 90 l .—he came a great many times after that about the britska—on some of these occasions Smith was with him—Brown came almost every day; perhaps five or six times—I agreed to sell the britska for 95 l ., a German shutter and some other things were to be added to it—Isaw both the prisoners several times—Smith generally came with Brown in a phaeton which was hired of Mr. Gray—on—the 16th of September Brown came and asked if his clerk had been there—I did not know who he meant—I said, "No"—he said he should have been there half-an-hour ago, with a check for 100 l . to pay me for the britska—while we were talking Smith came in with a check for 550 l ., and gave it into Brown's hand—he said "What do you bring me this check for? this is 550 l .; you should have brought me a check for 100 l ."—Smith said that was what Mr. Marsden had given him—Brown scolded Smith for not bringing him the cheek he ordered—it was dated forward—Brown said to Smith, "This is making it appear as if I wanted to do Mr. Clarke out of the britska"—he said to me," This is a very vexatious thing that he should have brought this—I can't pay you for it now, and I have made an arrangement to use this carriage—I wanted to use it for two or three days to go into the country—this will entirely put me out of my arrangement"—he told me that Marsden was a friend of his, in the corn-trade—that he had lent him 550 l . to make purchases in corn, and as corn was getting up, he thought it was a good speculation, and the money was to be paid when he demanded it—that he had seen Marsden and told him he had pur-chased a new britska and wanted a check for 100 l . to pay for it; "and now," says he, "I suppose he thinks I want the money, and he has written me out this check, which has a few days to run, thinking it would be of service to him"—he then proposed to give me a bill, and he sent Smith for a stamp—it rained fast and Smith asked if I would lend him an umbrella—I went to get one, but had not one at home—after that Smith went out and was some time gone, and Brown and I had some conversation—I said "A gentle-man's word is as good as his bill; I don't require a bill if you will pay me the money before Saturday night"—he said, "The money shall be sure to
