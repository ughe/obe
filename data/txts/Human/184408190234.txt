I am a consulting surgeon. I am not at all acquainted with the prisoner or his friends—Cowling told me the prisoner was to be tried for manslaughter—I said perhaps it was important for him to have as many witnesses as he could, and as a matter of justice I had no ob jection to state what I knew—I was riding upon the box of Cowling's omni bus this day with the driver—we were going very slowly, and were very close to Great Charlotte-street—I saw the prisoner coming along on horseback, at the rate of seven or eight miles an hour—the horse lifted his feet very high, and made a great noise, something like what they call a butcher's trot—when about half way across Black friars-road the prisoner hallooed out—the deceased was passing along from Rowland Hill's chapel, as if going along Blackfriars-road towards the Surrey gate—he was not going along the crossing that is used there—he was further in Little Charlotte-street—the boy was with him—they made a movement to cross before the horse, after the prisoner had called out—I did not notice the deceased's hat fall off, but he seemed to make a halt and hesitation, and turned round—the little boy ran back, leaving him in the road, and it was then that he was struck by the horse's shoulder—the prisoner was not beating or spurring the horse at that time that I saw—he checked when he hallooed out—he might have stopped the horse entirely, but I considered at the time it was the deceased's hesitation, and turning round in the road which caused the coalition—I should say the prisoner pulled up his horse within six yards of the deceased from the place where he was struck—he turned back of his own accord, and rode back to the accident, and then the omnibus I was on drove on—I had no idea the coalition would have killed the deceased—he was not thrown forcibly on the ground, but made a rotary motion, and then fell—I do not think there was any kick, as from the position of the parties, if there had been, it would have been at the lower part of the man, not his head—I did not observe anything furious in the prisoner's driving—it was not a place which was likely to be dangerous—I consider he gave sufficient warning, that if there bad been more persons on the crossing they might have got out of the way.
   MR. O'BRIEN . Q.  You could only see him when he came on the road? A.  Yes, I saw him about ten yards before that—whether he was spurring or whipping his horse previous to that, I do not know—I heard the prisoner halloo out twice at least—I did not hear any one halloo out previous to him—I did not hear persons cry out after the accident, "Stop him"—I was in a condition to have heard it—I will not say no one did cry out. JOHN KELLY. I wish to correct this gentleman's statement; it was two men that ran from each side of the road that stopped the horse—it was at least sixty yards from the place. Witness.  It is utterly false—there was not a person so situated that they could stop him—there was only one person went after him, and he did not reach him before he turned back. WILLIAM LITTLE. I am a farrier, and live in King-street, Lambeth. I know the horse the prisoner was riding on that day—it is an old horse—I should say it was not capable of going at any extravagant rate—he would go at the rate of six or seven miles an hour—one of his fore legs is a stiff leg—he cannot bend it.
