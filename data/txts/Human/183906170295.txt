   Q.  Then what did he give him these tools for? A.  These sort of wood tools the men generally find themselves—apprentices generally do so in my master's service—I did not know where I was to look for the tools when I went to the prisoner's, I had no notion where I was going—my master, and I, and his nephew, and the prisoner, all went to the police-office—my master and the prisoner had some conversation—I saw the pri soner give up his keys to the officer—we went to his place—the prisoner went through the passage to the back shop, then he returned from the back shop suddenly, as if he had made a mistake, and opened the bed-room door first—he did not wait any where before he went Co the police-office—I had nothing said to me about what I was to do—I had not the prisoner in custody—he walked to the hall voluntarily—I was never told to take care of him—nothing of the kind. ELISHA CREASY. I am an officer—I went to the prisoner's bed-room and shop—we found some tools, which were brought away—before the Magistrate, the prisoner pointed out some tools which were his, and some he said were his master's—the warrant was obtained first, and Mr. Sadler came with the prisoner to the door—I went out with the warrant, and went to his lodging—he offered me every assistance to search his place—the taps and tools were found there—this chisel I found in his jacket pocket, with Mr. Sadler's name on it.
   MR. CLARKSON called SOPHIA LEWIS. My husband is barge-toaster to the Waterman's Com pany, and a fellowship-porter—a lad whom I brought up is in the prosecu tor's service—I don't know the prisoner, I never saw him but once—I know Mrs. Cook and Mrs. Newland—I know the witness Clark very Well—on the 5th of June I met him and walked to the house of Mrs. Cook—Mrs. Newland was at that house—the prisoner being in custody, was the subject of conversation—Clark said he was quite aware of the prisoner haying taps and dies, that he had lent them frequently to him, and he had brought them back again—he said he was aware of his having knife-shanks, and Mr. Sadler had frequently lent him tools to work with—he said, "If we don't get Batchelor transported for fourteen years, I shall be out of employ and Sadler will have nothing to do." ROSE COOK. I am the wife of William Cook, a lighterman—on Wednes day, the 5th of June, Sophia Lewis came to my house with Clark, about ten minutes after one o'clock—Elizabeth Newland was there—the discourse turned on a young man of the name of George Batchelor, being in prison—I never knew him nor the prosecutor, in my life—Clark said he was aware that the prisoner had had taps, and dies, and other tools, and he had frequently lent them to him, and he had brought them back—he said he was aware of his having had some knife-shanks, for Mr. Sadler lent the journeymen and apprentices tools to work with, and he said, "If we don't get him transported for fourteen years, I shall be out of employ, and Sadler will have nothing to do"—I was so shocked, that I wrote to the prisoner's father, (I did not know the address, till Clark gave it me,) and after doing that I went to Mr. Humphrey's office, and stated this.
