WIILIAM COUNSELL. I live at Camberwell, and am a short-hand writer—on the evening of Friday, 28th July, I was present at a meeting at the Chartist Hall, Webber-street, Blackfriars'-road—I got there shortly before eight o'clock—I should say there were from 150 to 200 people present—the number increased afterwards to about 500—they appeared to be working men—a person named Bryson was elected chairman—I heard the defendant speak there—three persons had spoken before him, one was a person of the name of Keene—I took a short-hand note of Shell's speech—this is it—( reads  —"Mr. Chairman and fellow-workingmen—Being called upon to come forward and propose the second resolution to be submitted to your notice this evening, I do so with feelings of great pleasure and satisfaction, because the opportunity which now presents itself leads me to embrace that opportunity for the pur pose of expressing my opinion as to what I consider to be the policy of the English Government towards that unfortunate country, Ireland, as well as to express my determination and disapprobation of the same. The resolution I submit to you is as follows:—'Resolved, that this meeting do solemnly protest against the base and unconstitutional act of the British Legislature, as committed by them in the suspending of the Habeas Corpus Act in Ireland; and further, do hereby tender to Feargus O'Connor, Esq., Member of Parliament, and to those gentlemen who supported him, our best thanks for their manly and patriotic conduct on that occasion; and further, that we do hereby solemnly pledge ourselves, that we will render in the hour of trial our full aid and co-operation to our Irish brethren.' In making a few remarks upon the resolution I have submitted to you, you will at once perceive that the resolution which I have moved may fairly be divided into three points; first, condemnation; secondly, commendation; and thirdly, de termination. In reference to the act of the Legislature in suspending the Habeas Corpus Act in Ireland, it may-perhaps be necessary, for a few moments, that we take a retrospective view of the proceedings of that Legis lature in connection with Irish affairs. If we look back to history, and trace the proceedings from the time of the Union up to the present, we shall find that the policy of the English Government towards that unhappy country has been one systematic system of coercion. The Irish people at the time of the Union were promised equality with the English people; they were promised that they should have equal laws and equal justice with the English people. Now, Mr. Chairman, although I have not condemned the acts of the Legislature in connection with my own country, I have still to condemn, and more justly too, I think, the acts of the Legislature in connection with Ireland, whenever and by whomsoever an appeal has been made to the Legislature of this country for justice to the Irish people, it has always been met by a system of coercion entirely; the English people have been coerced as well, and more especially of late, than they formerly were. You will remember that the English people, as well as the Irish people, have wrongs which they justly complain of, and they complain most reasonably and most justly too, because, as men and Englishmen, and as the wealth-producers of this country, they consider they have a right to a fair repre sentation in the House of Commons, which at present they are denied. Such is the case with Ireland; and until, as my friend who moved the first resolution observed, Ireland is fairly represented by the people and for the people, no good will ever accrue either to the one country or the other.
