   Prisoner. Q.  Did I speak to you or the landlady? A.  To me—she went into the room first, but I was close to her—she opened the door and asked if Stephen was at home—some one answered, but I did not hear the answer—the door was on the jar, and I was close behind her on the top of the stairs—there was another man sleeping in the room—you said the coat was yours—I saw it lying on the floor. JOHN PLOWMAN. I live at 3, St. Ann's-road, Brixton, and know the prisoner—he lived at that house in Sept., and had done so about eighteen months—I saw him there on 19th Sept.—the razor produced is mine—about four o'clock in the afternoon of that day, I laid it up on the shelf in the room where I shaved with it—on that afternoon I saw the prisoner with a pistol in his hand—he gave it me to go and shoot it off—I said, "I would not, it was loaded too heavy;" and he took it and fired it off himself, and broke the stock off—this is the same pistol.
   Prisoner, Q.  Did you ever see the pistol afterwards? A.  No; I left the razor in the washhouse, on a shelf at the side of the fire-place. EDWARD RAY. I am a surgeon at Dulwich. On the night of 19th Sept., the prosecutrix was brought to me by Hunt—her head, face, neck, hands, and client, were covered with blood—I found the head and face extensively bruised, and several small contused wounds on the scalp—they were such injuries as might be inflicted by a blunt instrument—they might have been produced by the handle of a pistol—from the appearance the blows were very violent—there was a deep gash in the front part of her throat, about three or four inches long, which penetrated the windpipe just below the root of the tongue, and through this wound she was breathing—on the right side of the neck there was another wound of the same extent, but only penetrating the skin—they were such wounds as would be inflicted by a cutting instrument, and might have been produced by this razor—on her hands I found several wounds, one of them laying open the joint at the root of the forefinger of the left-hand—they were such injuries as might have been caused by her grasping a sharp instrument—there were wounds on both hands—the wound on the neck, and the bruises on the head were likely to prove fatal—I had her con veyed to the hospital, after doing what was necessary. CHARLES SAUNDERS. I am a medical student, at Guy's Hospital. The prosecutrix was brought there on 20th Sept., and 1 attended to her under, Mr. Bransby Cooper's directions, for some time—I considered her life was in danger until the wounds had closed—that was about three weeks or a month. Prisoner's Defence.  I did not tell her to go to the Registrar's-office at all; she showed me a paper on the evening of Wednesday, 25th, and tried to persuade me to go home with her to her lodging; I did not go till the 28th, Saturday night; and I went once or twice after that, perhaps three or four times, and when 1 found out in what state she was I had no more to do with her; I knew it could not be by me, as that was the first improper connection I had with her, on 28th July; when she found I had left her, she constantly kept sending threatening letters to me, saying she would go home to her friends and say it was me that was the cause of it, and that was the cause of a quarrel whenever we met; I did not get her to write any letters that night; that is all I have to say. GUILTY. Aged 17.— DEATH.
