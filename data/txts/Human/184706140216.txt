   Q.  How near the position of the heart was the wound? A.  It was in a true line between the fourth and eighth rib.
   Prisoner. Q.  Could not the appearance that you speak of be produced by a person striking my arm after the wound was inflicted? A.  It might, or the extension might be produced by drawing the knife out.
   COURT . Q.  Supposing the knife to be in the wound, a blow under or above would lengthen it? A.  Yes—it might be attributable to that cause, or to the mode of drawing out the knife—I think the arm must have been close to the breast when the side was struck, and that the prisoner stood somewhat backwards—I think it was a back-handed stroke.
   Prisoner. Q.  Your opinion is that I must have been in front? A.  No.
   COURT . Q.  A person near the arm, but behind, could have made that stab? A.  Certainly; but the arm is capable of being moved in so many positions that it is impossible to say where the blow was aimed—I question whether the prisoner is of sound mind.
   Prisoner's Defence.  I have heard the evidence, but that is all I know of the transaction; I know no more about it than as a dream; my temper is bad at times; my mind has been repeatedly irritated; my wife knows my temper to be hasty; how could a poor lieutenant on half-pay bring up a family if he spent his money in drunkenness? I commit my soul to my God, and my person to my fellow-creatures; this will be depriving me of my half-pay.
   COURT to MRS. BROCK . Q.  Has the prisoner ever shown any extrav gance of mind, or want of balance of mind? A.  Repeatedly—he has laid on the bed like a man raving mad, beating the cloths, and tearing himself when intoxicated—when he has been sober he has got up at one or two o'clock in the morning and smoked his pipe at the window, and said, "Oh dear me!"—he has been wounded in the head—he has got a sear—I never applied to any medical man to induce him to put him under any partial restraint—I always considered that if he lived many years he would go out of his mind, raving mad—as he got old he got worse—more than twenty years ago he leaped through a window, glass, frame and all—a person had locked him in—he was then sober—the other day he told me it was a wonder he did not go through the wall instead of the door; and in many other things he has spoken very inconsistently—he reads a good deal, and instructs his children, but he scarcely ever has patience to hear them—he would work himself up in such a way that he could scarcely do it—sometimes he would be in doors a month or six weeks together, and then these fits come on, and he would go out at one or two o'clock in the night.
   JURY . Q.  Did he complain of his head? A.  Yes—if he goes to stoop he puts his hand to his head and says, "Oh my head!"—he would not stoop to do anything—that is when he has been drinking—it has always been my impression that he would go out of his mind—he was very quiet some-times; you would not know a person was in the house, and sometimes his tongue would begin and go on till two or three o'clock in the night, without knowing what he was talking about—he has been to the station-house re peatedly and given information against two policemen—a man who heard his conversation at the window called him a crack-brained fool for the way he was going on, knowing he was not right—it was at certain times, about the new and full moon, that he was worse. NOT GUILTY, being Insane.
