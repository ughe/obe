WILLIAM CRAWLEY( the prisoner ). I am a carman and coal-merchant—Peacock employed me on 14th Dec. to fetch some flour from the railway station—he first spoke to me about fetching it as I was washing my van before my own door one morning between twelve and one o'clock—he said, "Holloa, Crawley, how are you?"—I said I was rather slack of work—he said, "I can give you an order to cart some coals"—I said, "I expect a country job; I cannot do that"—he said, "Well, I expect I shall buy some flour worth the money;" and he asked me how much I would cart it for—I told him 6 d . a sack—he said it was to be carted from the Bricklayers' Arms station to his house—I said I would not do it under 6 d . a sack; he said he could get it done for 4 d .—I said I did not care about it—a few days after, I was coming out of my door to go after some coals in my cart, and he said, "Good morn ing;" I said, "Good morning"—he said, "I have got an order for twenty-five sacks of flour, it belongs to a man of the name of Roberts"—he gave me this order ( looking at it ) for twenty-five sacks—he told me to go and get them; and he said, "Sign Roberts, or what you like"—he told me I was to get it early in the morning, but it was all coming to his place—to the best of my belief it was about three or four o'clock in the afternoon when he brought this order—I said I could not get it away myself with my horse and van—he said, "Can you hire?" I said, "Yes, I can; I often hire from Mr. Boddy, close by me"—we went to the public-house, and had a drop of gin—I think he stood one drop, and I stood another—I went down to Boddy's in the even ing; I saw his foreman, and made an arrangement with him for the following morning-next morning I went to the railway station, about half-past six o'clock; I took my own van and horse, and Boddy's man went with me with his cart and horse-when I got to the station I gave in this order to a person there; he said, "You must wait, you cannot have the flour till the delivery-foreman comes"—I went to a coffee-shop, and while I was there, a tall man came in, and in consequence of what he said I went to the station and obtained the twenty-five sacks of flour, leaving the order there—the man who came into the coffee-shop helped to load the flour—I afterwards described that man to Peacock, and he said he thought, by the description I gave of the man, that he was the man of whom he bought some of the flour, of the name of Bailey (that was when we were in prison)—having obtained the flour, I drew it from the station, and took it to Peacock's shop in St. George's-road, near the Elephant and Castle, it was there unloaded, and placed in his shop—I noticed the mark of "J. M." was on the sacks—Peacock then went with roe, and we had some half-and-half to drink—I was not paid in money, I had a sack of oats for my horse and some chaff to the value of 12 s . 6 d ., which the job came to—he asked me if I had signed the name of Roberts in the book—I said, "Yes;" he said, "That is all right, old fellow"—I had signed the name of "Roberts"—a few days afterwards, on 20th Dec, I went again to the station—it was on Monday morning, about half-past six o'clock, when I started from Boddy's—I took an order there for twenty sacks—this is the order, I received it from my wife on the Sunday night when I came home, and she told me something, in consequence of which I got ready, and went on the Monday morning—I got Boddy's man and one of his horses—the order I got was signed "Roberts"—I got the twenty sacks, and took them to Peacock's house—I was paid for that in corn and chaff—he asked me if I had signed the name of "Roberts"—I said, "Yes;" he said, "That is all right, old fellow" Peacock said he bought the orders of two men, Bailey and Wilson; and had
