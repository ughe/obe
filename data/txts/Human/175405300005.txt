 The Note read to this purport.
 ' Wakefield, Nov. 15, 1753.
 ' Mr. Dickson,
 ' Pay to Mr.  Thomas Jones, or his order, one ' month after date, the sum of 36 l. for value re ' ceived, and place it to the account of
 ' Yours, &c. '  John Richardson.'
 36 l.
 To Mr.  James Dickson, merchant, in London.
   Thomas Jones on the back. Accepted James Dickson.
 To Messrs. Hinton, Brown and son.
   James Dickson. I have given Messrs. Brown an order whenever he sees a bill accepted by me, to pay it when due, if brought to his shop.
 Q. Have you been used to keep cash with him?
   James Dickson. I have these five years.
 Q. Look at this bill of Exchange; do you see the name Richardson from Wakefield? Have you such a correspondent there?
   James Dickson. No, I have not, nor ever had any advice of such a bill being drawn; I have no concerns in the town of any kind.
 Q. Do you know any thing of this endorser,  Thomas Jones?
   James Dickson. No, I do not; I knew the prisoner when he lived with Messrs. Simpson's on Tower-Hill, and he was clerk there some time, and at that time he had great many draughts from me to carry to Messrs. Brown and son.
   John Greenway. I know Mr. Dickson, I have seen him write, I have been clerk to him for some years, and know his hand-writing very well.
 Q. Look at this bill of Exchange, do you know that writing, or any of it?
   John Greenway. These words, accepted James Dickson, are not Mr. Dickson's hand-writing, nor no part of it, it has not any resemblance of it.
 Q. Has Mr. Dickson any correspondent by the name of Richardson, at Wakefield in Yorkshire.
   John Greenway. I do not know any such name.
 Q. to Alnutt. If there is a direction on the bottom of a bill, as there is to this upon the banker, does that amount to a warrant or order for the banker to pay the money?
 Alnutt. Without doubt it has.
 Prisoner's defence.
 That bill I received of  Thomas Jones, a Lincolnshire grazier. I have nothing to say, I throw myself entirely upon the mercy of the court; I have no evidence here at all.
     Guilty, Death.
 309. (M.)  Elizabeth, the  wife of William Negus was indicted for stealing one gold necklace, one coral necklace, one gold mourning ring, one gold ring set with a stone, one other gold ring, one silver spoon, one tortoiseshell snuff-box, one pair of shoe buckles, a silver snuff-box, and one silver thimble, the property of Joseph Mason and two silver shoe buckles, an oak cabinet, a printed book, called the Whole Duty of Man, and other things, which in the whole amounted to 2 l. 8 s. 6 d. the property of Hannah Painter in the dwelling-house of the said Hannah, Feb. 13.*
   Sarah Mason. I know the prisoner; Hannah Painter is my mother, the prisoner lodged with her, she lay with me.
 Q. When did she come to lodge there?
   Sarah Mason. I cannot say the day, but she went away the 13th of Feb.
 Q. How long had she lodged there?
   Sarah Mason. About a year and a half; when she went away I missed the cabinet, it stood in the chamber window up one pair of stairs, under a looking glass.
 Q. Was the cabinet locked, or not?
   Sarah Mason. Locked.
 She mentioned part of the things that were in the cabinet.
 Q. Were all the things you have mentioned your mother's, or yours?
   Sarah Mason. Joseph Mason is my husband, these things were mine.
 Q. Were there any thing else in this cabinet?
   Sarah Mason. There were two silver shoe buckles.
 Q. Whose were they?
   Sarah Mason. They were my mother's, and there were four silver tea-spoons.
 Q. Was not the Duty of Man there too?
   Sarah Mason. Yes, it was.
 Q. When did you see these things in the cabinet, and how long before she went away?
   Sarah Mason. I did not see the things, but the cabinet was there the morning she went away; I missed it in about half an hour after she was gone.
 Q. Did you ever have any of these things again?
   Sarah Mason. No, nor never heard any thing of them.
