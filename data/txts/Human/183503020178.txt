   Gorken's Defence.  I sold two cows for Newman a long time ago, and paid the money—she came to me several times, and asked me to send her customers for her cows, for she wanted to sell all her things—I said I un derstood she had engaged with a person named Bartram to sell her things, I did not wish to interrupt him in the business—she said she wished me to send her customers at home, she would sooner do it than expose them at market—her son and her afterwards took me to a public-house and treated me with gin, to get me down to look at the cows—a week afterwards she pressed me to sell them—I asked Sapsford if he wanted any cows, not knowing there was any business transaction between them—Sapsford said he was not well enough to go himself, as he had broke three blood-vessels, but would send a man to buy a cow—he sent me to fetch his man—he said to him, "Now, to-morrow morning, (Gorken will go with you,) go and look at Newman's cows, and if you see one worth the money, take it, send it home, and we can settle with them for it"—I took the man as a customer, and told her I had no doubt she would have the money—at last the man agreed with her for the cow—they were to send it home to the Elephant and Castle, Upper Kennington-lane—instead of taking it there, they took it to the Elephant and Castle, Newington, and could not find Eldridge, and brought it home—in the evening, as I was going that way, I went to her door—she said they had taken it to the Elephant and Castle, Newington—I said it was Upper Kennington-lane, and any body knew Mrs. Eldridge and her son, as she was an independent person—she asked me to show them where the place was—I said I would; and said I should be glad of the commission to take home to my family—I went with the son; on going up Kennington-lane, he said to me, "Why, a man named Sapsford lives up this lane"—I said, "Yes; I know him very well"—he said, "Yes; so do I know him; my father owed him for some grains, but he must look very sharp for it, for he will never pay him"—I took him to the shed, and went away. JACOB SAPSFORD. Eldridge was my cow-keeper—I have had dealings with Newman's father for grains—I have not seen him for the last five or six months—I met Newman and Eldridge at the Elephant and Castle—I had a bill against Newman's father—I went home and fetched the bills—I tendered him the difference, which was six guineas and fourpence halfpenny—the money was put on the table before him, and also the bills—he said, after he looked round, that he should not look to me for it at all—when he found I did not offer him the full amount, he said he should look to Eldridge—I told him I had been many times to his house after the money, and the last time but one that I went, Mrs. Newman told me her husband was gone away and had taken the money away, and she did not mean to pay his debts—I asked young Newman if that was the case—he said that he did not mean to pay his father's debts—I spoke to him about the balance his father owed me, ten times before this occurrence—he owed me 5 l . 3 s . 7 1/2 d .—the cow is in my place now—Eldridge had no property in the cow whatever.
   MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  Had Gorken any thing to do with it? A.  I asked him to go with my man to buy it, and he did—he acted only as a jobber.
