1900. CHARLES SLOANE was indicted for stealing, on the   29th of May , 2 1/2 pints of gin, value 3 s  ., the goods of John Haynes , his master. JOHN HAYNES. I keep the Thistle and Crown public-house, in St. Martin's-lane—the prisoner was my servant. In consequence of suspicion, about six in the evening of the 29th of June, I went and found the prisoner down stairs—I asked what he was doing there—he said, "Nothing"—I told him he might go up stairs—I searched round the place, and found in the saw-dust place a bottle of gin—I went up to him and said, "These are the tricks you have been up to, are they?"—he said he knew nothing about it—I sent for a policeman, who went down and found another bottle of gin in the saw-dust tub, another in the copper-hole, and another behind the tub where the prisoner scours the pots—the prisoner said, "For God's sake, forgive me, I will tell the truth"—I said, "I cannot think of forgiving you.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  Are you quite sure you did not say, "You had better tell the truth, and it was of no use to deny it?" A.  I might have said so. GEORGE WESTON( police-sergeant F  6.) I found this bottle of gin in the saw-dust.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you hear his master say it was no use to deny it, he had better tell the truth? A.  I heard something of that kind said, but I was searching round the cellar. NOT GUILTY.
 1901. JAMES ADAMS was indicted for stealing, on the   5th of June , 72 bottles, value 18 s  ., and 1 crate, 2 s  ., the goods of Timothy Maber . JOHN HAYWARD. I am in the employ of Timothy Maber, a wine-merchant at Islington. The prisoner was formerly in his service—about nine o'clock, on the 5th of June, I went into the back-yard and saw the prisoner in the act of taking these bottles off the premises in a crate—I let him go outside—I followed—he ran away when I called stop thief; but was stopped—this is my master's crate.
   Cross-examined by MR. HORRY . Q.  You told him to bring them back? A.  Yes, I asked what he was going to do? he said he was not going to take them away—I never said that he said he was going to take, them away—I desired him to take them in again—he did so, and put them down in the yard—I knew he lived at Bagnige Wells. JOHN KELLY( police-constable N  360.) I took the prisoner running down Edward-street, towards Thornhill-bridge.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Was he running in a direction towards Btgnige Wells-road? A.  Yes.
   MR. HORRY called MARY ALLANSON. My husband is employed at a warehouse. I live in Wild-street—I have known the prisoner ever since he was ten years old—when he is a little excited he would not know what he was about—when he was very young his father struck him on the head. EDWARD GARDNER. I am a glass and bottle dealer, in Great William-street. The prisoner was in my service five years—he used frequently to com plain of his head. SARAH SHUDMAN. My husband is a carman, and lives at Kennington. The prisoner is my brother—his head is very bad; he frequently complains that he is out of his mind, and fancies he is mad—when he has taken a drop of strong liquor he is half frantic, and does not know what he is doing. JOHN HAYWARD( re-examined. ) I did not see any difference in the prisoner to what he was before—I never saw him in these flighty fancies.
