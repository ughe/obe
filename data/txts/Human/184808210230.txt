I have no doubt that most of you have presided at a public meeting during some period of your life, and, therefore, you are aware that some warmth of language is required on the part of the chairman, that the audience may be made acquainted with the nature of the motives for calling them together; and if I, on the 28th ult., while acting as chairman, gave utterance to language con trary to law, it was nevertheless dictated by views of humanity. We had received the day before, by public intelligence, a brief account of Irish dis turbances; and as that people were struggling for political freedom, with held from them by the same power that kept us in political thraldom, it was but natural we should tender our sympathies at such a crisis, and particularly when our grievances were so closely allied; and I do not think there was anything dangerous in the reply I made to Mr. Rooney, that we were willing to co-operate for the amelioration of the Irish people; common courtery required me to say as much, seeing that that gentleman headed a deputation of fifty members from the Mitchell Club; and certainly, had I returned a less favourable answer, there can be little doubt that a disturbance would have been the consequence, because there were a great number of policemen in disguise, in the room at the time, and they had made several attempts to disturb the meeting during the evening, and a greater number outside fully armed and ready to act at a moment's notice, fully determined to break up the meeting if possible prematurely. I was aware of their intention both inside and outside the room, for I had just received a note from one of those sent to watch their proceedings, telling me to endea vour to keep order if possibly could, because, said he, 'the butchers outside," this is the policemen, I do not disguise the fact, "are evidently bent on: slaughter, and all they want is a pretext to begin." That the police are capable of doing such things you are all very well aware, from the late in vestigation into the Bishop Bonner's-field police onslaught. It appears, however, that I conducted the meeting to its close without the least approxi mation to disorder. Then as for putting the public into great alarm, as the learned gentleman has stated, with respect to the Life and Property Protection Society, I can only tell him that such things are very common, and I should suppose he had seen similar societies in this country and other parts of Europe: it is certainly called a Life and Property Protection Society, and that was by the advice of counsel, and also by the advice of the Mayor of Nottingham, and the society founded in the city of Nottingham was approved of by the Mayor of that city; and our rules are the same. It has nothing to do with the Chartist Association, and in fact that society does not exist at the present. time in any locality that I am aware of, because it was assumed that the Chartist Association was sufficient to answer all the purposes.
