   Cross-examined by MR. PAYNE . Q.  Have you told us all that passed between you and the female and this man, before he went away, saying he would give you something? A.  There were a great many very abusive words passed with her—she said she would make as much row as she liked—she was very drunk—I believe she staggered down—I think I gave her a slight push, just to move her on, because she stood calling me in such a way—I did not see her head go against the stones, nor did I hear it—she staggered, and fell down at a distance from me, after I gave her the slight push—her head did not come in contact with the stones that I know of—that occurred before the prisoner said he would give me something—he was not gone above a minute, or a minute and a half, before he came back again—he went round into Norfolk-street—he lives there, and is a barber by trade—when he came rushing at me with this poker, or whatever it was, I drew my staff—I cannot tell whether I touched him with it or not; I think I did, when I was on the pound, touch his legs once or twice, to keep him at a distance, so that he should not hit me—after he got up and went into the house, I followed him—there was only one man in the house before me, and sergeant Golding fol lowed me in—only me and 285 had hold of the prisoner at the time he cut me; it was done nearly the moment we got hold of him—after he had used the razor I hit him on the head, which cut him back entirely—I believe this was one of the days of Camberwell fair; at least, this was on the Tuesday morning, about a quarter to I, and Camberwell fair was on the Monday—the prisoner did not appear the worse for liquor—the woman was not struck by any of us that I know of—I never struck her—I cannot say that she was or was not struck, as I left the house before the man was brought out.
   MR. BURNIE . Q.  You say you touched him when he was on the ground; was that before or after he struck you with the poker? A.  After he had felled me to the ground.
