JESSE BRAY. I am clerk to an attorney, and live in Prince's-road, Ken nington-cross. About one o'clock on the night in question. I was going along the Borough, and heard a rattle spring—I saw the prisoner running, pursued by the policeman—I did not stop him, but I saw him secured, and am sure he is the man—when he came up to me I stepped off the path, and attempted to stop him, but seeing his arm raised, I had an idea that he had a weapon—I stepped off the path, intending to attack him sideways—he struck at me, and I at him—he passed me, turned the corner, then turned with his face to the wall, and walked back again, as if going towards St. George's church—I did not lose sight of him, and am certain he is the man.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What were you doing out at this time of night? A.  I was walking down the Borough, going home—I am in the employ of Mr. Devenport, of Ely-place—I was coming from Tooley-street, where I had been spending the evening with a few friends—I had drank nothing but porter, and not exceeding three pints of that—I have some slight knowledge of having seen the prisoner before, somewhere about the Elephant and Castle—he only turned the corner about three yards—I ran past him for about two yards—I saw nobody in Union-street but the prisoner, Bray, and myself—I went back with him to the prosecutor's—there was a crowd round the door afterwards, but not at the time I came up—a crowd followed us from where we took the prisoner into custody, about ten yards from the corner of Union-street. WILLIAM APTHORPE. I am a private watchman at the Half Moon in the Borough. I was outside the gate of the Half Moon on the night in question, and saw the prisoner coming towards me, followed by the policeman, hal loing, "Stop thief!"—I am sure he is the man—I saw him drop the bag produced from under his coat—I picked it up and gave it to the policeman when he came back with the prisoner in charge.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you a watch-box there? A.  No, I walk up and down the yard, and sometimes sit down—I had been on duty since nine o'clock—I was standing outside the gate at the time—[it is sleepy work—it is a very light neighbourhood. WILLIAM PARNELL.( policeman, M  5.) I saw the prisoner in company with two others, in the neighbourhood of Blackman-street, Borough, between half-past four or five o'clock on the afternoon in question—I am quite certain he is the person—he was only a short distance from the prosecutor's house—they were walking along together in the direction of St. George's church—I did not see him again till next morning.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Then you saw him in custody? A.  Yes, in the cells—they were not walking in a direction towards the Surrey Theatre, but coming from it—I was going towards it—I met no other parties that after noon that took my notice—I was not examined before the Magistrate. DAVID HANSELL( policeman, M  117.) I was on the spot, and picked up this lantern in Mr. Gilpin's shop, about two yards from the door.
   MR. GILPIN re-examined.  This is not my lantern—it was not there over night.
 (The prisoner received a good character.) GUILTY . Aged 26.— Confined One Year .
