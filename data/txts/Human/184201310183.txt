I have known the prisoner near upon a fortnight—on Wednesday evening, the 19th of January, between eight and nine o'clock, I met him near the Corn wall-road—he gave me a trifle of money that evening—I cannot swear how much, but I should say between 3 l . and 4 l . in silver—I saw he had some gold, but cannot say how much—I bought a new bonnet and a number of other things with the money, and some he brought himself—he had never given me such a sum of money before, not more than 5 s . or. 6 s . at the most—he had given me about that much on the Saturday evening—he brought the moleskin-dress, an 1 the pilot-coat himself—the silver he gave me was mostly in shillings, and there were about 2 s . worth of. four penny-pieces, and two or three half-crowns. JOSEPH CROUCH. I am a carpenter, employed on the prosecutors' pre mises, and have a little workshop there. On Wednesday, the 19th of January, about half-past five or six o'clock, the prisoner came to my workshop, and asked for a chisel to ease a door—I asked what for, and said he would spoil any door on the premises—he took me to the folding doors, which are on the flooring of the soap-factory, in a line from my shop to the coppers, and said, "This is what I want to ease"—I told him if he did such a thing, he would injure the door, and I would not allow it—he then left me, and went to work at the coppers—in about half-an-hour I went down stairs to get a pail of soap-ashes, and found him against the counting-house without a light, between the copper and the counting-house—I asked why he was there without a light—he said he did not want one—I said it was very wrong his being there without a light, and then, left him—I had not been up stain above a quarter or half-an-hour, before he came up and wished me good night—that was about a quartet-past six—he then left, and never spoke to a soul there—I followed him with my light, but he shut the folding-doors, and I saw no more of him—the folding-doors are generally open till we all go away, that we may see persons come up the passage, but he shut them, which would prevent my seeing into the passage—we cannot see the counting-house from our door without coming outside—next morning I came to work just after seven, and found the position of two of my screw-drivers altered, placed different to what I had left them when I went away the night before—I went to the counting-house door and found marks on the desk and on the door, ex ternally and internally, which might have been made by either of those screw-drivers—the door must have been opened from the inside—it could not be opened outside—it throws back—I know the counting-house win dow well—it is large enough for me to get through.
   Prisoner's Defence.  I never asked the man for any chisel; there are other men employed on the premises: on the night of the robbery I was off the premises before any of the men left—there was an excise man there the whole day, and that day the collector was late; he was the last person there, I believe; I bid all the men good night when I left, and the boiler saw me out of the gate. GUILTY. Aged 19.— Confined Twelve Months.
   Before Mr. Recorder.
