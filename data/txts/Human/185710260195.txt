Cross-examined. Q.  Did the pot boy sleep in the room too? A.  Yes, and Welch slept with him.
   Seers. Q.  Was not there any one who slept with me? A.  Yes, a brick layer's lad—the pot boy did not always sleep in that room. JOHN GOLDING. I am foreman to Fletcher and Warden, butchers, at Kew—on 22nd Sept, about a quarter to 8 o'clock, Welch came for a mutton chop, and gave me a sovereign; I gave him 19 s . 7 d . change, and took it in doors to Miss Warden, who acts as cashier—Seers had been to my house that morning for some beef steak, which came to 7 d . or 9 d ., and paid me with a half crown—he did not appear to be drunk—I got change from Miss Warden—he said, "Well, I did not know you would have to go after change, or else I would have let it be till by-and-by "—I did not know what he meant—I have seen Welch at my house several times, purchasing things—Miss Warden afterwards gave me the sovereign, and I marked it, and gave it to the policeman—in the course of that day I went to Ridley's buildings to look for Welch, and found him doing something on the roof, in his shirt sleeves—he was in his jacket when he came to my house—I have no doubt at all that he is the person—Henry Smith was in the shop.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you any doubt now? A.  Not much, but I had at the time; so much so, that I left him to look for somebody else—I said that I could not see any person like the man—it was after that that I made up my mind that it must be Welch—the prisoner said something about not taking him into custody if I could not make up my mind—I had a strong suspicion, but there was a difference in his dress—he was not taken for an hour and a half or two hours after that—I have seen him come to my house, but I never saw him with anybody else; he Has come and bought articles several times.
   MR. CLERK . Q.  You told the prosecutor that you had a strong suspicion that Welch was the man; how near were you to him? A.  He was upon a high place and I was on the ground; he continued at his work while I went round the building—he was about eight feet above me, he had no conversa tion with me, or with the policeman—when he came away from the building with the policeman he had his jacket on, and then I could swear to him. SUSANNAH WARDEN. I act as cashier to Messrs. Fletcher and Warden. On 22nd Sept. Golding gave me a sovereign about a quarter to 8 o'clock in the morning; I gave him the change, half a sovereign and 9 s . 6 d . in silver—I put the sovereign into a little bowl which was empty—about two hours afterwards I gave it to Philip Nunn, the shop boy, and sent him to the Coach and Horses for change—he brought it back and I put it in a desk, where it remained till I gave it to the policeman—Golding marked it. PHILIP NUNN. I am shop boy to Messrs. Fletcher. On 22nd Sept. Miss Warden gave me a sovereign to get changed at Mr. Huntley's; I brought it back and gave it to her.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Do any of these men work about there? A.  I have seen Welch twice. HENRY SMITH. I am journeyman to Messrs. Fletcher. I remember Welch coming to buy a mutton chop, Golding served him—he gave a piece of money to Golding, who went to get change—I saw him give the change, a half sovereign, 9 s . 6 d . in silver, and 1 d .—I think I had seen Welch in the shop before, but not to take notice of him—I have not the least doubt of his being the man—Seers afterwards came for a bit of mutton, but he bought a bit of beefsteak—I saw Welch again on the building about 10 o'clock.
