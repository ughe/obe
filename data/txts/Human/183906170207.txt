Q.  Did Blundell give you any description of the man who took away the boxes? A.  To the best my recollection he could give very little description of him—I believe he did give some description—I remember he could speak very slightly of him, in fact I asked every one of the clerks—as for as my recollection serves me, Blundell gave a very slight description of the man, but he did give a description that he was dark, tall, and so on—I had seven or eight descriptions from different clerks there—some described him as one sort of man, and some as another sort—I had seven or eight differ ent descriptions of him from persons, the greater part of whom were con nected with Mr. Hartley's wharf and office—I think Blundell said he thought the man had grey whiskers, but he would not speak distinctly on it—I particularly asked him that—he certainly said he thought the man had grey whiskers—he did not give me that description of the man before Lewin Caspar gave me any description at all—that I swear positively—Lewin Caspar had mentioned about the grey whiskers to roe before I saw Blundell at the office—Blundell's description corresponded with Lewin Caspar's as to the whiskers, but not as to the person—Blundell did not describe the man's age at all—I did not ask him—I do not recollect that any thing passed between us respecting the man's age—I do not recol lect what he said about the man's age at all—I will not swear he did not describe him as about sixty years of age—I spoke to half a dozen clerks about it, and I cannot recollect—I spoke very little to Blundell about it at all.
   Q.  At what o'clock did Lewin Caspar leave you the night you met him and his father at the Swan with Two Necks? A.  I think about half-past ten, or between ten and eleven o'clock—it was Monday night—while we were in the coach, I expressed a wish to see Mr. De Mole before the bills were issued—he immediately desired his father to inform Mr. De Mole of it, and he got out of the coach for that purpose.
   MR. BODKIN . Q.  With respect to Bradley, did you take care of her at the express desire of the Magistrate? A.  Yes, and Mr. Hartley also—I have been an officer nearly twenty-one years—on my solemn oath I did not in any way suggest to her to go to Mr. Humphreys to hear her evidence read over—Mrs. Abrahams attended as a witness at the police-office on the 14th of April—it was very near the end of that day's examination—there were only one or two questions asked her—her examination was postponed—Solomon was not made a witness until public application had been made to the Magistrate, and their sanction obtained, on a statement of the cir cumstances—that was after the interview which took place between the counsel for the prosecution and the Magistrate in another room.
   COURT . Q.  This communication was in a private room was it? A.  Yes, it was with regard to Moss—I think Solomon was admitted as a witness two or three days after.
   MR. JONES . Q.  About what time was it that Ellis Caspar got out of the coach on your return from the railway? A.  As near as I can recollect about ten minutes or a quarter past nine o'clock—it might have been some thing later—it might be half-past nine o'clock, but it was not earlier than a quarter past.
