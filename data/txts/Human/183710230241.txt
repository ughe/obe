   MR. PAYNE . Q.  Is this the first time you have stated about the pri soner sitting next to the drawers, so that nobody could have got to them without his knowledge? A.  It is—it slipped my memory at the time the depositions were taken, but being mentioned to me afterwards, I recol lected it—there is no betting-club at my house, which meets after twelve o'clock—never since the house has been mine. CHARLOTTE NOONE. I am the prosecutor's wife. I recollect the pri soner and his brother being in the bar-parlour with my husband on the night in question—in consequence of a disturbance my husband came out, and gave me his bag of money, watch, and ring, to take care of, while he put the people out—I put the property into the drawer of the bar-parlour, and turned the key, but did not take the key out—there was nobody in the room but the prisoner and his brother—nobody else came there until the money was missed, which was about half an hour after they were gone—the prisoner sat with his back to the drawer—nobody could get to it till he moved—when I put it in he asked me if he should rise—I said, "No, lean forward if you please, and I can get at the drawer"—his brother was sitting opposite to him, very near to him—there was a light in the room—it would be utterly impossible for one of them to open the drawer and take the bag oat without the knowledge of the other—they were left alone in the bar-par lour while I went to the bar to serve customers—my husband vat on, the other side of the bar at that time
   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you ever said a word before about its being utterly impossible to get to the drawer without his knowledge? A.  I be lieve I have not—the prisoner's brother had lodged some time in our house. JAMES ANDREWS. I am a policeman. I took the prisoner into custody on Tuesday, the 26th of September—I told him the charge—he said he was very willing to go wherever I liked, he knew nothing about it—I was coming down from his house with him, and got as far as Newington-cause way, he said he would not go further till he had something to drink—I persuaded him to go further, and again he said he would have something to drink—I said, "Well, you can stand a drain,  I suppose you have. plenty of money then, as you want to drink"—he said "No, I have only 18 d ."we went into a public-house, searched him, and found on him three half-sovereigns, and 1 l . 12 s . in silver—I asked how he got it—he said he had saved it up to pay his rent—I have the money here—Mr. Noone examined it in the Inspector's room—before he examined it, he said some of the gold he could swear to—I then handed it to him, and he pointed out half a sovereign—he did not state why he could swear to it before he had looked at it.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you say any thing when you were examined before about his desiring to get something to drink? A.  No, I did not—I took him at his own house, about six o'clock, on the day of the robbery.
