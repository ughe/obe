HENRY WHITNEY. I was a police-inspector at this time, and on duty at
Kennington Road Station, when George Pilfold and Constable 40 L came
to me about 12.20, and from what they said I went to 9, Windmill Street.
and saw the deceased's body lying on the bed next to the wall--the prisoner
and his dumb son were there, and Walsh-on the right side of the hearth
I found a patch of blood about 12 inches by 8, and a great quantity of
blood about the room and about the bedding--the woman's clothes were
quite saturated-I sent for a doctor-I asked the prisoner his name--he
said “George Pilfold," and that he was a tinplate worker-I asked him if
the deceased was his wife-he said “She is not a wife to me, a
b g ood job if she was dead "-I said "I am afraid she is dead "_the

he proceeded to take the things out of his pocket, and I said "I will search
you myself” in the inside breast-pocket of his coat I found this brown-
handled knife-I could trace no blood on either of the knives--I saw blood
on the dumb boy's clothes, and asked the prisoner how his son came by it
- he said "He got that by lying down there," pointing to the blood on the
* hearth-the dumb boy made some signs, and another brother who was in
the room, not George, motioned to him to be quiet-I know that it meant
that because he ceased directly-the sergeant was acquainted with the sign
language a little, and he commenced to comunicate with the dumb boy,
and the same son said "He won't tell him anything, let him stop till to-
morrow and go to the gentleman at the schools. (The interpreter) -I said that
I should take the prisoner in custody on suspicion of murdering his wife-
he said "I will go with you"-I examined him, and found no blood on
him, and I handed him over to Sergeant Hawkins-I measured the room-
it measures 11 feet by 10-I made a survey, and there was no sign of any
struggle taking place-the bed is a piece of sacking with some flock--I also
looked at the chairs, but found no blood on them.
Cross-examined. There was nothing inconsistent with them both having
laid down and gone to sleep that night—the prisoner had not his coat and
waistcoat off when I went in-it was not necessary for me to search him-
he would have given me everything willingly-he did not make a show of
resistance or give me any trouble-he was drunk, and I do not think he
knew what he was about
