I did not make any memorandum of the notes I brought with me; it is not the custom in France; they were of the Bank of France; I do not know what Bank they were. The bills for 1000 francs were the same as the 500 franc bills in all respects except the amount. The 1000 franc billets de banque were like this one. I was a grocer at Paris. When I came here I left the business; I do not now carry it on. The bills I left with Miss Perrot were seven for 1000 francs, and five for 500 francs; I do not know whether I left more with her. I made an inventory for her when I was in Paris. I do not recollect whether I told her the amount of the bills; I gave them to her all together to take care of, in her bedroom. I have seen her open the drawers in my presence. I recollect they were put into a drawer; but I cannot tell what drawer. There was a box in her bed-room. I have no doubt that this is a translation of one of Miss Perrot's letters, made by herself - (looking at it) - I was at Mr. Askew's house at the time I gave the bills to Miss Perrot, and continued there about a month. I gave her the bills to take care of for fear of losing them at my furnished lodgings. I am not sure whether my box was in that room then. I did not put the bills into the box, because it is not comfortable to put them in with clothes. I received the bills in my shop. I cannot say that I took them all in the shop, but when I got 500 francs I have exchanged them in the Rue de Banc. I cannot tell the name of any one person from whom I received any one of them. I have been to an agent of exchange several times, but I do not know his name. I received one 1000 francs bill from a servant of the director of the prisons, Mons. Ronne; he is not a changer of money. I had more money in England. I had one more 1000 francs bill; I changed that near the Haymarket three or four weeks after I came here. I have not changed any other French paper money. I have not frequently received money from Perrot: I have never received any of her. I never received any communication respecting a situation as teacher, through a person named Pascan; I do not know the name. I never received any communication respecting my becoming teacher of French in a school, or heard any thing of it; and never applied for 30 l. to enable me to take it. When I became acquainted with Miss Perrot she was a very respectable person. I never went to her drawers to take money. It is possible I might have her keys, but I do not remember it. I had some French gold in the drawer. Sometimes I went to her drawer - very seldom - two or three times. Mr. Askew told me I should pass as her brother: he did object to my intimacy with her; that was always the subject of our quarrel. I have lodged in the same house with her, at Mrs. Broadfoot's. I did not say to Mrs. Broadfoot yesterday, that if she proved any thing against Miss Perrot or me, I would say she had asked me to sleep with her. I have said to her in her house,
 "Your conduct is abominable; you send your son to the Magistrate and destroy my character, and you know what I know, and what I can say." Mr. Askew took the lodging for me in that house; I had no quarrel with Mrs. Broadfoot till I found she was to be a witness against me. I was once going up stairs at Mr. Askew's, and Miss Perrot was showing my bills to Miss Hudson, and I said,
 "Why do you show them?" Miss Hudson was then in the room or on the same floor: Miss Hudson has told me she has seen them. I never said at Cheltenham, in Miss Hudson's presence,
 "Mr. Askew is a very bad man, and I will bring him as low as the ground." I never had any thing good to say of him. I have conversed with Mrs. Broadfoot, but won't undertake to state what I have said of Mr. Askew; I have always looked at him as the dirt. I never said I was married to Perrot, or that we were man and wife, nor has she in my hearing.
 Mr. PHILLIPS. Q. When you intended to come to England, did you keep a sum of money for that purpose - A. Yes. I brought near 11,000 francs. I gave some gold to Miss Perrot; I have been to her drawer for it. The Bank in Paris is a public Bank; I did not know the name of any person there. The gentleman who's servant brought me one of the bills, is a director of the prisons. I had never quarrelled with Broadfoot, till after Askew was complained of; on our arrival from Cheltenham she treated us as her children.
   GEORGE HUBERT. I am an attorney. I was engaged by Miss Perrot about the 1st of September. I went by her direction to Mr. Askew's, and demanded the property, of which she had furnished an inventory; this is the inventory; I believe I did not produce it the first time I went, but I requested a settlement of accounts between them; I claimed upwards of 70 l. for wages, and also the property she stated to be in his possession. He said what property she had was locked up in her chest of drawers; he said he had great complaints to make against her - she was a bad woman. I asked him how he could convince me of that; he said he had documents in his possession which would prove it; I requested him to show me them; he said
 "If I knew you better I would." I said
 "One document would be more convincing than anything he could say." Before I went to him, Madamoiselle Perrot had described a certain document which she said was in a chest of drawers, and when I asked Mr. Askew to show me the document on which he relied, he went up stairs, and brought down a letter; I read it two or three times. He said, perhaps, I did not understand the French language; he had a translation of it. I did understand it, and I said it was extremely extraordinary that he should be in possession of a confidential letter, and asked him how he had obtained it; he said it was of no consequence how he had obtained it; but he had got it.
