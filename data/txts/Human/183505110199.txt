CHARLES FIRMIN. I am a carpenter. I was with the prosecutor on Tuesday night, the 5th of May—we went to the Unicorn at West Ham, and afterwards to the Swan, at Stratford—I saw the three prisoners at both places; we left the Swan at half-past eleven o'clock, we were sober—the prisoners followed us out—we had a bit of a spar—we did not spar, but agreed to have a spar—both our jackets were off—I did not see the pri soners come up myself—I had seen his watch twice that evening—once in the Swan, and at the Unicorn—I afterwards saw Sims taken into custody.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q  What time did you go to the Uni corn? A.  At past nine o'clock—we drank there till eleven o'clock—I remem ber Parker taking his watch out at eleven o'clock—we were taking beer there,—we had no spirits at all—I should think I did not drink more than a pot—I will swear I did not drink two pots the whole day—I was not drunk—I can not say I was sober—I had my senses, and could walk very well, and so had Parker—he was a little elevated; he was a little fresh—we stopped at the Swanabout ten minutes, and had half-a-pint of gin-and-cloves—the prisoners had part of it—they followed us into the house—Parker said he should like to have a bit of a spar—he said he was able to stand against a man as big as a house, in a jocular sort of way—I consented to spar—I did not double my fist—Parker said he had lost his watch, two or three minutes after he came out—Hill was with the other prisoners—they were about a yard off—I did not see the prisoners when we were talking about sparring—not when I was pulling off my jacket—I did not notice how near they were to us at that time—Parker was not far from me—I did not see any thing done to him.
   Cross-examined by MR. BODKIN . Q.  Did you say any thing to either of the prisoners at the Swan? A.  No more than asking them to drink. SAMUEL GUTTERIDGE. I am a horse patrol. I received information from Parker on Wednesday morning, about four o'clock, and later in the morn ing I got the particulars from him—I went with him to a house of ill-fame in the Marsh, and found Ann Sullivan, who I knew well before—I brought her out to Parker, and said "I have an accusation against you, for robbing that man of his watch"—she said, "I never saw him—he cannot say so"—I took her into custody; and knowing that Sims lived with her in this house I went in search of him at Old Ford—I told him I apprehended him on suspicion of stealing, Parker's watch—he said, "I know nothing of the watch—I never saw it"—he then said "Charles Firmin said, Parker has got a good watch in his pocket; take it, and I will say nothing about it"—I said I knew Firmin perfectly well, and could not believe any thing of that—I met Hill near Strat ford the same afternoon—I handcuffed them together, and said to Hill, "I apprehend you on suspicion of stealing this watch of Parker's also"—Sims said "We know nothing of the watch; Firmin persuaded us to take it, and we would not"—Hill said "He did persuade us to take the watch, but we were hard-working men, and we would not take it"—I locked them up in the cage, and next morning Sarah Sims, the prisoner's mother, came to the door of the cage—she had the watch wrapped up in a handkerchief, and gave it to me, after saying something—the prosecutor has identified it—I have frequently seen the prisoners together before.
