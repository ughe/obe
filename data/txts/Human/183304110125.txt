   GEORGE COLEBATCH. I am carpenter to the Corporation of London. I saw the things which were brought back from the van fitted to the premises - I saw a cistern lined with lead fitted, it fitted the place exactly - I met Mr. Montague on the 23rd of March there; he told me there were some things at the Green-yard, and he wished me to bring them back - I got a cart about one o'clock, brought them back, and fitted them as near as I could - some of them were tenant's fixtures, and some landlords, but that depends on the clauses in the lease; I should say a water-closet was a landlord's fixture - I cannot tell whose the partitions and skirtings were - I saw some mahogany window-sashes which had been cut to take out the glass: I do not know how old it was - I had never been on the premises before.
 COURT. Q. Is not the skirting a ledge of wood put up along the floor, to make the partition flush with the floor? A. Yes, and by removing that and the partition the room would be restored to its former size - they were not the original fabric of the house, they were put up like a screen.
   WILLIAM MONTAGUE, ESQ. The house in question belonged to the City of London - I was there on the 26th of February, in consequence of a statement made to me that there were very considerable dilapidations there - I found the shelves and closets had been removed, a cistern and water-closet taken away, various partitions and some of the doors of the rooms - a mahogany glazed sash had been taken away; the water-closet and cistern were the landlord's fixtures.
 Cross-examined. Q. Was not the front sash cut to make a place for a fishmonger? A. It appeared to he cut for the purpose of taking out the glass with more facility - it might have answered for a fishmonger.
 COURT. Q. Was not the frame and glass removed in one piece? A. No, part of it was evidently broken, part of the broken sash remained on the premises - the frame part was left, but that which holds the panes of glass in was removed; the shutters were up; if they had not been up, the whole would have been open - the manner in which it was done led me to the impression that it was done for the purpose of taking the glass away.
 MR. ALLEY. Q.Was this a part of the house? A. Most unquestionably.
 COURT. Q. That shop front could not have been let with the premises? A.Certainly not.
   JOHN REID. I am a subscribing witness to this lease - The lease covenanted that the tenant should give up all the fixtures in the house when he took it, and all which should be subsequently put up; it expired at Lady Day, 1833.
   GEORGE COLLINGS. I am an attorney. I produce the commission of bankruptcy against the prisoner's brother.
 Prisoner's Defence. My brother purchased the lease of Selig in 1822 - the premises then consisted of a private dwelling-house, and he contracted with a carpenter in Cullum-street, to alter it into three shops - the centre one he occupied himself; he paid the carpenter 300l., and he laid out about 200l. more, and Mr. Selig had expended 1500l. or 2000l. on it; in 1824 my brother became a bankrupt, but he had previously mortgaged the lease for 300l., and the assignees would have nothing to do with it; my sisters were partners with him in his trade, but he was a bankrupt on his own separate account; the trade paid in full, and my sisters purchased of the assignees his one-third interest in these premises - of course I need not say that, when my brother laid out this money, he had no idea that the City would take them down; but when the City took it in their heads to take these premises down, they gave notice (on the 12th or 13th of February) to the tenants to remove; the object of the tenants then was to dispose of the fixtures, and my sisters employed me to take them down, to sell them to the best advantage; I said, "There are King's taxes due, and the King has power to come in, and, as you are going away, the best way would be for  James Adams to put Poole in possession" - but the King's tax-gatherer came in, and distrained; I saw my brother, and said, "It is not worth while to stand out - let them take the things;" he agreed not to act; Poole however remained by desire of my sisters; the fixtures were appraised, and my sisters purchased them, and the whole of the property again became my sisters'; we were proceeding to remove them, when, on the Saturday evening, one of the men came to me and told me to go down - I went, and saw Herdsfield; I said, "What is all this about?" he said, "I act under the orders of the City Comptroller - I have orders to stop every thing coming from the premises, and I shall take possession of the house;" he said, "I understand you have stripped the lead off the roof," and he went up to see, but he was afraid to get out on the roof; he said a great many things had been taken away - I said,"We have taken the things we have paid for;" he said he had nothing to do with it, that he was employed by Mr. Hookey; he then asked the men their names, and said, observe, "I do not insist upon their giving their names and address, but I advise them to do it" - I gave mine, and he put it down; he them said, "It is very well you gave your names, or I should have locked you up;" he then said,"The next thing is, you must walk out," and he pushed us out, and left a man in possession - I went to Mr. Poole and was telling him the facts, and in a few minutes Herdsfield came in; he called for a light, which brought out Mr. Poole, who said, "What is all this about?"
