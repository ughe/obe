EDMUND HULL. I am a commercial clerk—I held four shares in this loan club—I paid 2s. a week—the club met every Saturday at Thorne's coffee room, from 7.30 to 9 p.m., for the purpose of taking the members' money and granting loans—the prisoner's son took the money—he handed it to his father, and then initialled the book—I was present on the sharing-out night on December 19th, when the prisoner said his son had absconded with the money—he produced this sheet of blue paper, and handed his books to me when I asked him for them—they are the day book, ledger, and the I.O.U. book ( Produced ); they are properly kept by the secretary—a check secretary, a trustee, and a committee-man named Rawlins checked the ledger, which was made up from the day book—the treasurer entered the amounts in the day book when he re ceived them—the balance sheet has not taken in the full amount, but it is approximately correct—it does not include the dividend paid out, nor the interest that the members paid—about £33 has to be added, including what was due for fines and that sort of thing—the difference would make the amount about £300 for the 237 shares of the 120 members—I was present when the prisoner made a statement to the committee and was questioned as to his duties, as to his handing the money to his son without authority, and if anyone gave him that authority, and he said, "No "—he was questioned further about the bank book, and he said he had not seen it except once casually at his son's house in August, 1905, when he did not take the trouble to look at it—no order was given as to the money being banked, but the members' impression was that it was in the bank—he acknowledged it was in the bank, but when he was questioned as to what bank, he could not give us the name; he gave us to understand that his son had put it in the bank and kept the bank book—he said that he had borrowed about £75 to make up the deficiency for 1904—5—and that in 1903-4 he had borrowed, to make up a deficiency, but he did not say how much that was—he said he knew that he had gives the money to his son, but he never gave us any satisfactory answers—he would not reply to some questions—we have gathered since that he had borrowed £60—he told us that he had banked part of it in 1904 in the three different names of his son, his son's wife, and that of Mr. Rawlins' at the Post Office in Catherine Road, East Ham—I am not sure, but I think there is proof of that—he was asked, "You knew of this, and still trusted your son with the monies?"—he said he did not know—he was asked how long things had been going wrong, and he said two or three years—we said, "You knew things had not been going straight for two or three years?" and he said, "Yes "—the books were supposed to be audited by a committee—they did not do it—I asked a trustee if he had looked into it, and he said that about six months ago a committee had been appointed, made up of the son, the treasurer, a trustee, and one
