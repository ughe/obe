towards the chaise—he said, "We will go to Wandsworth now and settle it"—at that moment a man, who I have since ascertained is Houghton, the gardener, living on the premises, came into the yard, and asked what was the matter—I told him I had come to take the prisoner, and was searching for the trowsers—he seemed much surprised—I told the prisoner I would not go till I had searched this stable—he was standing at the stable-door with the door just open, ajar—he then allowed me to go into the stable—he had not prevented me, but stood at the door as if he wished me not to go in—I went in with the two lads, Houghton, the prisoner, and his child—when I got in I desired Speed again to stand by the prisoner—I commenced, and searched the first and second stalls of the stable, found nothing, crossed over to search the corn-bin, and while search ing the bin the prisoner said, "Let us go to Wandsworth, and have it settled"—I said, "No, I will not go till I have made a more diligent search"—the prisoner then walked down to the fourth stall himself—there are four stalls all in a row—I saw him move a truss of hay from one side of the stall to the other—I followed him down—there was nobody in that stall—I went to it, and desired him to go and stand by Speed, saying I did not require any of his assistance—I then moved the hay from where he had put it—it was in the direction he had moved it to, but there were several trusses there—I cannot swear it was the same truss—I moved two trusses and some loose hay which was underneath, and discovered something laying, which appeared to me to be a goose—it was a piece of flesh—I said, "What is this? this is a goose" loud enough for every body in the stable to hear—at that moment I heard the stable-door shut, looked round, and the prisoner was gone—I ran to the door, tried to open it, and found it was fastened outside—Speed found a two-pronged hay-fork in the stable, and commenced prizing the door—I assisted him, but could not open it—I went back to be positive as to what I had seen in the stall—the two lads and Houghton followed me—I moved the remainder of the hay off, and thought it was a pig—I found it was the trunk of a female, the body—it was laying on its abdomen with the back uppermost—Speed turned it over—I had no difficulty then in discerning that it was a woman—the arms were cut off at the shoulders, and the legs cut off at the upper part of the thigh; the head severed from the body at the lower part of the neck, and the thigh-bone was taken off right at the hip—it was quite gone—the belly was cut open, and the bowels taken out—I then went to the door, and succeeded in breaking out—the head and all the four limbs were gone—I made a further search an hour and a half after—(I had not left the stable)—I searched in a third stall with another constable, removed several trusses of straw, and under it found a mattress rolled up, and two blankets rolled up in it—I unfolded the mattress, and discovered a
