I went there on Saturday night; on Sunday my master gave me a caution that I must not let in any body without acquainting my mistress or my young master; I told him I would not; they said my young master had been very extravagant, and run into debt at a great many places; and that the bailiffs were after him. On the Monday they sent me out with two bad shillings; they gave me more money with them to help put them off; they said they took them, and must put them off; I was to buy some bread for their dogs, they kept some pointers, and some lard for pancakes, which makes me remember that it was Shrove-Tuesday; I passed the two bad shillings, and brought my mistress the change; on Tuesday they sent me out with another; I thought it something odd having another the next day, though I did not think any thing of their making them. I fetched the prisoner in a ladle one night and some mould; two or three days after this I went to warm my young master's bed; I went down stairs, and went into the parlour for some beer; and I saw a little pair of moulds lying on the table, about four inches square, with two quarter guineas upon them; and there was the ladle I fetched him in, and a little box of quarter guineas on the table; I went up, and staid a considerable while upon the stairs to watch; when he came in again, as he was then gone to the vault; at last, going down stairs, I hit against the wainscot; he ran out with a hanger, and said to me, it is well you was not ran through, D - n you, what do you do there? When I went up to my mistress, she asked me what had happened; I told her: she said, Did you go into the parlour? I told her no.
 Q. Had he a hanger in his hand?
 Clark. Yes; it generally hangs in the day time in the kitchen.
 Q. Did you see him yourself in the parlour?
 Clark. Yes, the mould was all marked; I saw him put the mould together, and pour the metal in; then he broke the mould in two; I saw this while I was sitting upon the stairs, waiting to go down with the chamber-pot; the parlour door was wide open; and the hanger lay across the table; after I went, the prisoner came up and opened my chamber door to see whether I was in bed.
 Q. Did you see him coin a quarter of a guinea?
 Clark. I saw him pour the metal into a a mould, which I suppose was the same as I had seen; and there was some quarter guineas in a little box upon the table.
 Q. Was that after you had been in the parlour?
 Clark. Yes.
 Q. What was the mould made of?
 Clark. Round it, I believe, was iron, it was black in the inside.
 Q. What do you apprehend it was made of?
 Clark. I can't tell; I did not take them off the table.
 Q. Was it possible for you to see, as he poured the metal, what colour it was of?
 Clark. No.
 Q. Did you ever see a mould before?
 Clark. Not till I saw young Mr. Byerman's.
 Cross Examination.
 Q. The parlour door was open, and your master was at the vault, you say?
 Clark. Yes.
 Q. And remained so?
 Clark. Yes.
 Council for the Crown. Did you go to any magistrate?
 Clark. When I was turned out, a person took me to Sir  John Fielding's, in order to right myself. I told them the stuff they colour'd the money with was behind the pewter shelf?
 Q. Did you go of your own accord to the justices?
 Clark. Yes: they would not grant a warrant for the bad shillings. I was served with a copy of a writ to make my words good: then I told the whole story.
 Q. How long was that after this warrant?
 Clarke. Two months.
