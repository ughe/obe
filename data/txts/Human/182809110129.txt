 Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS. Q. How many people have been transported out of your house? A. Never one; I defy any one to prove it - I know Timbrell, but he is a false man if he says that ever I had a person taken out of that house, since I have been in it; I had no lead stolen from me, nor taken from me - I do not know  Fredrick Moody: I have six children - I had three sons, but they are not in this country; I have nothing to do with my son's transactions - I have not had a son at home for eight years; I am bound to come to tell the truth - four persons were transported out of a house which I kept; two of my sons are at Sydney, but they are gentlemen, they never did so well - I do not know the name of the person who came into my house to have the blood washed off; his name is George, that is all I know; he had been living in my house about a month - I do not know that he fought eight or ten rounds in the street; I do not think he fought at all, I saw none - I do not know any man named Harris, but the constable; I was stupified with the blows - I felt great effects from them, and I do now; I never told any body I was in a state of insensibility for ten minutes, and that anybody in the street might have taken my handkerchief; I never said so to Harris - I do not know Charles Treble, nor William Wallers; the reason I went in the street was, when I saw the coat and hat in the passage, I went to see what the event of that was: it is not in my power to say how many people were about - there were a great number; my handkerchief had a large round ring and a table in it, and one corner of it was painted with red; I do not know that I saw the prisoner searched - I was in the watch-house, but his mother was abusing me at the time, and she very likely got as good as she gave, in your opinion.
   FAITHFUL BRIGHTON. I live in the back premises. I was at the door about eight o'clock in the evening; I saw Mrs. Jones lying on the ground - I saw a person pick her up, and after that the prisoner came up, and merely took the handkerchief off her neck; I knew him before, and can positively say it was him - I did not see who knocked her down.
 Cross-examined. Q. What part of the house do you live in? A. In the back premises; there are some little cottage at the back - there was a great mob; I cannot say that there were not full two hundred people about - there were fifty or sixty round Mrs. Jones when she was lying on the ground; I contrived to get sight of her, but I do not know how - when I came out I went and threw it into the mob; she had not had too much to drink, she is not such a woman - it was a red handkerchief, with flowers and a table in the middle; no one interfered to save the handkerchief - she had been lifted up by some people before she lost it; I do not know the man who was fighting with Stocks - George was not a lodger of Mrs. Jones.
   GEORGE HARRIS. I am a constable. I was sent for and took the prisoner in St. Ann's-lane - I found nothing on him; I was not present when this was going on - Mrs. Jones fetched me from near the watch-house.
 Cross-examined. Q. Had you any conversation with Mrs. Jones? A. Yes, and she told me she was in a state of insensibility for ten minutes or a quarter of an hour; she said any person might have taken it, but she was pretty certain it was the prisoner - I am sure she said that to me.
   ELIZA JONES re-examined. I did not fetch Mr. Harris I am sure - my son-in-law and a man of the name of Adams fetched him, and he asked me if I could point him out the man; I went down to the Three Elms, and Adams took the prisoner and turned him over to Harris - Harris appeared at Queen-square; if I were to die in your presence this moment, my Lord, I never said to Harris that I was in a state of insensibility for ten minutes or a quarter of an hour, and any one might have taken my handkerchiof, but I believed it was the prisoner - I said I had been a wife thirty-eight years, and never had such blows in my life.
 Cross-examined. Q. Had you taken your tea that night? A. Yes, at five o'clock, but I am a person who never takes liquors for months together, when I do take it, it is as a remedy for the spasms in the stomach - I did not take notice whether Harris was sober; I had some conversation with him, to tell him who it was that struck me - if I were to tell the sincere truth of my mind, I do not think he was sober; I did say I did not take notice whether he was sober, but it was because I did not wish to injure the matter - I know I was treated by the prisoner as I have stated, and that is all I have to say; I do not altogether take upon me to say whether Harris was sober or drunk - in my own opinion I do not think he was sober; if I did say I did not take notice, it was because you were bothering of me, or because I did not wish to injure the matter, which way you please to take it.
 MR. PHILLIPS to GEORGE HARRIS. Q. Is there the least pretence for saying you had the slightest appearance of intoxication about you? A. Not the slightest, I was as sober as I am now.
