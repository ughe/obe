   Cross-examined by MR. PRICE . Q.  How long have you been a clerk in the Post Office? A.  About three years and a half—I am very conver sant with the general routine of the Post Office duties—I expect to rise. as we are in the habit of doing, as vacancies occur—when a President is re moved, I come rather near the presidency—Mr. Barnes's situation pro duced him about 500 l . a year, I rather think—he has always been consi dered a very able, intelligent gentleman, and an excellent officer, knowing the duties of the Post Office very well—but not performing them.
   Q.  Do you mean to tell us this gentleman was not active, able offi cer who performed his duties well? A.  Yes, I do—I have been frequently with him in the office—it was his business to superintend my conduct—it does not occur to me not to make mistakes—the duties are not extremely intricate—it requires no skill or experience at all to make up bun dles of letters—it requires to he done with great expedition on busy days—the business ought all to be completed by twelve o'clock—Mr. Barnes has assisted the junior clerks on busy nights—in doing so, he has undoubtedly done more than his own personal duty—it is the usual way to sent the money through the hands of a messenger with the letters to go to the Inland Office—it is the usual and proper way with a president—when Mr. Barnes did it, it would alleviate the duty of the messenger—Mr. Barnes would be entitled to transfer the money he receives for the postage through the tunnel by the hand of a messenger—he was not compelled to carry them to the tunnel himself—if I had been disengaged I would have done it, if he had asked me—we are all together in the office, but not pro miscously—the messengers are on one side, and the clerks on the other—there are fire places in the office, round one of which we(the clerks) gather, and the messengers round the other—the President and clerks mix round the fire—the messengers are in the habit of stirring our fire—the messen gers are not allowed to come and sit with us—they come when we send for them—I do not know whether the President gives security to the Crown for the situation he holds—I am in the habit of attending the window, but I am not called the window clerk—it I a term used among us—there are seven of us altogether—Mr. Barnes may have sent the money to the tun nel by any of the inferior clerks—probably if he had asked a President he would not have objected; hut there being only one President on duty, he could not ask him—there are two Presidents, and a superintending Presi dent in the office—there are sixteen clerks.
