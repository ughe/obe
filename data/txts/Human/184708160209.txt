   Cross-examined. Q.  Is Berkeley-place in your beat? A.  No; it is in the same division—I know nothing about the house where this happened—I found the articles I have produced in the house of Susan Brooks. SUSAN BROOKS. I am the prisoner's sister—I know these articles that have been produced—the prisoner brought them to me last Monday fortnight, in the state they are now in—I had this necklace, a smelling-bottle, and a pincushion, all of the prisoner at the same time—I had no others.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Were you taken into custody on this charge? A.  Yes; I was remanded first, and then released by the Magistrate—my brother was also taken.
   COURT. Q.  Do you live with your mother and father? A.  We have a mother but no father—I do not live with her—I was living at my situation—the things were found where I was living as servant—they were all de livered to me at one time, on the Monday.
   MR. METCALFE. Q.  Where was the prisoner living before this? A.  At Cumberland-market, I think—I do not know whether she was there long—she bore a very good character—I never heard anything against her till this—she is nineteen years old, I believe.
   COURT. Q.  Were these things given to you as a present? A.  Yes; she gave me a parcel at the same time, to deliver to my brother—I did not open it. EDWARD BROOKS. I live at No. 8, Walmer-street, New-road. The last witness and the prisoner are my sisters—the last witness gave me a parcel to give to my sister, as she was going home to see her mother and brothers on the Saturday morning—I gave West the parcel—I did not know what it contained till he brought it to the station; it was in a torn piece of rag. BENJAMIN WEST( policeman. ) These things were delivered to me by Edward Brooks, out of a box in the back room of his lodging—they were tied up in an apron; when we untied them, the prosecutrix owned them—I did not bring the apron away with me, therefore the parcel is not in the state in which I found it; the outside cover is off.
   MR. METCALF to EDWARD BROOKS. Q.  Do you know anything of the pro secutrix's house? A.  Yes; I believe it is a common brothel—I went there, on the 21st of July, to see my sister, and when the door was opened a lady came down stairs tipsy, and my sister told me that it was her mistress, and that was is general the case, as she never knew her to go to bed till three or four o'clock in the morning—I should know that lady—I believe it was the pro secutrix.
   COURT. Q.  What time was it? A.  Between seven and eight in the even ing—my sister told me it was her mistress—I saw her go across the passage into the room, and she could hardly walk—that is not the only reason I have for thinking it is a brothel—my sister told me when it was time to go to bed, that her mistress would wish her to go to bed on the sofa, and in the presence of a lot of men—she had complained to me before, and told me she must give her mistress warning; and that her mistress said if she left she would give her no money, and threatened to throw her out into the street—that was what my sister said—before she went to this house she had been living eighteen months in the country—I believe she has always borne a good character up to this present time.
