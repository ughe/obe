Q. Do you know the prisoner at the bar? - A. Yes; he lived with me about four years, up to the time he was taken; I have trusted him with several hundred pounds; I might say two thousand pounds, to go to the banker's; and I have left him in my house, with five thousand pounds property, and never missed a shilling.
 Q. Is your opinion such, of him, that if he were discharged at this moment, you would take him again? - A. I would.
 Q. As to his general character for honesty, have you any doubt of it? - A. I never heard any thing against him till this charge; or I would not have trusted him in my house.
 Court. Q. What was he with you as clerk? - A. Not particularly as clerk; he used to go of messages, and errands.
 Q. What age is he? - A. I take him to be about forty years.
 Q. What salary had he? - A. He had no particular salary whatever; he used to go with me wherever I went almost.
 Q. By what means did his subsistence arise? - A. When he wanted half-a-guinea I let him have it.
 Q. Had he no demand upon you for his work and labour? - A. Never; I used to clothe him.
 Q. Did he live in the house with you? - A. He frequently slept in the house; if I went any where he dined with me.
 Q. He was under no regular establishment with you? - A. No.
 Q. And his living with you was accidentally what you close to give him in return for such service as he did you? - A. Exactly so.
 Q. Had he been bred up in the office of an attorney? - A. No; the way I first became acquainted with him was, I tried a cause for him, between him and the sheriffs of London, in which I succeeded, and recovered 300l. and odd for him; he was a china-man.
 Q. When he came into your service, there was an end of his china trade? - A. Yes.
 Q. How came you to think of taking this man, in any shape, into your service? - A. I took him out of charity; for all that he had to receive out of that business, he had to pay again directly.
 Q. Has he lived with you ever since? - A. Yes: I have trusted him to take money to the banker's, and he never robbed me of a shilling; I never heard any thing bad of him in my life till the present time.
 Q. Is he a single man? - A. Yes; he used to sleep in my dining-room.
   JOHN CLEGHORN sworn.
 I have declined business some years; I was a wholesale clothesman; I live in the Minories: I have known the prisoner, I should suppose, near twenty years; I never heard any thing disrespectful of him; I never heard of his being guilty of any thing like a felony; I never heard his honesty impeached before. GUILTY.
 (The Prisoner was recommended to mercy, by the Jury and Prosecutor, on account of his good character).
 Tried by the London Jury, before Mr. Baron THOMPSON.
 Court. (To Dickson). Q. Are you now willing to receive him again into your service? - A. I am.
 Court. (To the Prisoner). You have stood in great need of that character; that has induced the prosecutor, and the Gentlemen of the Jury, to recommend you to mercy, upon this occasion; had it not been for that recommendation, and that character, which, I believe has come from a description of men, very different from those, whom unfortunately we see daily and hourly in this place, your punishment would have been very different; but there is no doubt, that the persons who have appeared in your favour are persons of character, and their evidence having impressed the mind of the Jury, has had its due weight. The Court have taken into consideration the circumstances of your case, and finding the readiness of Mr. Dixon to receive you again into his service, and to entertain you as he has been accustomed to do, are desirous that you should return to that employment, bearing in mind the lesson that you have had to day-that, till the moment before this offence, your character has not received any impeachment, you will be careful during the remainder of your life, and use double diligence that, you may keep it in future unspotted, remembering the narrow escape you have had; the sentence of this Court in consequence of that good character, is, that you be fined One Shilling, and discharged.
