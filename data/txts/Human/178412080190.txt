 Mr. Garrow. I fancy, my Lord, we have now got far enough for me to trouble your Lordship with an objection.
 Court. Go on.
 Mr. Garrow. My Lord, I take it to be a principle, and it is the practice of the law of England, that all points may be used in order to save the lives of prisoners; but I am perfectly convinced, that by your Lordship and by the Jury, many of whom are known to me to be men of good sense, and great humanity, no point will be pressed against the lives of the prisoners, I am perfectly convinced, that if this case depends on the question, whether half an inch or a quarter of an inch of the point of an instrument, has penetrated through the door, the lives of the prisoners are perfectly secure; but, I am to address myself to the question of law, and I will endeavour to confine myself very strictly to that question: I take the liberty of stating with great submission, that according to the ancient common law notion of burglary, it was indispensibly necessary that there should be a breaking, accompanied by an actual entry, that I take to be the ancient common law of England; the reason that burglary is considered as an offence highly criminal is, that it is an invasion of the security of the subject in his castle; I am aware that there have been some determinations, in which that ancient principle has certainly been considered with a degree of latitude in the construction, that is not always to be found in the determinations of the Judges, in questions of life; I am aware of those decisions which hold, that where a pane of glass is broken, and an instrument introduced noctanter, that that is a burglary; but, I may say without offence, that there are very great authorities, who wish it was a new question, and had not been so held; but, I take it the principle of all these new determinations is this, there is such a breaking of the castle of the proprietor, that his property may be taken out through the aperture; and I take the principle of those determinations, which speaks of an instrument introduced, to mean not the instrument by which the breaking is made, but an instrument by which the property of the proprietor is removed, introduced subsequent to the act of breaking, and after it is complete; here the introduction of the instrument is part of the act of breaking, and no man living can say, that this centre bit was introduced for the purpose of taking out the property of the proprietor, no, it was for the purpose of breaking into the domicile of the proprietor; and I admit that, as to that branch of the crime it is complete, but the Court will not say, that the introduction of the instrument for breaking, was for the purpose of taking out the property; if there had been the least property of Mr. Brown near this hole, that the hand or a hook could have removed if introduced, subsequent to the breaking, I do admit I could have had nothing to say on the subject; as to all the cases that have been mentioned of the introduction of a hand, a foot over the threshold, or a pistol, they all seem to proceed on the idea, that the breaking is precedently complete, and this is considered as an act of dominion over the property of the owner, acquired by the breaking; as where a pistol is introduced, by which terror is applied to the true man to compel him to part with his goods; but there is no case which has said, that the introduction of an instrument in the act of breaking, shall satisfy both the distinct requisites of the offence, namely breaking and entry to steal;
