Captain Miller - Mitchell left my house at two o'clock one Sunday afternoon, to go to France, and left a paper, signed"Captain Hall;" I never spoke to the prisoner about it - the prisoner always answered to the name of Miller; I am certain of the prisoner's person - he had not mustachios when he visited at my house; he used to wear a blue frock coat - he (the prisoner)had a quantity of boxes at my house; I saw him open one case - it contained horse harness; there was a hamper of medicine - he took the medicine out of it, and put it into a wooden box; I heard him say he had a phaeton, which he wanted to place at the George, half-way-house, Commercial-road - the week before Mitchell left my house, a van of property was brought there, and a backney-coach came on the Saturday-night; the prisoner told me he was going to France, in the steam packet, with Mitchell and his wife, who he called Mrs. Miller - Mitchell left my house in a backney-coach, the very same morning as the prisoner left; Mitchell used to carry pistols in his pocket - I never saw the prisoner with any thing of the kind.
 COURT. Q. Can you fix the date of his leaving on Sunday morning? A. I am sure it was the latter end of November, 1829.
 Cross-examined by MR. HEATON. Q. You cannot recollect whether it was on the 22nd? A. I cannot - the prisoner did not bring the van of goods.
 COURT. Q. Did the box he opened come in the van? A. Yes; he told me it was medicine - there were bottles and jars.
   THOMAS MAGNUS CATLIN. I am a solicitor - Mr. Dodson was a client of mine. In October, 1829, in consequence of an advertisement, I saw the prisoner, and entered into a negociation with him for the purchase of his business as a surgeon and apothecary, in Charlotte-street, Portland-place; the purchase was completed - Mr. Dodson was a young man; the prisoner was to remain in the house for three weeks after the purchase - I believe that part of the arrangement was inserted in the written agreement, which I have not got; I saw him at the house on the Monday after the treaty was completed, which was on Saturday - (I have ceased to be Mr. Dodson's solicitor, and have given up all the papers;) this was on the 16th of November - the prisoner's name remained on the door for three or four weeks after that; it was taken down some time after the docket was struck - I only saw him in the house two days after the treaty was completed; I was present at a conversation between Dodson and the prisoner that day; Mr. Dodson asked him when he would pay him the 118l. which he owed him - he said he had got nothing but bankers' bills, but that he was going into the City the next morning, and would either pay Mr. Dodson, or call at my house and leave it with me - at this time the prisoner wore more the habit of a quaker than any thing else; he was not like a captain - I never saw him after that Monday; I struck a docket against him on the 24th of December - we endeavoured to arrest him first, but could not; immediately we found he had gone abroad we struck the docket - that was our reason for striking it; I am not aware that the debt of 118l. had any reference to the negociation about the business; he had told me on the 3rd of November that the debt he owed Dodson should be paid on the completion of the business - the amount was then mentioned as 118l.; it had then been agreed that Dodson should have the business, for which 700l. in all was to be paid - Dodson's mother was to pay for the business.
 Cross-examined by MR. HEATON. Q. The debt the prisoner acknowledged he owed Dodson was 118l.? A. I believe it was - I understood so from Dodson and from the bankrupt; I am not aware that Dodson and the prisoner were acquainted previous to my answering the advertisement - I think I first saw them together in September - I think it was after I had written to the prisoner respecting this purchase; Dodson was not above twenty-two or twenty-three years old.
 Q. How much money was to be given for the business? A. I do not know - several arrangements were made, and alterations; Holtom first asked 400l. for the business and lease, but an alteration was made and a valuation, and the sum actually paid was 700l., including fixtures, wine, and furniture - I cannot tell the particular items; they were all classed together - I believe I wrote Mrs. Dodson one or two letters; I know Mr. Dodson was living respectably, and believe he was able to lend 118l. - I am attesting witness to the agreement; it was not stamped - (Looking at four letters) these are my handwriting; I was present when Mr. Camphell, a friend of Mrs. Dodson's, paid the 700l., on behalf of Messrs. Postlethwaite, her bankers, on Saturday, the 14th of November.
 Q. Now, do not you know that the difference between the first agreement and the second was just 118l? A. Not that I am aware of; I never knew it.
 Q. Do not you know that the second agreement was a fraudulent one, made for the purpose of deceiving Mrs. Dodson, to get the 118l.; and that it was 118l. more than the real purchase-money? A. I do not; I never knew it from Dodson or any body; there was a different arrangement made, and I was informed by both parties, that the sum to be inserted in the deed was 700l.; and, to the best of my knowledge, that was the bona fide sum for the purchase; Mr. Dodson went up to take possession of the business on Saturday, the 14th: the prisoner was to introduce him to his patients for three weeks at least.
 MR. PHILLIPS. Q. Was a verbal arrangement entered into as to the time Holtom was to remain on the premises after the valution? A. Certainly; he agreed to reside on the premises for three weeks from the 14th of November Mr. Dodson was merely to lodge with him during that time.
 MR. ADOLPHUS. Q. When the 700l. was paid to the prisoner, was any demand made on him for the 118l.? A. No; we were at Mr. Campbell's house in Friday-street, Cheapside; Mr. Campbell handed the money to Holtom, in Dodson's presence and in mine - I went to my office; I believe Dodson and the prisoner went to Charlotte-street together - the money was paid in bankers' acceptances, and on Monday the prisoner said he was going into the City next morning, and would leave the amount with me or pay Dodson.
   EDWARD HARRISON LINCARD. I am a surgeon. About three weeks or a month before the prisoner's final settlement with Mr. Dodson, he told me he was in difficulties, and was about selling his business, and wished to gain time with his creditors till he had sold it.
