 NOT GUILTY.
 1511.  JAMES ASKEW was indicted for stealing, on the 11th of September , five rings, value 80 l.; two pairs of ear-rings, value, 25 l.; part of a comb, set with diamonds, value 50 l.; a diamond cross, value 25 l.; a gold chain, value 10 l.; a gold buckle, value 4 l.; an opera-glass, value 2 l.; three silver spoons, value 3 l.; three silver forks, value 3 l.; thirty-nine pairs of stockings, value 3 l.; twenty-nine shifts, value 7 l.; a shawl, value 30 l.; a veil, value 5 l.; a lace handkerchief, value 3 l.; a dress, value 1 l.; and nineteen towels, value 10 s., the property of Elizabeth Virginie Perrot : and seven promisory notes for payment of 1000 francs each, in French money, value 41 l. 13 s. 4 d. each; and five other promissory notes for payment of 500 francs, value 20 l. 16 s. 8 d., the property of Victor Bertrand.
 MR. PHILLIPS conducted the prosecution.
 ELIZABETH VIRGINIA PERROT. I became acquainted him, the prisoner, in Paris a year ago next month; I was first  milliner in his house, and kept his house. My salary was 2000 francs per year; I was to have board and lodging, and washing. I continued seven months and seven days at that salary; it was then increased to double; soon after that I saw a gentleman of the name of Victor Bertrand, he came on a visit by the invitation of Mr. Askew; I am quite certain Mr. Askew invited him. Those letters are Mr. Askew's writing (looking at them) I know his writing very well; indeed when Mr. Bertrand came, in the latter end of April, or beginning of May, 1824, he placed some money, and some French Billet Banques of the Royal Bank in my hands. I went to Cheltenham on the 4th of July; the prisoner and I were then on very good terms. I did not take all my property to Cheltenham; I left the bills in my chest of drawers in the bedroom; they were in a piece of whitey-brown paper, together with this letter, which is from a nobleman. I know a gentleman of the name of Hubert, but I never told him the signature of this letter before he saw it. I also left all the property stated in the indictment. (enumerating them.) Mr. Bertrand had put the promissory notes into my possession, to take care of. All the jewellery was given to me, but two rings, which I bought. I went to Cheltenham on the 4th of July, and received all these letters from Mr. Askew. I remained on good terms with him until about the beginning of August, when a disagreement began, of which jealousy was the cause. I received a gold watch from Mr. Askew, as a present, a fortnight before he had me arrested; he arrested me on Saturday, the 14th of August, for the money I had received for him at Cheltenham, which amounted to 55 l. he at that time owed me about 70 l. I gave the 55 l. and 10 l. to gentleman who arrested me. Mr. Askew came down to Cheltenham, and caused me to be arrested himself. He turned me out of the house on Saturday evening; he signed a paper before I went out of the house; this is the paper (looking at it.) It was signed at the desire of my attorney at Cheltenham. (read)
 "I have received all the bonnets, goods, &c. of Madammoiselle Perrot, delivered to her by me. Dated the 14th of  August.  J Askew." At the time he signed this paper, he owed me 70 l., which he has never paid me. I returned to London on Monday, and made application for my property to Mr. Askew, with a witness - (Mr. Hutchings,) but I never got it. When I went to Cheltenham, I left my drawers locked; I left an inventory of some things, but not all: this is it - it was written by Mr. Bertrand before I left Paris. In consequence of this refusal to give me my property, I sent Mr. Hubert for my property; Mr. Askew said,
 "I have no objection to deliver to you your clothes, which are in the drawers, but not the bedstead and chest of drawers;" he said my clothes, were a few stockings and a few shifts, which I should have, but the furniture was not mine. I went a second time with Mr. Hubert, about three weeks after. Mr. Askew then said he would not deliver anything without his attorney; he did not deliver anything then to me. About a week after that, part of my property was brought by Mr. Hubert - a pair of diamond ear-rings, some stockings, and a few dresses, but no shifts. I had no other jewellery; the earrings were in the inventory; they are worth about 5 or 6 l. I was not present at the opening of my drawers. Before Mr. Hubert went to Mr. Askew's, I described to him something that was in my drawers; I told him there was a letter from a nobleman; I did not mention the inventory, I did not know that it was there; I received these ear-rings on the Saturday, about six o'clock in the evening, and made the complaint on Monday. I went to the magistrate with Bertrand; the magistrate decided the cause at the second sitting; my attorney was there and counsel, and Mr. Askew had advice likewise. When Mr. Hubert brought this property, I did not tell him that was all that I had left; I was very much hurt; I have never got my property; I am sure I left the bank-bills wrapt up in a paper with this nobleman's letter; I gave Mr.  Hubert  an inventory of my property as well as I could make it out; part of it is written by myself, and part by Bertrand; this is it (looking at it.)
 Cross-examined by MR. ADOLPHUS. Who is the writer of this letter - A It is a Nobleman, and I should be sorry his name should be stated; I will not tell his name; it is the first General in the army of the Emperor of Russia,  Nicolas Switzer. He desired me to direct to him as His Excellency. I never heard him called by any other title but Excellence or General. I did not bring all these things from Paris: Bertrand brought some things. The clothes were not all included in the inventory which Bertrand made out in Paris; there are some things in Paris still. Bertrand brought me 29 shifts from Paris, a black silk dress, and some jewellery; the comb, the cross, and the small chain, the opera glass, the three spoons and three forks, another gold chain which I now have, and some other things which I cannot recollect: that is not all the linen and clothes he brought over; he brought over a lace veil. I never made an inventory of the things he brought over. I lived at No. 4, Rue de Grenelle, at Paris, when the prisoner came and engaged me. This letter is Mr. Bertrand's hand-writing (looking at it;), the direction is his writing. I think it was in my drawer, but I have received a great many letters from Bertrand, and cannot be positive, as I have not seen the inside; it is the writing of Bertrand, but addressed to Harriet Duland; she was brought by Mr. Askew as a witness.
