CATHERINE DAVIES. I am the prisoner's wife, and live at 55, Gravel Lane, Southwark—we keep a general shop—on the 13th December, a little after twelve at night, my husband was putting up the shutters—there were some boxes in the way—he asked me to remove them, which I refused to do—he did nothing to me; it was my fault entirely—he moved the boxes himself, and came to me in a passion, and I flew at him and struck him in the face with my fist—he did not strike me back—we were scuffling together, and both of us fell by the door leading to the shop, and I felt that I was stabbed, but how I can't say—I did not see any knife—I said I was stabbed—he said I was not, for he never touched the knife—we were by the door between the shop and the parlour, at the top of the steps—there was a large knife kept on a slab just by the door for cutting the bacon—I was wounded in the stomach, on the left side—the knife penetrated all my clothes; I was dressed as usual—I suppose a doctor was sent for; I hardly remember anything after that—the doctor attended me at home, I dare say over two months, but not so frequently lately—I was told that I was likely to die, and I made a statement, as well as I could, in the presence of my husband and the inspector.
   Cross-examined.  We had been married three years, and lived very comfortably together—my husband has never struck me; he was a good husband and good to my child—he had been out on this night and might have had a little drink—I was in a great passion and hardly knew what I did; I believe I did say "You have been out whore mongering again"—in the scuffle our clothes might have touched the slab on which the knife was, it projects a little over the steps—in the scuffle I tore the lappel of his coat and scratched his face—I might have scratched his wrist with my nails.
   By the COURT . He was not in the habit of drinking—we had been the best of friends during the morning; there had been nothing to put me out, only I thought he was rather bad tempered, and I thought I would be the same—I don't know why I used that offensive expression to him—I was in such a dreadful rage and bad temper I hardly knew what I said. HANNAH BEVINS. I am unmarried, and now live at 85, Chatham Street, Walworth; I formerly lived at 55, Gravel Lane—on the night of the 13th December about 12.20 I saw the prisoner putting up the shutters—I went away and returned in about 10 minutes; I then found the shop closed—I heard Mrs. Davies scream "help and murder" and heard some one fall—I unlocked the door and went in, and saw her lying on the ground in the passage near the parlour door; the prisoner was leaning over her—she said "I am stabbed"—I asked him if he had done it; he said "No"—she was lifted into the parlour into a chair by Mr. Fotherby—I didn't see any knife about; there were no supper things on the table—they occupied the shop and two parlours at the back.
