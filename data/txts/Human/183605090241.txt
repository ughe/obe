Merton—that is about four hundred yards from my hut—I was at the White Hart at Merton on Saturday evening the 10th, with my wife; a man named Claridge was there, and almost all the men who work on the iron rail-road, because that is where we receive out money—I received my money about eight o'clock—I saw Mrs. Haggerstone come in between ten and eleven o'clock as near as I can tell—Claridge was there when she came in—the prisoner was not there—she staid there till twenty minutes to twelve o'clock—I heard Claridge ask her to drink with him, and saw her take the pot off the table and put it to her mouth—I cannot say whether she left the house alone, for we all went out of the bar—she came and asked me for a loaf, saying she had no bread for her children, and all the shops were shut up, and I let her have a loaf from my bag—I missed both Claridge and her from the house while I was at the bar—I went out about five minutes after (I dare say), because I stopped to have a glass of rum at the bar—I did not see Mrs. Haggerstone afterwards—I and my wife went to Saunders's beer-shop to pay sixpence which I owed, and while we were in there the clock struck twelve—we left the beer-shop a about half a mile from our hut, down the fields—we went directly home from the beer-shop—when we got home we found the prisoner there lying down on the bed, with his clothes on—his wife was not there—nothing passed between me and him then about his wife—my wife and I went to bed—some time after we had been in bed, I heard voices outside, it was John Green and William Steel—I do not know the time, as I had been asleep—the persons outside said Mrs. Haggerstone was sick—they gave an alarm—they stopped a little while, and said, Mrs. Haggerstone was drowned—they had not left the spot—they remained to see if Haggerstone would go with them—I wanted him to get up and go—I said, "If it was my case, I should go and see about it"—he began crying, and said he could not leave his children and his baby—he had four children, and one of them is mine or ten months old—he did not get up then, and the persons went away—I got up a little after five o'clock on Sunday morning—Haggerstone was still there—he was getting up then to go with Serle and Hart, the constable and overseer—I do not recollect asking him any questions.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  You have spoken of expressions be used; I suppose, at times, men in your class of life quarrel with their wives? A.  Sometimes—we do not always live without quarrelling—I cannot tell whether he had been drinking that night or not—I had been drinking a little, but I was sensible that I was going home, and, when I got home, I heard the clock strike—the children were in bed with the prisoner when I got home—Mrs. Haggerstone did not tell me, when she asked me for the loaf, that her husband had just given her 8 s . 8 d ., which ha had received—she had money, for she paid me for the loaf—I did not see what money she had—she had more than what she paid me—the prisoner knew Hart the constable, and Serle the overseer, very well—Claridge is no acquaintance of mine, only he worked on the road with Haggerstone and me—he had been at my house—he came there on Sunday morning, but never came inside the hut—Steel and Green first said Mrs. Haggerstone was sick, and then that she was drowned—they called out to the prisoner and told him so—I cannot say justly at what time I got home—it might be a little after one o'clock
