Prisoner's Defence.  As I was coming along a lad named Mike Coleman called me—be is always living in the prosecutor's house—he asked me to carry that frame for him—I said yes, and coming into George-street he went another way—he was at the house the same night before the girls went to bed—he was always there along with them, and he had a key of the door—he got in and took it—he told me so—that he had a key of the back-door and the room-door, and there were no bolts on the door at all. NATHAN SMITH re-examined.  I know the lad be names—I never saw him at that house—I had lived there about three months—I have no doubt that lad was along with the prisoner—I had met him one day about three weeks previous to the robbery, and have not seen him since—I had employed him when I kept a broker's shop in Bermondsey-street, but heard he was a bad character and parted with him—I know nothing of the prisoner. GUILTY. Aged 19.— Confined One Year.

 1705. JAMES CLARKE and RICHARD JACKSON were indicted for stealing, on the   16th of May , 1 purse, value 1 d  .;2 sovereigns, 4 half-crowns, 10 shillings, and 4 1/2 pence in copper, the goods and monies of Sarah Emery , from her person.
   MR. PAYNE conducted the prosecution. SARAH EMERY. I live in Wellington-street, Newington-causeway. On Wednesday last I went to Mitcham to see a friend—it was the day of Dorking-races—I left Mitcham between eleven and twelve o'clock at night, and was the worse for liquor—I went into an inn at Mitcham—I had three sovereigns in my purse when I went into the house—I had received them from my friend—I could not find a coach at the inn—I waited there about a quarter of an hour, and had a glass of brandy-and-water—I changed a sovereign to pay for it, and received 19 s . in change, which consisted of half-crowns and shillings—I put them into the other end of my purse—I also had one shilling in my pocket, and 4 1/2 d . in copper—not finding a coach, I walked on towards Tooting, and between Mitcham and Tooting I heard a glass coach come by—I then took my purse out of my pocket and put it into my stocking—when the coach came up I asked the man if he could convey me to London—he said yes—it was the prisoner Clarke—he got down and let me inside the coach—there was somebody rather shorter than himself on the box, who I cannot recollect—I found a little girl and boy inside—the little girl was very anxious indeed to get home—after getting a little way the horse would not go on—we got out and walked a little way, as Clarke asked me—I walked a very little way, being very ill and very sick, I was obliged to get in again, and the children got in again soon alter—soon after a man came and took the children away—they said they must have some other conveyance to get them home—I do not know where this was—they left me in the coach, and I went to sleep—I was awoke by Clarke asking me if I was cold—I said, yes, I was very cold—he covered me over with a great coat—I do not think he stopped long inside, but I slept so soundly I do not know—I went to sleep again directly—I cannot say when
