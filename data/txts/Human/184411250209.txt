I am a waterman—I was on board the Waterman  steamer on the 25th of Oct., and was at the wheel—after we pasted the schooner, which was aground alongside Lower Tunnel-pier, the captain gave me orders to put the helm a-starboard—I was in the act of doing so at the time we passed the schooner—it was before we passed the schooner that he gave me the order—I knocked the helm hard a-starboard with the stool.
   COURT . Q.  Was that in order to pass the schooner? A.  Yes—it was the usual order at that place, in order to pass the pier-head to go in to the stairs.
   SIR J. BAYLEY . Q.  In your way up from Woolwich had you called at Greenwich? A.  Yes, we kept the Surrey shore part of the way up from Greenwich, till we bad directions to go to Limehouse-pier at Limehouse-hole—we came out from Limehouse, and made a direction as clear at we could come up the river as far as the Tunnel-pier—I remember the colli sion taking place.
   Q.  Did you receive any orders what to do with the helm previous to that? A.  After the weigh of the boat was off, I had orders from the captain, and I put the helm according to which was the right way for it to be put, in order to put her into the Tunnel-pier—after this wherry was stopped I shifted the helm—I did not feel the knock against the wherry—I did not see the collision, and did not feel it exactly—I heard it mentioned that we had run a boat down—no order was given me just before that, further than to put the helm hard a-starboard—that was an order given to get out of the way of the boat, and, as usual, to get into the pier.
   COURT . Q.  Then it so happened that at the time you wanted to get out of the way of the boat, you wanted to get in to the pier? A.  Yes, that is the usual course when we are going to the Upper Tunnel-pier—we go in towards the Lower Tunnel-pier, and put the helm hard a-starboard, and shoot in alongside the Upper Tunnel-pier—after the order was given, I blocked the helm hard a-starboard, and ran to the starboard quarter—I threw a stern rope to the assistance of the persons that were overboard, and I lent a gentleman a hand to get a stool to chuck  overboard, to restore the people's lives. GEORGE THORNTON. I was the engineer on board the Waterman  No. 6, on the 25th of Oct., when this accident happened. I had been on deck previous to arriving at Lower Tunnel-pier—I went below, and in quite two minutes after I got below I received the orders, "Ease her, stop her, turn her astern"—as well as I can judge, we were about the Lower Tunnel-pier at that time—those orders were given rapidly, almost as quick as they could be given—I obeyed those orders. WILLIAM SALVAGE. I am a clerk in Somerset-house. I was a pas senger on board the Waterman  steamer on the 25th of Oct.—I saw a schooner lying athwart the tide, between the two Tannel piers—shortly after, I heard the captain cry out in one breath, "Ease her, stop her, turn her astern"—I got on to the gangway, and saw a skiff, with five or six men in her—she shot out, and came in contact with the fore part of the right hand paddle-box of the steamer—the boat was upset, and the persons all immersed in the water—in my judgment the accident decidedly could not have been avoided.
