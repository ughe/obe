548. THOMAS JOHNSON was indicted for feloniously uttering a forged
acceptance to a bill of exchange for 231. 14s., well-knowing it to be forged,
with intent to defraud James Cope.
MR. PAYNE conducted the Prosecution.
JAMES Cope. I am an engraver, and live in Frith-street, Soho. I have
known the prisoner about a year and a half-be owed me an account in Oct.
last, and on the 14th of Oct., in the afternoon, he came to my house, and pro-
duced this bill of exchange--(looking at it)--he said he had drawn that bill
on Mr. Breese, as the result of his quarter's account with that gentleman, and
if I would give him the difference we would square up, or something-he said
Breese was one of his employers; that he was a coach-plater, living in Hart-
street. Covent-garden: that it was a genuine bona fide trade bill, and would
be met—it had what appeared to be Breese's acceptance on it at the time, and
he was speaking of that-in consequence of what he said, I took the bill-I
knew there was such a person as Breese, and that he was a respectable person
- I gave the prisoner 111. odd-I cannot exactly tell what he owed me, but
I believe I paid him 117. 98.--the bill is for 231. 148.-he owed me the differ-
ence-I think it was 91. 118. I gave him it was that or 11l. 98.; but the
learned gentlemen are surrounding me so that I can hardly tell which; if I had
a fair field I could do.
COURT. Q. You gave him the balance, whatever it was? 4. Yes-I
presented the bill to Breese when it became due-it was not paid.
Cross-examined by Mr. PARRY. Q. I believe you are a bill-discounter ?
4. Not professedly I have occasionally discounted bills-I am not manager
of a loan society, I am employed by one I have discounted several bills for
the prisoner, parporting to be accepted by Breese (the bill was here read,
dated 12th Oct., 1846, at three months, for 231. 148.)
EDWARD BREESE. I live in Hart-street, Covent-garden, and am a coach-
plater. The acceptance to this bill is not my handwriting-I know nothing
of it-I never authorized the prisoner or any person to accept this bill for me.
Cross-examined. Q. It is not at all like your writing, is it? 4. It does
a little resemble it in the name Breese, except the E-I have known the pri-
soner about twenty years, but had not seen him for twelve years, till within
the last three years-he is a master smith-during the three years we bave
done a little business together-I never accepted but two bills for him in my
life-the last was in January, 1846-he came to me, and said, if I would
oblige him by accepting a 191. bill, he could not exactly tell what time the
party would discount it, and asked me to leave the date-this is it, (looking
at it,) and the only one in existence-there never was but one previous to this,
which was one for 141., and the prisoner destroyed that the 191. was an ac-
commodation bill-I did not owe him a shilling—I had paid him my accounts.
Q. Was there not an account between you at that time? A. Nothing but
a few jobs-not 11. altogether-I accepted it entirely to accommodate him, as
he said in three months he could take it up I did not pay that bill-he gave
me a memorandum at the time.
Q. About the first or second week in Oct. were you at the prisoner's shop?
A. I don't think I saw him himself for a week previous to this bill being
dated-he had done a few jobs for me and I for him, but I did not see him
-I think I was at his shop the beginning of Oct.- bad paid the balance
of his account, and had his receipt he wished me to be security to a loan
Society for him, which I declined.-everything was paid he did not at that
time speak to me about the 191. bill-he came to me, and stated that he had
