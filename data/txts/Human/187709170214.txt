Condensed milk is 7 1/2 d . a tin, the best, but you can get it 4 1/2 d .—I should give a tin to one of my children at twice—besides the prisoner and her five children and the baby who is dead, there was a man lodger—there were two children four years old; there were five there, and there was another child who did not come home—sometimes three or four children were taken there to nurse in the day time—Emma Langridge's child was between sis and seven months old—I have seen her pay 8 d . or 9 d . for the" day, and I have seen her give the children a penny and twopence to get some biscuits—she used to pay every night when she fetched the baby, it was only there in the day time—the prisoner's children were nice, healthy-looking, fine children, in fact they are finer children than my own—one of them has gone out two or three times to earn something—I saw the children a very few minutes—except at the time they were at school they always seemed to be very well and well nourished—I should give a baby of that age a pint or a pint and a half of milk a day and a nursery bis cuit, which you get twenty four of for 6 1/2 d .—I give my children one biscuit at night and one in the morning and then they would last a week for the 6 1/2 d .—Ialso give different little things—milk is 3 d . a pint in that neigh bourbood and 4 1/2 d . a day would provide the very best—it would cost 3 s . or 3 s . 6 d . a week to keep the baby—I think 5 s . a week would cover the expense for a baby like that, without luxuries—once whenl I was over at the chandler's—shop 1 saw the baby at the window and I said "Oh, I say, that baby does look bad!"—I never saw brandy and egg given to a baby before—when my children are ill I have given them a drop of brandy in the milk if they have been troubled with wind—the prisoner's rent was 5 s . 6 d . a week—I do not know what the man lodger paid.
