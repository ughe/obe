MR. RYLAND . Q.  Had you any money by you at the time you re ceived the money from Glynn's? A.  Not any—I paid away the whole of that money when we took the Axe, except one 5 l . note—on the 28th of October we let the house, and were paid 170 l . in Bank of England notes—I did not take the numbers and dates of those notes—there was one 20 l ., three 10 l ., ten 5 l ., and 16 l . in cash; therefore I had then eleven 5 l . notes altogether, including the one in question—when I counted the money in the bag on the 29th of October, there was 160 l . in it, consisting of one 20 l ., three 10 l ., eleven 5 l . notes, and about 50 l . in gold; and I had about 5 l . in my pocket—I have lost about 30 l .; a 5 l . note, a 10 l . note, and five sovereigns.
   COURT . Q.  How can you tell that the note in question was not one of those you paid away? A.  This was the only 5 l . note I had in my possession of those I received from Glynn's—I paid all the others away—I am certain this was the note I retained—after I had paid away the other 5 l . notes, I looked at the one remaining, so as to know the number of it—I swear that—I took the number of this note the day before I left the Axe—it was the only note I then had in my possession, and it was in my purse—here is the memorandum I made—the note is dated July 13th, 1841, and No. 64319—I wrote that on the 27th of October, the day before I left the Axe.
   MR. RYLAND . Q.  What was your reason for making that memoran dum, when you had only one 5 l . note left? A.  Because I should know it was my note—my husband took the other notes from Mr. Brown, who took the Axe of us—after we went to the prisoner's mother's house on the 28th of October, we put the money altogether—my husband had not ac cess to the bag—he never saw the money after he gave it to me—I never told any body the number of this 5 l . note, till it was shown to me—I stopped it at the Bank—I went with my husband to do so, on the 18th or 19th, I think—I do not know whether or not my husband took the paper with him—I told them the number and date, and they stopped it—I do not think I produced the paper—I think I stated the number and date from memory—I believe the prisoner's mother takes in washing, and does what she can for her livelihood—I believe it is a respectable house—I do not know—I knew the prisoner a year and a half ago—she used to do needlework for me—she goes out to needlework and dress-making—she made me two dresses at home once, and also came to my house to work; and in September they went to live at this house, and she wrote me a note, saying they had apartments to let, and would be happy to accommodate us—she did no millinery at home, while I lodged there, nor did she go out—she did nothing at all while I was there—her mother took in a little washing, and there were other lodgers in the house—there was a lady, her daughter, and son, in the first floor; a Mrs. Holt, her daughter, and niece, and a little girl.
