   KENT CASES .
   Before Mr. Justice Coltman.
 258. JAMES SLADE was indicted for feloniously uttering a forged acquittance and receipt for 1 l  . 1 s  . 2 d  ., with intent to defraud John Dunkin and another.
   MR. WILKINS conducted the Prosecution. ALFRED JOHN DUNKIN. I live at Dartford, Kent, and am a printer—I was in partnership with my Father—I have known the prisoner from about 1835—he owed us 1 l . 1 s . 2 d . for printing—the money had been applied for at least twice a-year, and I should say four times—Newman, who was in our service, was directed by me, in March last, to call on the prisoner, and in con sequence of the answer he brought me, I wrote copies of two letters to be sent to the prisoner—I applied for a summons to the Court of Requests, which came on for hearing I believe on the 6th of Oct.—my father attended—it was adjourned for my attendance—I attended on the second occasion—the prisoner was not there—I attended a third time—he was then there, and put in a receipt—this is it—the officer of the Court produced it—the prisoner said he had paid the money, and there was the receipt, or something to that effect—I will almost swear those were his words—these words "Paid 24th of Sept 1839. J. DUN  KIN, " are not my writing, nor my father's, or any one of our firm—the Com missioners decided in the prisoner's favour, and I gave him into custody immediately after—the Magistrate accepted bail for the prisoner, and as he came out of the Court, he came up to me, and said, "I an very sorry, but I had had a drop too much aft the time"—I said, "I have nothing whatever to say," and pointing to my solicitor, said, "What you say must he said to my solicitor."
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  When were the goods had for the bill? A.  On the 2nd Oct., 1837—we had, perhaps, six men in our employ, but they are never in the shop—they do not understand it—Mr. Colquhoun was my solicitor—Mr. Lock manages his criminal business—I had directed the summons to be taken out—our books were produced before the Commis sioner, who examined them—I never quarrelled with the prisoner—I under stood he was of a quarrelsome disposition, so I kept away—I believe my father applied to him one day, and he blowed father up very much—this was two or three years before—the prisoner lived at Bexley at the time, and then at Bexley-heath—he is a carpenter—I applied personally to him for the bill about Christmas, 1887—the last time was in 1840—I applied personally between those times—I think I called on him twice at Bexley-heath—I have applied by letter repeatedly—I remember one of the Com missioners saying the debt was barred by the statute of limitations—I argued the statute myself with them—the prisoner did not in my presence say, "I do not wish to take advantage of the statute"—I had intimated my intention to have a policeman ready to take him on the third occasion—it was notorious—I had taken a rule nisi—the officer was in attendance the whole of the 20th of October.
