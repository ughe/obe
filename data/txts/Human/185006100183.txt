MR. COCKLE to MR. WILKS . Q.  Although the prisoner had only dealt with you once before, yet you knew previously that he was in the same business? A.  Yes; I had known him as having been in that business about six months—the amount of the order he gave on 31st Dec, was 59 l . 6 s .—the preceding order was 16 l . odd—I believe he paid that in cash in the counting-house—when he said, "Send the invoice with them, "I said, "I am not the master, the goods must be paid for on delivery"—I had no master there at that time—it was a regulation of my own—I had no authority to sell to strangers, except on cash, without my master being there—I had no discretion—I believe 50 pounds of this was recovered from Mr. Welter—the prisoner did not say that he had been disappointed by a Mr. Edwards, who owed him 53 l . on a bill of exchange—on 1st Jan., I charged the prisoner with having committed a crime, I sent for the policeman, and be refused to take him in charge.
   Q.  Did you not say to the prisoner, in the presence of the policeman, after some altercation about this, "Very well, mind, I will take care yon shall have the handle of leather which we took this morning sent back to you, and then if you don't pay on Saturday you will have a writ?" A.  No; the prisoner lent out for some gin, and made two glasses of gin-and-water, one for him self and one for me, but I never touched it—the policeman drank the glass that was made for me—the goods were sent on Tuesday 1st Jan.—I went on the day after—I did not find the prisoner there—I did not see Mr. Weller there any time during that week—I went about eight o'clock each morning—the prisoner was not taken on this charge; he was in Whitecross-street prison for debt before we could find him. JAMES WILKS re-examined. Q.  Did the prisoner come and pay for the first parcel of goods? A.  He did, in the afternoon—the foreman said when the goods went, that Mr. Tucker was gone to our house to pay for them.
   Witnesses for the Defence. WILLIAM LITTLE( policeman, M  118). On Tuesday afternoon, 1st. Jan., I was on duty near the prisoner's house, and was called in about five o'clock—I found the prisoner, the carman, and the prosecutor's foreman—he wanted to give the prisoner in charge, but be seemed to hesitate, first saying he would, and then he would not, saying he wished to have it settled some other way, he wished to come to an arrangement—the prisoner said lie had been disap pointed, and he should be able to settle the bill on the Saturday following, provided the bundle of leather was returned back which was taken away in the morning—that was agreed to by the prosecutor's foreman, and they shook hands—the foreman replied that he would send the leather back on the morrow morning, and if the whole amount was not paid on the Saturday morning, he should serve him with a writ—the words the foreman said were, "Very good, it shall be done, the leather shall be returned," and if not paid for on Saturday, he should serve him with a writ—after this agreement had been come to, they took some gin-and-water together, and the prosecutor's foreman left the house—they parted in a friendly manner.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  I am afraid you have forgotten part; you have not said that you took any gin-and-water? A.  No, I did not—I swear that I partook of no gin-and-watch—I was sent for to take this man into custody—Mr. Wilks did not complain that the man had got his master's goods, which he agreed to pay for or return them, and he could neither get goods nor money.
   COURT . Q.  Then what reason did he give for sending for you? A.  He wanted to charge him with obtaining goods under false pretences.
