 1221. WILLIAM TUCKER , stealing 1,765lbs. weight of leather, value 60 l  .; the goods of William Boutcher and others.
   MR. CLARKSON conducted the Prosecution. JAMBS WILKS. I am warehouseman to Mr. William Boucher and two others. On Monday, 31st Dec, about three o'clock in the afternoon, the prisoner came to their warehouse at Bermondsey—he asked to see some low-priced butts of leather—I showed him two parcels—he selected some, which amounted to 59 l . odd—he said, "You will send them in to-morrow morning, and send the invoice with them"—I said, "Yes, but I am not the master; I must have the cash for them on delivery of the goods"—he said, "Well, send them before nine o'clock in the morning; I shall be sure to be at home, and I will give the man the money"—I had sold him a small parcel of goods on the Friday before—that was for cash—it was the first time I had sold to him—after he was gone, I had the leather weighed—next morning I saw the leather loaded, and gave the invoice to Saxby—I told him to be sure to bring back the goods or the money—he left the yard with the goods at a little before eight o'clock, and returned a little after nine without the goods—(I suppose the distance from our place was about a mile and a half)—in consequence of what I heard from him, and by the direction of Mr. Vicary, I went with Saxby and the horse and cart, about eleven o'clock the same morning, to the prisoner's house—I knocked at the front-door, and spoke to a female—in consequence of what she said, I went round to the yard-door, knocked, but no one came—I waited about five minutes—I left Saxby there, and I went round again to the house-door—I knocked twice, and the door was opened by a female—I went in and went through the house into the warehouse—I saw the leather there—one of the parcels contained thirteen bales, of five butts each—I took up one of the bales, and opened the back-gate, and gave the bale to Saxby, who put it into the cart—I went back and attempted to get another bale, but the men in the yard locked the yard-gates; locked the warehouse-door, and locked me in and leather and all—I called to Saxby, who was outside, to come round to the house-door—he came into the house, and I came out and left him in charge—I went back to my employer, and communicated what had happened to Mr. Vicary—it was then about half-past eleven o'clock—I went back to the prisoner's house, and was let in at the front-door by the same female—I waited from half-past four till five—I then saw the prisoner—I asked him why he did not give the man the money as he promised—he said he had been disappointed of some money, and he could not—I said, "Well, then, I must have the goods"—he said that I should neither have goods nor money—I then sent Saxby to my employers, and after some more conversation with the prisoner, on the return of Saxby I sent for a policeman—the policeman said he did not know what it was, whether it was false pretences or a debt, and he could not detain the prisoner—at last, when I found I could not get the policeman to take him, I left—the prisoner told me I might stop as long as I liked, he should neither give me goods nor
