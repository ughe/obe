   Q.  Had you not been fighting a stand-up fight with him, and when the policeman came up, he ran away? A.  Yes, he ran away when the police man came—I tried to defend myself as well as I could—I was too drunk to stand—I will not swear I did not hit him—he knocked me down, and ill-used me, and I somehow got my coat off while some women held him—I broke three or four panes of glass with my fist, not with my arms—this was after he had runaway—it was before I knocked my head through the glass that I found myself at home, wounded—there was blood all over me before that—both my arms and head bled very much. MARGARET BUCKLEY. I am fourteen years old, and am the prosecutor's sister. I met him in Russell-street, Bermondsey, between seven and eight o'clock—he was able to walk, but not well—he was very drunk indeed—I helped him home—I met him above a hundred yards from home—he went home quietly down the court—we met the prisoner in the court—he hit my brother, and knocked him down—the prisoner was tucking his shirt collar down when I met him—he struck my brother several times while he had him down, and every time he struck him blood flowed—I saw something glittering in his hand, like a knife—Robert Parker was sitting at the door of his sister's house—several persons came out of that house—Parker's brother came out, and pulled the prisoner from my brother—I saw my brother go into the door of his house, but I did go in.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Do you know of your brother being helped home by a policeman that night? A.  No—he was drunk—his coat was on when I met him, but off when he came out of his own house again—it was on when the prisoner attacked him—he came out after I saw him home—there was no fighting then—the prisoner went into his motber-in-law's house, and when my brother came out, he broke some windows, and said Clements should come out and fight him again—I then saw a policeman, who took my brother home—there are scrapers outside my brother's house—he did not fall on the scraper.
   COURT . Q.  When your brother got home the first time, was he bleed ing? A.  Very much, from both arms and his head. WILLIAM FENSON( policeman. ) I was called to Fawcet's-place on the 1st of August—I saw the prosecutor standing there, surrounded by several per sons, bleeding from the head—I asked how he received that wound—he said he got it in falling with the prisoner, who was there, and walked away on my coming up—I took the prosecutor home—he had no wound on his arm, for I held him by the arm—it was about half-past eight o'clock—he was very drunk, more like a madman—he had no shirt on, nor anything but his trowsers—I went to the top of the court—I was called back again, and saw him fighting through the window at the prisoner, who was inside the house—he broke three panes in the window, and two over the door, and split the pannel of the door, trying to get in—he put his arms through the glass, and broke the door with his fist—I observed he was bleeding from the arms—he went in-doors to his wife—when the prisoner found he was gone in-doors, he came out and went away.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What condition was the prisoner in? A.  Stipped, all but his shirt—the prosecutor's arms went right through the glass. ROBERT PARKER.
