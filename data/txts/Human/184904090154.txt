was a passenger on board the  Sir John Franklin  named Beattie—I have been in the navy, and left thirteen or fourteen years ago, because I thought I could do better—I did not get into any scrape there—I was never flogged—I have never been tried for anything—the last flogging Abrams received was eight or ten days before he died, and between that and the first flogging he had been flogged eighteen or twenty times—that might have been during a period of five weeks—that was before we rounded the Cape—he did not die till after we had passed the Falkland Islands—I swear I never saw the captain present when he was flogged, or heard him give any order for him to be flogged—the captain was very seldom on deck so early as four or six o'clock in the morning—it was generally then that the boy was flogged—they used to say that the boy was dirty, but I never saw it—I never heard the crew complain of it—the crew did not go up to the captain in a body and complain of his dirt, or I should have heard of it—when I saw his body I saw a few sores on his feet—they were scrubbed with sand, at the chief mate's orders, by the boy Herbert Taylor, an apprentice on board—they were small dry sores, just by the ankle, as if the skin had been knocked off, and they were healing—there were four or five of them, and I believe they were on both legs—I did not bear that he had got the venereal disease in the country—I have heard it talked about since the trial came on—I never heard of it on board—he never said anything about it, or said he was ill—if he had had the disease I think he would have come to me, knowing I had been so long in the country, and could judge of it—he never did come to me—there is a disease of that kind out there—Herbert Taylor had to rub his feet with a piece of canvass and sand, because his feet looked rather dirty—the first flogging was about a fort night after we left Auckland, the next might be two days afterwards, and after that there was a flogging pretty nearly every other day—it generally took place between four and six, which was the chief mate's watch. MATTHEW CAREY. I reside at 2, Queen's-court, Albert-street, Deptford. I was a seaman on board the Indian , and recollect Abrams coming on board at Auckland to feed the stock; he was then in a good slate of health to all appearance—there was a seaman, named Williams, on board, he com plained to the prisoner that Abrams had vermin about his neck, and the prisoner ordered his hair to be cut—he made a mess on a sail, and the pri soner said he should not sleep in the lower forecastle any more, and he went down again and made a mess in the coalhole, and the prisoner flogged him for it; that was about three weeks after he came on board, and I believe it was the first time he was flogged—after that he was kept on the forecastle, and we were forbidden to go near him, because he had got vermin—his dress consisted of a cotton shirt and a pair of duck-trowsers—it was not very cold weather then, but we were getting into cold weather—he was kept there when his hair was cut, from about eleven o'clock in the morning till three or four in the afternoon—he was not subject to that treatment every day, only on that particular day—we went below about twelve the next morning and saw a mess on a sail, and he said he could not get the hatch off; the hatch was put over, but it was easy enough for any one to lift it off if he had placed his head against it; I have done it myself easily—he was in the habit of sleep ing in the forecastle till the day his hair was cut; he was then put down in the lower forecastle—he used to sleep in the top-gallant forecastle; there were not enough berths for the hands on board; he never had a berth assigned him with the rest of the seamen—he used to sleep on the chest, and every time the door was opened, he was exposed to the wind and weather—we had some very severe weather when we got off Cape Horn, and it was very cold
