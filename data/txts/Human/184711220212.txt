I am a shoemaker—I lodged in the back room, over the kitchen, in the house where this happened—I was at work by myself, heard the noise of an explosion, and directly my place was filled with sulpher—I got up and opened the door to escape, but not, the smell was to strong—I dot down stairs after a while, and got into the street—I was nothing of the prisoner—I have seen him and his wife at work rolling papers—I have not seen him fill them—I believe they contained something combustible—I asked him a few days before that, as I passed the window, if that was the way made fireworks—I do not think I said "fireworks"—I said, "I never saw anything of the kind before, is that the way you do it?" and he said they did not fill them—I understood they were fireworks, or something of the sort—I do not recollect the exact words that passed—I did not take particular notice of them—his wife said they did not fill them there, they did not it for fireworks—that was in his presence—she said they were filled at their sister's, that they only made the cases—the prisoner made on answer.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Were there not two boys there? A.  Yes—I never saw them letting off fireworks of any kind in the yard.
   MR. RYLAND. Q.  Have you seen the prisoner letting off fireworks in the yard? A.  Yes, a day or so before this happened. THOMAS GRAY. I am a cap-maker, and lived with my family in this house, in the back room first-floor—I, my wife, and children, were at home when the explosion took place—it sounded like the pop of a gun—my win dow was open, and smoke came in—I took the child up in my arms and opened the door, and the moment I opened it the flames and smoke rushed up to my face—I put the child in some one's arms, jumped out into the yard, and they threw the child after me, my wife jumped out, and when I came back I found her lying on her back in the yard—after that I con veyed her through the passage—after I jumped through the window I heard three different reports, the same as the first—the prisoner came into the yard and said, "Oh my God, look at my hands!"—I did not notice whether his hands were burnt—I knew the two boys—I have seen the prisoner's brother Robert at work there, filling the squibs with gunpowder, I believe—the prisoner has been sitting at the window when I have seen Robert mak ing squibs—I did not see Robert after I got out of the house—I saw the other boy, James Vincent, come out at the back door—he seemed to be burnt—the shirt on his right shoulder was burnt—he got over into the next yard—I have seen the prisoner rolling papers for crackers—I spoke to him once on the subject, two or three weeks before the explosion—I said, "Sup pose they were to explode"—he said, there was no danger of that, he always worked them damp—he gave me a cracker to feel, and it was quite damp—on the Tuesday before, I saw the prisoner let off two squibs in the yard, and then Vincent came and let off another—on the Thursday, the day before the explosion, he let off a lot of what I think he called Roman candles—one was much larger than the others, and he exploded a lot of gunpowder on the window-sill, which filled my room with smoke—about a fortnight before that I saw him put a quantity of squibs on the side of the hen-house, in the yard—I did not see what he did with them—on the Tuesday before the accident I told him he ought to be ashamed of himself, he was worse than a child—my wife is not here—she was burnt a good deal, and has been confined to her bed a fortnight.
