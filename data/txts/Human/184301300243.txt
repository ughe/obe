COURT . Q.  What are the provisions of the deed? Do they follow the blank deed, and only alter as to the sums? A.  It purports to be a convey ance by Miss Constable to the prisoner and Miss Black well, in consideration of 4000 l . and 8 s ., composed of a mortgage debt of 2350 l ., 1109 l . 7 s ., the debt due to Stanley's executors, 161 l . 1 s ., the amount paid to Sutcliffe and Birch, and 380 l . for the amount of our debt and interest, altogether making 4000 l . and 8 s .—there is no provision of any annuity or any surplus for her—she ex pressed her readiness to give the prisoner up any surplus there was, and there is a covenant by the prisoner and his sister to indemnify her for mortgage debts, taking them to themselves—there is mention of the estate in Wales—it is a very small estate indeed—Miss Constable and the prisoner both said it was a very small cottage—there is also some property in Huntingdon that is not valuable, worth 300 l . or 400 l ., I believe, not more.
   MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  She attended, on the fourth occasion, for the purpose of executing this deed? A.  Yes, and I believe she then executed it, though I am not the attesting witness.
   COURT . Q.  Before she executed the deed, did you explain it to her? A.  I explained it to her fully—I did not read it in full, but I explained it to her, and she understood it—the consideration was supposed to be exactly 4000 l . of this deed; and when I came to add up these executed deeds, it came to more; therefore it became necessary to have the stamp altered, and she went into the calculation with me—I checked it with her, and the prisoner—I made her quite understand, that the prisoner was not to be her trustee—that she was to have no expectation—that she was to discharge her father's supposed debts and have nothing—she never gave me to understand that there were any debts of her father's beyond this mortgage debt, and those debts mentioned here—all the property was to go in liquidation of those debts—she was to be free of the debts.
   Q.  And also free of any inheritance,—to be abroad in the world without a shilling? A.  Yes; but she always stated the property to me, and the prisoner too, as worth very little more than the mortgage debt—I did not take any pains to sift into the reality of any debt to the Stanleys—I did not know whe ther he was a man capable of lending 1 s .—I heard nothing of him—I have got a minute the first time I saw Miss Constable and the prisoner, that he (Stanley) was a friend of Mr. Constable, and acted for him—I only knew what they told me, that Mr. Stanley was a friend of Mr. Constable, and that he was an elderly gentleman—I understood that—I knew nothing except what Miss Constable and the prisoner told me.
   Q.  Did you not think, for the protection of a lady, ignorant of law affairs, that it was your duty to satisfy yourself that this consideration existed? A.  I did, in the fullest way possible—I explained every thing to her, and I think Miss Constable knew very well what she was about, from the four or five interviews I had with her, and if she misled me I cannot help it—I acted for her afterwards in several things.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  Am I to understand that you knew nothing of the circumstances of Stanley? A.
