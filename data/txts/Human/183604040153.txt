   Q.  Then supping he held that situation when he was on duty there, I take it for granted he had opportunities of defrauding the revenue? A.  I don't know, I suppose he had—he had the care of the money in the same way—he could not have applied it to his own use long—he would have been obliged to have returned it—if he had the money as I have, I suppose he might use it—he might have destroyed a foreign letter, and pocketed the postage—there would have been an inquiry about the letter—persons inquire if a letter is missed—I do not receive letters for the colo nial department—it is the gentleman who does the hags—I have the oppor tunity of pocketing the money and destroying a letter—this paper is a list of the letter found in Mr. Barnes's hat—I merely took the places and stamps—I know nothing of the names—there might be more letters for Madras—the paper enables me to tell the places from which they come and to which they are destined—I have not the names—the postage of other letters in London to the same places would be the same on all letters—a private soldier's would go for one penny—Mr. Barnes ought to have asked the bagman for the money on these letters, and not me—Mr. James was the bagman on duty on Friday—he is here—the letters lay exposed in the prisoner's hat—any person in the room would have seen them—he was sitting by the fire at the time I took the account of them, apparently asleep—he did not appear unwell, or fatigued—he said he was sleepy.
   Q.  Is it usual to give him money and letter without making inquiry when he asks for them? A.  We have no right to ask him, certainly—I should not have given him money without his producing letters—he has asked me for money with letters, but not without them—I do not require to see the letters—If he held them in his hand it would he quite sufficient—I had not time to take down the full addresses of the letters, and I did not think it necessary—I did not do it for both those reasons—the Foreign Office is a large room, much about the size of this court—there are two fire-places. One for the messengers, the other where the clerk generally sit—all the clerks in the Foreign Office are entitled to come into this room, messengers and all—the windows are opposite the fire-place and the door—(looking at a plan of the office)—there is a screen between the door and fire-place, and the window at which I was on duty—it is ten feet high, I should think—it goes nearly the whole length of the room—when the door of the screen is shut the persons at the fire-place cannot see the window where I am—that door is exactly opposite the window at which I was placed—the door in the screen shuts with a spring of its own accord—it must he held or fixed to be kept open—I often leave the window to go to the fire-place in the course of the day—I leave nobody at the window then—it is closed—it has a slide, which is fastened by a catch or spring—that spring is in bad condition—I had left the window when I looked into Mr. Barnes's hat, and nobody was behind the screen—there was a messen ger at the further end of the room, with whom I had no communication—I am a permanent clerk in the establishment—it is the course of the post
