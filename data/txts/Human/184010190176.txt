I am in the employ of Mr. Wood, a pawnbroker in St. John-street-road. Howes has pawned things with me which ap peared to be gold—it was gold of an inferior description—I lent 5 l . 10 s . on it—it turned out to be worth something more than 4 l .—an attempt was afterwards made to pledge a lady's gold chain—I tried it and found it to be spurious, and detained it—an action was brought against us in about a week by Lilly—Lilly told me he bad bought it and paid for it him self, and knew it to be gold—I went to him to take him into custody—he said he had left the action entirely in his solicitor's hands—he said he had sent it to pledge, that it was his—my employer had about 32 l . 10 s . to pay in consequence of that proceeding—I knew Lilly before—I formerly lived with Mr. A tensor, a pawnbroker, and knew him there—he offered to pledge seve ral things there, generally new clothes—they were things made up entirely to impose—on one occasion our young man put a mark on them, and Lilly attempted to throw an inkstand at him—the lady's gold chain was returned to Lilly's solicitor after the action—I suppose my master paid the 32 l . 10 s . to Lilly's solicitor.
   Cross-examined by MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  How long ago was this throw ing the inkstand? A.  Between two and three years—the chain was offered four or five months ago, about April or March—our solicitor returned the chain—I have been a pawnbroker between seven and eight years—I do not exactly know that it was a D that was tried to be marked on the articles—it was done with ink, to let the next pawnbroker know he was a regular thief, and prevent his pawning it. SAMUEL HOWES re-examined.  I took this gold chain to endeavour to redeem a bar of gold with it, but they would not take it in—Lilly said he would bring an action against them, and wanted to know what attorney would bring it—I recommended him to the attorney who brought the action—after the chain was brought back and returned, they wanted to bring another action against Wood for special damages, and I refused to swear there was any special damage. JOSEPH THOMAS LAWRENCE. I am shopman to Mr. Barker, a pawn broker. I do not know either of the prisoners. On the 9th of November last Howes pledged a suit of clothes and a ring with me—he wanted 4 l . 10 s . on them, and said he had had 4 l . on them the week before—I said I could not make them more than he had before—he gave me another ring, and I lent him 4 l . 10 s .—they are worth about 43 s .—these clothes are riggers or duffers  —they are made to shine as they do by extra pressing.
 ----  SHOWELL . I am in the service of Mr. Spinks, a pawnbroker. To the best of my belief, Lilly is the man who came to our shop with a pair of table-spoons, which he represented were silver, and a lady's chain, which he represented as gold—he wanted 5 l . 10 s . for them, which he had—I think the table-spoons are worth about 1 l ., and the chain about 30 s .—I do not know Howes.
   Cross-examined by MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  How can you judge of the worth? A.  Because Mr. Simmonds got it assayed—we tried the usual test, but it is made up for the express purpose of standing the test—if this were genuine gold it would sell for about 8 l ., I think.
