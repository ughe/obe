Cross-examined. Q.  Did you take the trouble to measure exactly where-abouts in the wall this hole was? A.  No, I did not—I suppose it was a four inch wall—the hole was nearly on a level with the floor; it might be three inches and a half below the level of the cupboard—I did not take notice—a communication could have been made with the loft by knocking out one brick; the top brick was what we term a bat , that is, half a brick, or a three-quarter brick—there was about a half sieve basket among the rags, and a butter firkin; none of that was my property—I do not know whe ther any of it belonged to Mr. Hodges—the prisoner's three children were at this very time in the house, sitting by the fire in the front parlour—when I met the prisoner, and spoke to her about the fire, she said she would go and see who was there, or something to that effect—I do not think I mis took excitement for the effect of drink; I am quite certain of that.

On the night of 20th Sept., I was at my own house, at Greenwich. I discovered the appearance of fire, and went into Mr. Brinfield's house, No. 2, Bennett-street, where it was raging—Mr. Moore went with me; we went up stairs together—about six or seven pails of water were used to extinguish the fire—I gave Mr. Moore two upstairs, which he threw on—a hammer was fetched, and while he was using it to make a hole, I threw on three or four more pails—I saw the bricks which had been displaced; it appeared to have been recently done—as I was going up the stairs I saw a crack in the lath and plaster wall, and through that crack I could see the fire burning distinctly—the hole in the cupboard was apparently made from the cupboard into the loft; the hole appeared to be made at the bottom of the cupboard; the bricks appeared to have been driven through into the place where the rags and paper was burning—my brother constable has the bricks, which he found afterwards and fitted—I saw some mortar in the loft, which appeared as if it had been forced through.
   Cross-examined. Q.  I believe this poor woman and her children were in a state of wretched poverty? A.  They were, there was no furniture in the upstairs room. JOHN TESTER. ( policeman, R  89). I took the prisoner into custody on 20th Sept. Before doing so, I examined the hole in the wall—it was on a level with the floor of the cupboard—the first brick was placed on the floor, and the other over it—they were both above the level of the floor—in my judgment, the hole was made from the cupboard into the loft, by the way in which the bricks fitted the hole—there is a notch on the brick, showing that it was punched out from the cupboard side ( producing the bricks )—I fitted the bricks so as to tell in what way they were placed—I took possession of some of the rags that were left unburnt, and produce them—among them is a piece of blue shirting—I also produce some rags which I found in the room—there is some blue shirting amongst that, which corresponds with that found in the loft—it was about 11 o'clock when I saw the prisoner—she was intoxicated.
