He told me he had written it; I was close by him when he wrote it, but it was not by my direction; he did not read it to me. I saw Mr. Abraham several times with him; I don't recollect that I ever read it or saw it till now; I swear that; I don't recollect hearing it read; I don't know when it was written; it was written since my return from Cheltenham: I think it was before I employed Mr. Hubert. I had been con sulting Mr. Abraham about Mr. Askew's not delivering to me my chest of drawers and all the furniture of my bed-room. I went to Mr. Abraham before I employed Mr. Hubert. I don't know whether this paper was written to Mr. Abraham as instructions what he was to do; there is no part of my writing on the outside of that paper. I applied to Mr. Abraham perhaps about a fortnight before I went to Mr. Hubert, to whom I was recommended by the French Consul. I never mentioned to any one about the 9500 francs as being too much for me to lose: I mentioned it to Mr. Hubert when I begged him to apply for it. I never mentioned to Mr. Abraham or his clerk the particular things that were in the drawers. Mr. Abraham gave me an order to fetch my things from Mr. Askew, but not the jewellery: he was not employed for the jewellery, but for my agreement and the furniture of my bed-room. I may have told him about the jewellery, but as I expected to get the furniture, I thought I should get that. I don't recollect that I told him about it. I told him of a bill which I had paid Mr. Askew for my furniture. I don't recollect whether Mr. Abraham asked me for particulars, that he might make a demand; I can't swear it. This is the list I made out for Mr. Hubert to make the demand; part is my writing and part Bertrand's Mr. Bertrand lived in the Rue de Grenelle, No. 39; he is no relation of mine; he was a grocer; I have called him my brother by the order of Mr. Askew. Mr. Askew told me so in the carriage from Paris to London. Bertrand was not coming to England then, but he was invited to England by Askew. Mr. Askew gave me a particular reason for calling Bertrand my brother; I used to write to him very often: Mr. Askew desired me to call Bertrand my brother, for the reputation of his house, after Bertrand arrived in London, but not before; I said so at the Police Office; but the first time he told me to call him my brother was in the carriage. He then gave me no reason at all for it. I had never called Bertrand my brother before I came to London, but always spoke of him as such at Mr. Askew's. Mr. Askew told me in Bertrand's shop,
 "My desire is to make you happy when you come to London, and Bertrand will be there by and by." Bertrand gave me the Billet de Banque in the beginning of May; he brought them with his own things in his trunk, which was locked: it was kept in my bed-room, and I always had the keys; there were two locks, one was a padlock. Bertrand had been in London but a few days when he gave me the Billet de Banque; I was in my bed-room when he gave them to me out of his pocket-book to keep for him. Mrs. Hudson saw them one morning, and Bertrand came in and scolded me for it; that was about a month before I went to Cheltenham. Bertrand had some French and English gold beside. He never borrowed any money from me or any body to my knowledge. He had some bills which were not Billet de Banque, and I think he had some other Billet de Banque; he gave me more, and he used to go to the drawer, and take out what he wanted. I will not swear that I have seen him with any other Billet de Banque; I do not recollect when Bertrand came to England. He used to sleep in a warehouse at Mr. Askew's house; he removed from there about three weeks before I went to Cheltenham; he went to live in Berwick-street, at Mrs. Broadfoot's; Mrs. Askew took the lodging for him. I have sent him sometimes little different things, when he used to give me the money; Mr. Askew gave me permission to do so; he had a quarrel with Bertrand before I went to Cheltenham, and Mr. Askew forbade him the house, but gave me permission to send him some victuals; after I was at Cheltenham, Askew told me that he continued to send him things, and wrote so to me. He continued to lodge at Mrs. Broadfoot's, and I lodged there on my return from Cheltenham. Mrs. Broadfoot had never turned him away up to the time I left London for Cheltenham. Although Bertrand was not in the house, I continued to supply him with provisions, but not at Askew's expence: Bertrand paid very dear for it; the Billet de Banque were left in my possession because he went into furnished lodgings, and they are not always very safe. I told Bertrand on the Saturday evening, that I should bring them to him on Sunday morning before I went to Cheltenham, but did not, because we went too early - myself and two young ladies. I went on Sunday morning; I knew that I was going on the Thursday; Mr. Askew had before desired me to go, but I always refused, and even on Saturday night, when my box was packed up, I had not agreed to go. On the Thursday, or the day before, Mr. Askew returned from Cheltenham, and said he had taken a house, and he hoped I would go to oblige him, but I had not agreed to go; I was very cross with him on Saturday night; I had been to Bertrand, and promised to give him his billet de banque on Sunday morning; I saw him between ten and eleven o'clock at night; he lives but a little way from Mr. Askew, who lives at No. 85, Oxford-street, nearly opposite the Pantheon. Mr. Askew went to Bertrand for him to come again, and persuade me to go to Cheltenham; he had before talked of taking a house in the country, and wanted Bertrand to go and keep it for him; I did not, upon my oath, see Bertrand on the Sunday morning, before I set off; I went at six o'clock, and it was a wet morning, which was the only reason I did not give him the Billet de Banque; I had to dress myself and to breakfast, I never left any direction with Bertrand to go and take any of these things. I never took any account or number of these bills - it is not the custom in France. There were some of them like this one, but I cannot tell whether they are all alike; I did not look at them particularly. I did not take many white stockings to Cheltenham; I took five or six pair, and was obliged to buy some there. Mr. Askew told me not to take much with me, because unless it pleased me, I need not stay there. I did not take even a white dress; I was obliged to buy some.
