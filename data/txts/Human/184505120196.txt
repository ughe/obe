HENRY TURNER. I was not examined before the Magistrate, or at all in this case.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Do you know where your brother is? A.  No—I heard from him last about a fortnight after this took place—he was then down in Somersetshire—I did not tell my master or the officer—I did not know they were searching for him—I went with them once to search for him at a place where they thought he was lodging, but he was not there—I could not tell where he was to be found—that was before I had the letter.
   COURT . Q.  Under what circumstances did he leave Mr. Godfrey? A.  He was too long gone with goods on Greenwich fair day—Mr. Godfrey complained of it, and they parted—I never saw the prisoner till the day he was taken—I have been with Mr. Godfrey above four years.
   MR. HICKS re-examined.  When the prisoner came to me he was dressed in a leather apron up to his collar, with a strap of leather round, much like the dress worn by Mr. Godfrey's men—I asked him both if it was Mr. Godfrey's order, and if it was Mr. Godfrey's handwriting. GUILTY. Aged 41.— Confined Two Years .

WILLIAM CHURCHILL was indicted for stealing, on the   5th of May , at   Camberwell , 1 wooden till, value 2 s  . 6 d  .; and 3 pence; the property of Airth Mungo , in his dwelling-house; and afterwards, about the hour of one in the night, burglariously breaking out of the said dwelling-house.
   MR. PAYNE conducted the Prosecution. WILLIAM SMITH. I am pot man to Mr. Airth Mungo, who keeps the George public-house in the Camberwell-road, in the parish of Camberwell. I fastened up the house on Sunday night, the 4th of May, about twelve o'clock, or twenty minutes after—I fastened the front door with an iron bar and a prop, and the back door in the same way—there is an inner door near the bar, leading to the private part of the house, that fastens with I bolt only, nearest to the bar—I bolted it—about ten minutes after one I was called by the police—I went down stairs, and found the till taken out of its place, and put on the counter—I had left it in its place under the counter, and a little copper money in it—I believe none was taken—the doors were all three unfastened and open—about eleven o'clock on the Sunday night I had seen three persons in the tap-room—I believe the prisoner to be one of them—I saw them come in and out—the last time I saw them was coming in—I did not go into the tap-room after that, and did not see them go out—when called by the police I observed some soot on the counter, and likewise about the stove in the tap-room—I saw a cap and a pair of shoes lying there—they were also sooty—a person could get from the back of the house over the garden fence, and so get away over an open piece of ground—the policeman has the shoes.
   Cross-examined by MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  You did not take anything into the tap-room to them? A.  No, I did not—I was at the bar, and could see them—our house is a kind of passage; people walk through all the day long; they use it as a thoroughfare into George-street, which leads into Southampton-street and into New Peckham—it is out of the way to Camberwell-green—I know Wells by sight—he comes in sometimes, like another customer—he lives just behind the house.
   MR. PAYNE . Q.  Did Wells come in that morning after the police were there? A.  Yes, at one o'clock—he told them something—the soot appeared as if somebody had been up the chimney.
