ELIZABETH HORROD. I am a widow, living in Three-Cup-alley. I know there was a mangle on those premises—I do not know myself whose
 it was—I went there with the intention of purchasing it—I have since seen it at the police-office, and it is the one I saw on those premises.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Had you ever seen it before? A.  Yes, about a year and nine months ago—I did not buy it, because it was too dear——there was no crack in the iron-work—there was something the matter with the spindle, and the handle would not keep on—I did not offer 1 l . and my own mangle for it—I have paid about a quarter of a year's rent—I took the place of Mrs. Fossett, not of the prosecutor. JOHN DAWSON. I am a furniture-broker, and live in Old-street-road. The prisoner called on my wife, and said he had got a mangle to sell, I sent two of my men for it, but I went to the premises first, and treated with the prisoner for the purchase of it—I was to give him 4 l . 15 s . for it—he asked 6 l . or 7 l .—the mangle came to my shop, and I paid for it—there was a mangle claimed by Mr. White, and he removed one—but I mean to swear he has removed a wrong mangle.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Was the one removed by Mr. White the one you got from the prisoner? A.  It was not, it was one I purchased of a man named Webb, who is here—the one I bought of the prisoner has the name of Moore croft on it, and the one they swore to was one of Baker's make—the mangle identified by Mr. White has one of the ears in two pieces—I was not present when the prosecutor identified the mangle, I was in bed—I did not object to their coming on my premises.
