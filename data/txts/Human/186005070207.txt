I have seen him wash himself in the public room. JOHN JAMES PARSONS. I am a labourer of Kingswood-place, South Lam beth—on Friday, 16th March, about half-past 9 o'clock, I was returning from work, and saw the prisoner and the deceased come out of the Elephant and Castle—they crossed over the road and went into one of the railway arches—there were one or two others with them—I stood at the gate of the Vaux hall station and looked in—it was within the enclosure—I stood there two or three minutes, and saw them pull off their smock frocks for the purpose of fighting—they stood sparring for a minute or two, and then the prisoner rushed at Faye with his head downwards, caught hold of his legs, and turned him over—he fell on the back of his head very heavily—he was the heavier man of the two, and about forty years of age—he laid there for a few seconds, groaning, and was picked up—he stood up as well as he could, and then Harper went at him a second time—there was more sparring, and he went in to him and turned him over in the same way, and the fall was much heavier than the first—he fell on the back of his head—they were both intoxicated—I remained two or three minutes, but he was not sensible—he never spoke; he groaned very much, and was dragged away to the Elephant and Castle—I then went for a policeman, but could not find one, and when I came back I saw the prisoner and Harrington fighting.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Had you ever seen either of them before? A.  I had seen the deceased—I cannot say that I have seen the prisoner, but am quite sure he is the man—they passed me on the opposite side going to the arches, but I could not hear what they said to each other—the deceased was much heavier and taller, and apparently a much more powerful man than the prisoner—they were both intoxicated.
   MR. SHARPE . Q.  How long was all this taking place under the arches? A.  I should think twenty minutes—I did not see the back of the deceased's head and cannot say whether there was any wound.
