   Cross-examined by MR. WILDE. Q.  How far is Mrs. Stansburry's yard from the railway station. A.  I should say two miles and a half, or it may be three miles—I observed the two pieces of lead at two o'clock in the morning, on the 2nd of Sept.—there was gas burning in the warehouse—I never took it in my hand—I had looked down upon it and noticed it—there were only these two pieces on the premises—there were a number of persons coming backwards and forwards to this particular spot, perhaps fifteen or sixteen—there are only nine porters there at nigh—I do not know how many there are by day—I know nothing about the day duty—I saw this lead safe at two o'clock, and the prisoner was found with it about eleven o'clock in the morning—I did not miss it from the premises till ten o'clock in the morning—we have had great reason to complain of persons pilfering on the premises—I did not offer the prisoner five shillings if he would tell me the person who had taken the lead.
   MR. BODKIN. Q.  How long had the lead been there after its arrival? A.  It came on the 31st of Aug.—my attention was called to it before the morning of the 2nd of Sept., and I had spoken to the men about it—I saw it in the same place on the 2nd of Sept., at two o'clock in the morning, I missed it at ten o'clock, and did not see it again till after the prisoner had been taken, about seven o'clock in the evening—I am quite confident it is the same lead I saw at the station—the prisoner has been in the habit of coming there for about twelve months—during that time we have lost things—this is the lead—it is worth about 20 s .
   MR. WILDE. Q.  Was Mr. Knowles present when the wagon was loaded? A. I am managing clerk to Mrs. Stansbury, the carrier, at the George-yard, Aldermanbury. The prisoner was in her employ—he and a man named Neale went with a cart to the Bricklayer's Arms—I can not say whether the prisoner went with Neale on the morning of the 2nd of Sept.—I was going out of Mrs. Stansbury's yard that morning, and met the prisoner in the street, within two yards of the yard—it was from half-past eleven to twelve o'clock—he had a bag on his back, which appeared to con tain something heavy—I said, "Well, John, what have you got there?"—he said, "A parcel a person gave me at the station, to deliver just down here"—I said, "Who gave you the parcel?"—he hesitated, and said, "That is of no consequence"—I said, "What is it you have got?"—I put my hand to the bottom of the bag, and felt it was very weighty—I told him to come to the gateway, and show me what he had got—he came and opened the bag, and I saw two pieces of lead in it—I said, "Here is something very mysterious here, it has the appearance of rogueishness , take it to the office up the yard"—he took it to the office, and I had it weighed—one piece weighed 84lbs., the other 36lbs.—he gave no further account of how he became possessed of it—I said I must make further inquiry into it, and the lead was detained at the office—I did not see the prisoner again from that time till seven o'clock in the evening, when Mr. Shaw brought him—the lead was then produced—the prisoner said nothing particular about it that I took notice of—he had said in the morning, when I asked him a second time, that he found it in Neal's van; and another account that he gave in the evening was, that be
