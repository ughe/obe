WILLIAM BARNETT was indicted for stealing on the 22nd of November , 9 locks, value 27s.; 1 pair of brass butts, value 15s.; 2 bolts, value 8s.; 6 ends for blinds, value 6d.; 1 brush, value 6d.; 1 latch, value 2s. 6d.; 4 locks, value 2s.; 6 drawer-handles, value 18d., and 8 window-rods, value 2s., the goods of Hugh Welch Cooper.
   BENJAMIN PHILLIPS. I am a Policeman. On the 28th of November, about half-past five o'clock in the morning, I stopped the prisoner in Gray's Inn-lane, carrying a heavy basket - I asked what he had there; he said some furniture to put in a house in Blackfriars-road, and that his father was a master builder; I said I did not believe him - he said I might see what he had got; I examined it, and said I should take him to the watch-house - he took up the basket, walked a few steps, then threw it on me, and ran away; I sprang my rattle, and Haines stopped him in three minutes - I am sure of his person.
   WILLIAM HAINES. I am a Policeman. I was in Bell-court, Gray's Inn-lane, and heard a rattle spring - the prisoner passed me almost immediately; I pursued, and never lost sight of him till I brought him back - he refused to go; there was a struggling between us, and he took hold of the lamp-post, but I brought him back.
   GEORGE EVA. I am foreman to Hugh Welch Cooper, a builder in Wakefield-street, St. Pancras - these things are all his. On the evening of the 21st, at half-past five o'clock, I put them on a shelf in the first floor of his workshop - the door was locked; I found the window forced in next morning, about seven o'clock, and every thing in confusion, from one end to the other; the premises are near the bottom of Gray's Inn-lane.
 Prisoner's Defence. I live by King's-cross - I was going to work, and found these things in the fields.
     GUILTY. Aged 19. - Transported for Seven Years.
 Fifth Middlesex Jury, before Mr. Common Sergeant.

 235.  WILLIAM FARROW was indicted for stealing, on the 1st of March , 1900 sheets of printed paper, value 12l., the goods of George Smallfield, his master.
 MR. PHILLIPS conducted the prosecution.
   GEORGE SMALLFIELD, I am a printer, and live at Homerton; the prisoner was in my service for sixteen years and a half. In consequence of having paper burnt, I had occasion lately to take stock; I missed a great number of volumes - I never gave the prisoner permission to take these volumes, and sell them for waste-paper; they are all saleable works - the Rev. Mr. Aspland's hymn forms a part of them, which is a work now on sale.
 COURT. Q. Did you ever give him authority to sell waste-paper? A. Not on his own account; he had sold some for me, but certainly he had no authority to sell this - this is not waste-paper.
 Cross-examined by MR. ROWE. Q. Had you given him permission to sell any other volumes? A.None for himself, he has on my account; I took stock in October last - my business is not very extensive; I have about twelve workmen now - they could go to my warehouse, but the greater part of them had no right to go; no other person has ever sold paper for me.
   ALFRED ANDREWS. I am in the prosecutor's employ. About the 4th of November, 1830, I was returning from Stamford-hill, and saw the prisoner in the high road, going to London, with master's cart; I got up in the cart - there were two bundles of paper in it - he went to Old-street-road, and left the paper at Riley's house; the prisoner gave me 6d. - he had before that spoken to me respecting some paper which I saw him take; he held up his finger, and said, "Never you say a word, or you will ruin me and my family;" that was one day when we took some paper - that was all that passed.
 COURT. Q. A word about what? A. About some paper that was in the cart, I suppose; he took some six or seven times - I do not know when he said that; it was some time before, when we were taking paper there.
 Cross-examined. Q. How long have you been in your master's service? A.Three years next May; I do not know where this paper came from - the first time I saw it was at the top of my master's lane; I had before been with him when he took paper - I did not mention this to my master till after the prisoner was in trouble.
   JOHN RILEY. I am a cheesemonger, and live in Old-street. Shortly before Christmas, 1830, the prisoner brought some printed paper to me, in Mr. Smallfield's cart, which I bought of him; it was, I think, between 30 lbs. and 40 lbs. - I sold that paper, with others, to Faulkner, Mr. Wilson's man; the prisoner did not make any representation to me - it was in two bundles, and I paid him 3d. per 1b. for it; I sold some of the best at 4 1/2d. per 1b., and used the inferior part at my own place.
 Cross-examined. Q. At what hour of the day was this? A. He generally came between ten and one o'clock- I saw his master's name on the cart, and he brought it openly into the shop.
   WILLIAM FAULKNER. I am servant to Mr. Gilbert Wilson- he lives in Upper Thames-street, and deals in waste-paper. I bought some paper of Mr. Riley the latter end of March, or the beginning of April; I took it home to my master's - I remember Mr. Smallfield coming and seeing the same paper which I got from Riley - this is it; I am sure it is part of what I bought of Mr. Riley.
 Cross-examined. Q.What is your master? A. A rag and waste-paper dealer; we buy a great deal from various quarters - we frequently buy volumes of booksellers; I took this home, and it remained there six or seven weeks; I took other paper with this, but of a different size - this is of a particular size; I think this paper remained at my master's from March to October; there is another man who weighs the paper, and stows it away in piles.
