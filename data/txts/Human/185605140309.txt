   Cross-examined by MR. MAC ENTEER . Q.  You say that when he first came in he appeared in a state of great excitement? A.  Yes; I never saw him so excited before; but I was never robbed before—he came into my service on let Dec. last—I had a character with him from Mr. Fuller, a clergyman in my parish, who had only had him a month—he gave me the character which he had with him—I have communicated with several of his former masters since—I cannot exactly say that the inquiries were satisfac tory, but I do not wish to say otherwise—he lived for a year and a half at Mr. Kemp's, who seemed quite satisfied—one of the prosecutors afterwards found a dozen and a half of old silver spoons, a pint tankard, and a tray which had been taken out and left—with that exception all the plate was gone—I cannot form an estimate of the weight of it—a great deal of it had been presented to me—it was worth, I believe, 500 l . or 600 l .—there is an entrance by the area to my house; it has a lock, and with that gate and one door, you can gain admission to the house—he told me that he had not spoken to a policeman when he found it out—I said, "How did you know when you came home that the people were not in the house?"—he said, "I looked into the dining room"—I said, "Did you look into the drawing room?"—he said, "No"
   Cross-examined by MR. PARRY . Q.  Have you made inquiry about the character of Peshell? A.  Yes; his master called on me—he been a high character; and I hope his master will be here to-day; here is a letter from him giving him an excellent character ( produced )—I asked Chamell why he did not call me when he found out the state of the home—he said that he did not like to disturb me.
   Cross-examined by  Mr. COOPER . Q.  Have you made inquiry about Tillet's character? A.  I believe his master has a good opinion of him; I know nothing about it, except that he is a respectable man. THOMAS THURSTELL. ( police inspector V ). On Saturday, 5th April, about half-past 7 o'clock, I was fetched to Mr. Fisher's—I examined the premises, but found no trace of any entry being effected at any part of the house—I examined the area gate, the door leading from the area into the house, and the front door, but there was not a single mark on either of them—I had the lock of the door leading from the area into the passage, taken off by a smith, but could discover nothing to show that it had been tried—in the butler's pantry I found a small plate chest which bore marks of having been forced—I have examined the larger chest carefully, but there are only very slight marks on it—I am of opinion that those marks were not made then, but that the chest was actually open—there are two locks on it, and both were in perfect order—these ( produced ) are the keys; they were given to me by Charnell among other keys on a ring—the door of the plate closet was perfectly sound—it is a large iron door, about the size of one of the doors of this Court—it could not have been broken open without a sledge hammer—the proper key was in the door, and it locked it.
