ARTHUR PEARsoN LUFF, M.D. and M.R.O.P. On the 11th June I
received from the coroner officer a glass jar containing a stomach, liver, a
portion of brain, and a portion of a lung also a second jar containing
intestines, kidneys, and spleen; and a small tin box containing vomit,
and a paper pareel containing sheets and pillowease; I analysed all
the viscera, and found no poison-in the vomit I found a trace of what
E believe to be strychnine; it was too small to be weighed by the
most
delicate balance-in death from stryehnine it does not necessarily
follow that traces of it will be found in the contents of the stomach; if
it does not produce death very rapidly, it undergoes absorption and
elimination, mainly in the urine-the period within which death happens
varies considerably, according to the strength of the dose and the con-
stitution of the patient-it might be discoverable elsewhere-it would
depend upon the length of time elapsing after it has been swallowed;
I could tell of cases where three hours after death none has been dis-
covered-the smallest dose that has been known to kill an adult is half a
grain, and the smallest that ever killed a child between two and three
years of age is 1-16 of a grain-vomiting is a rare symptom after
strychnine-] have heard the evidence given to-day of the post-mortem
appearances; they are consistent with death from stryehnine.
Cross-ecamined. No poison was found in this viscera; I analysed it
carefully-in my own experience I have always found stryehnine in such
cases-i is the rule that so much of the poison is absorbed as it takes to
produce death, and whatever is over and above that remains-if it is
absent from other parts of the body I should consider that the most
likely spot to find it would be in the spinal cord-congestion of the
upper part of the spinal marrow is not an uncommon post-mortem sign
-the spinal cord was not sent to me-I agree with Professor Stevenson
in this, that stryehnine can hardly fail to be detected in the body within
two hours-.] stated before the Magistrate, "I should not, from my
examination of the organs, be justified in saying that stryehnine had
been taken, or that the deceased had died from the administration of
strychnine, judging from the organs that were sent to me, and from the
result by analyses"- is simply from the result of my examination
of that which was sent to me-from my examination of the organs in
this case I should not feel justified in saying that the deceased had died
from stryehnine, but after hearing the other witnessses, combined with
my analyses, I say it is possible that it was a death from stryehnine; that
is my opinion now-if death ensues within two hours after the adminis-
tration of stryehnine, I should expect to find traces of it in the body-a
grain would be amply sufficient to kill a boy of eight ; if more were
