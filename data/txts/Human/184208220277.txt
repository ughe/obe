   Cross-examined. Q.  Was the prisoner in the act of falling at the time the blow was struck? A.  I believe he was—we were all in the act of falling together—Smith No. 119, was the other constable—he is not here—I did not see Collins's hat go off—Collins had hold of the prisoner first, not me—I did not remain at Tower-street station all that night—I stopped there till I had lodged the prisoner there, and then I went back to see where Collins was—I had been in reserve in Kennington-lane before the meeting—I saw some beer there, and I had a pint myself, for which I paid twopence—that was all I had—I had had nothing to drink before that except my tea at breakfast—I had my dinner in Keanington-lane—that was when I had the pint of porter—I believe there were cans of beer, not very large ones—the men were not allowed to have what they liked—Mr. Grinsell, the superintendent, did not allow us more than a pint each.
   COURT . Q.  How long had yon been in reserte there? A.  I think from about half-past ten o'clock in the morning, and remained there all day—we paid for the beer ourselves.
   JURY . Q.  How did you come on the ground? A.  Through the rush of the mob on us—I had hold of the prisoner at the time, and we all fell together—a gentleman, who I believe is a witness, came in to our assistance at the time, besides the constable No. 119. JOHN BROWN. I am a pensioner from the army, and live in Upper Crown-street, Westminster. I am not of any trade or occupation—last Monday evening, I was on the road that leads by the side of Ken nington Common, passing in the rear of those who were standing on the footpath, looking over the palings enclosing the Common—I saw the pri soner, not in the dress he is now in, but in a smock-frock, draw his hand with a stone, and throw it into the Common at the police—I did not see what sort of a stone it was—a policeman immediately jumped over the palings like a shot—the prisoner went a little to his left to conceal himself behind the mob—the policeman immediately seized him by the collar with his left hand—the prisoner resisted violently—the mob journeyed  about, shoved  both the policeman and the prisoner about, and shooed  them, I be lieve, into the middle of the road, where I saw the prisoner and the police man down on the road, the policeman uppermost, and the prisoner covered over, with a considerable quantity of blood on him, which I thought flowed from himself, but which I found afterwards was from the policeman's head—I told the policeman that that was the man that threw take stone—I was junted  about with the mob—I drew a cane, which I had in my hand, in a menacing manner, at the mob, which kept them back till two or three po licemen came in and secured the prisoner.
