   Q.  Was it stated also that the sums had not been filled up in the former deed? A.  I know no deed but the one the Thompsons had executed—I know nothing about sums in a former deed—I never saw the Thompsons' deed of reversion, but they told me it was without consideration, therefore it was of no use—it was not stated that the sums had not been filled up in a former deed, I will swear that—the account was drawn out to insert a certain sum to make the deed that I was about to convey to the prisoner valid—the prisoner proposed that it should be 800 l .—Newhall said no, he thought two sums of 400 l . would be much better—I know of no account—there was no memorandum, or any paper written, in my presence.
   Q.  Did you not just now say there was an account? A.  That is the ac count that you have; that is the one I heard Mr. Newhall had spoken of the other day, when he said he had an account for 1,200 l .—when I was at the prisoner's, the prisoner, Newhall, and myself were present, and alone—I saw Newhall writing, but not on such an account as that; it was a small piece of paper, which he took out of his pocket, the back of a letter, or something he was making memorandums, and certain sums were named—the prisoner said it should be 800 l ., that would be sufficient—they first talked of 1,000 l .—Newhall thought it would be too much, and then the prisoner said it should be 800 l .—Newhall said he thought two sums of 400 l . each would do much better—that was to make the deed I was going to give to the prisoner valid—Newhall said that a deed without a consideration was of no use—what memo randum there was he made himself with his pencil, and Mrs. Blackwell ob jected to it—she put her head just inside the room; she was not in the room, but she called the prisoner out of the room—I heard her say something to him, and he said, "Oh, no, you may trust him with anything," and she went down stairs again—I did not write at all, I will swear that—the memorandum was made with a pencil, which Newball took out of bis own pocket.
   Q.  About how long were you arranging all this? A.  The arrangement, whatever it was, was not more than an hour altogether; I dare say it was an hour—Mr. Newhall, in the prisoner's presence, went into the history of the prisoner's uncle, and the prisoner sat quite dumb—he came to me next evening, and I said I thought Mr. Newhall's conduct with respect to his uncle was very extraordinary indeed—he said he thought so too; he never was so astonished at anything in his life; and be then again repeated that his uncle had no funds, and he would have died in the workhouse if it had not been for him.
   Q.  Just look at this paper, you may read it carefully? A.  There is not one word of truth in it—the sums of money that appear there as paid, were paid out of a sum of 300 l ., for which we had sold our house at Kennington, in which my father had resided for fifty-two years, and I can give you the number of every note that was paid—it was in 50 l . notes.
