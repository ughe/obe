MR. GOWAR re-examined.  Hemorrhage on the lungs produced by violence does not diminish in general—I never knew it do so—if the violence was great I should think it likely that the hemorrhage would continue; but there are so many shades, it depends entirely upon the degree of violence, and the injury occasioned by that violence—in this instance it seemed to increase, under a cer tain state it would diminish—the natural tendency would be to heal—I do not consider the blow on the temple likely to have produced this hemorrhage—I attribute the redness of the neck to squeezing—that alone was not sufficient to occasion the hemorrhage, nor do I consider that the two combined were sufficient—there was no evident mark of any blow, or pressure, or violent impact on the head—there was extravasation about the neck and shoulder, resulting from the following of the blood from the lungs, but that was from the position of the body after death—if she was seized by the throat, and forced violently upon the ground, I think that might have produced the mischief, by a violent jarring  of the system—if that might were so, it would be natural to ex pect to find some mark on the back, but I saw none, nor any bruise—a per son might be pushed down, and receive a violent inward shock, without any external appearance of it whatever—I cannot say that the same concussion would be produced, if in a struggle she fell down, as if she had been thrown down and pressed upon—I found nothing to indicate that she had been cut down and passed upon. ELIZA OLDS. I am the wife of Joseph Olds, a bricklayer, and live at No. 3, John-street, next door to the prisoner; I married his son. On the satur day night before the deceased's death I heard a piece of work there—the youngest boy, William, came to me; and, in consequence of what he said I went in—I found the prisoner and the deceased there alone—the deceased opened the door the moment I got there, and the prisoner shut it again—the deceased went towards the door again—the prisoner would not let her open it—he took her round the waist, and she fell down—she desired me to open the window—I jumped on the table to open it, and Mr. Palmer jumped in at the window—he lives in the same street—my mother was then on the floor by the front door, and my father by her—I do not know whether he had hold of her or not—in my fright I did not see—I do not know how she got on the floor—she was not on the floor when I jumped on the table to open the win dow, but when I opened it and Mr. Palmer jumped in, she was, and the prisoner had hold of her, I did not see in what way—Mr. Palmer went away before I did—I remained with the deceased—my husband came in, and took the pri soner away—he was gone about an hour and a half—I had not seen the de ceased on the Friday—I had seen her on the Thursday—I was with her on the Sunday—she did not appear to be very well then—she complained of being very stiff and sore—she was up, and down stairs on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday, and on the Thursday also—she seemed as if she got very weak and had—she had been very ill for a long time, and getting worse—she had been getting weaker and weaker every day up to this times—she got worse and worse from the Sunday till the Thursday—she died about half-past two o'clock on the Thursday—she was choked, through vomiting blood—I went for the doctor, but she was dead before he came—the prisoner was quite tipsy on the Saturday night.
