Cross-examined by MR. KELLY. Q. Were these articles of use in carrying
on the slave-trade? 1. I presume so-1 acquired my knowledge of what is
useful for the slave-trade from the representations of Mottley himself, who was
on the coast for years that is all I know it from--I have heard the Golup-
chick was sent to England to be sold, having been seized, being fitted up for
the slave-trade-I believe before a vessel sails out from a British port for the
coast of Africa it is examined by the authorities to see if it is fitted up, or has
any articles for the slave-trade-these articles were at Portsmouth, before the
vessel went to Liverpool where she received her cargo
JOSEPH Banks, sen. I am a cooper, and live at Portsmouth. I remem-
ber the Golupchick being there in 1810, and while there, I went on board her
-I was employed to do something to the water casks they were double
Jongers--they were different sizes, but these would contain about 1,000 gal-
lons ench-there were about a dozen--they were entire when I first went on
board--they were found on either side of it, full of water-Mottley, appa-
rently, assumed the command of the vessel at that time-I saw Jennings there
-I did not know Bernardos--I received directions from a person calling him-
self Jennings-he gave me directions to do something with the casks--he
took me on board the vessel in the shipwright's boat-1 found the casks full
of water in the hold of the vessel—I think of the larger kind there was about
twelve, and I should say fifty of the smaller-I numbered each stave of the
largo casks; I rased them with a proper rase-iron which coopers use, then
took the hoops from them, took the casks down, put the hoops inside, and
made close bundles of them, so that they could be put together again-1 re-
ballasted the vessel, and stowed them on the top of the ballast in packs, each
cask in a separate pack-I did the same with the smaller ones, and left others
for water for the voyage--it took me from the 8th to the 19th of Sept. 1840
to do this-while on the vessel, after putting the large casks into the hold,
my attention was attracted by a noise in one of the smaller casks, which
proved to be what was called shackles when I was in the West India trade-
did not count them, but consider, from their weight and appearance, there
were nearly 200 pairs-nothing was done with them while I was there to
all appearance they had been in the vessel before I left the cask Tearly in
the same place as I found it we did not take that cask to pieces; we took
the head out, hearing the noise I did not put the head in again myself.
