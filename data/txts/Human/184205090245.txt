ALFRED BENJAMIN ALLEN. I am assistant to Mr. Shillito, a surgeon. I was called on Wednesday night, the 6th of April, to the stable of Mr. Shiell—the trunk of a woman was shown to me—Superintendent Bicknell, and several officers were there—I examined it—the head bad been severed from the body by a very clean cut through the integuments, and through the second and third vertebrae—I imagine that to hare been done all by one cut—both arms had been taken off above the shoulder joints by a clean cut through the flesh and muscles, and the bones were rather hacked as if with a knife or chopping.
   Q.  Did the appearances present such as would be produced by an axe? A.  Not so much as the thigh bones did—they appeared more like hacking—the legs and thighs bad a clean cut through the integument, and the bones divided just below the great trochanthus  —the flesh was cut, but I think the bones were chopped—this axe was the most likely instru ment to produce the appearances—the bowels were all gone, the abdominal and pelvid viscera—there was a clean cut from the sternum to the pubes, and all the way round to the vertebrae.
   Q.  Are you able to form any judgment whether the incision had been made before or after death? A.  After death, I should say decdodly—we have the means of forming a judgment on that subject—the body was in a most healthy state—there was not the least appearance of disease whatever that I could observe in any part—I did not see the heart or lungs—Dr. Ridge had an order next day to make a post mortem examination, and I was not there—my examination terminated on the Wed nesday—I saw the body again, but did not examine it—from the appear ance of the blood vessels as the body lay, they appeared perfectly empty, both the blood vessels and arteries—the body appeared perfectly empty—from the general appearance of it she was not at all likely to have died of apoplexy or any sudden disease—I should say she could not have been dead longer than three or four days, or I would say five day at far thest—there was not any appearance of decay—decomposition had not taken place at all—there was a very slight smell—I saw the axe that even ing, but did not examine it till before the Coroner's inquest—I certainly think the marks on it are stains of blood—they have more the appearance of blood than anything else—I saw some of the bones on Wednesday, and also afterwards at the inquest—the large bone has the appearance of part of a thigh bone, and here are portions of the skull, and a portion of the knee joint—merely the top of one of the lower bones—these two pieces roost decidedly are portions of a human skull—the serrated edges of the sutures are quite visible—I am rather inclined to say the female was pregnant, but there was not sufficient to decide that—I merely come to the conclusion from a slight appearance on the nipple, and would not speak de cidedly—I should be very sorry to say this is the thigh bone of a human being, in the highly calcined state of it—I did not examine the body in conjunction with Dr. Ridge—there must decidedly have been a most in tense heat to make the bones so highly calcined—I merely looked at the
