EMMA TAYLOR. I live at 36, Mark's Road, and am employed at the South Eastern Railway Station—it is part of my duty to clean out the waiting-room—I was there early one morning, just about the time the Paris mail starts—I do not know the day of the month—I saw the prisoner and prosecutor about 7 o'clock, or a little after, in the general waiting-room—they called for pen and ink, which I gave them, and left it with them—I did not see whether anything was written—I am prepared to swear that it was before 10 a. m.
   Cross-examined.  I had never seen Pfeiffer before—I saw him next in Horsemonger Lane about three weeks ago—the solicitor spoke to me about seeing him—it is not uncommon for pen and ink to be called for—I was very busy—a gentleman came and asked me if I remembered seeing a gentleman on this morning there, and then I remembered it.
   Re-examined.  I picked out the prisoner at Horsemonger Lane from ten or twelve others—I do not remain there all day, I generally leave about 10 o'clock, when my work is done—I am not there of an evening, I am only a charwoman. EDWARD KIPPS. I am a tanner, of 94, Bermondsey—I have known the prisoner six or seven years; he is a respectable, trustworthy man—I have had transactions with him in selling skins for shoe manufacture—he was in my employ six years ago, and afterwards set up as a leather dresser—I do not know whether he is able to carry out contracts for the purchase of skins—I have done business with him within two years, and have a bill of his running now, and should be willing to have another, I have so much confidence in his honesty—to my surprise one day, Mrs. Pfeiffer called on me to say that her husband was in trouble, for conspiracy and fraud; would I be bail for him, and she called again on 25th August and asked me if I would refund the money for him—on 25th August Mr. Woolf and Mr. Bergman called and Mr. Woolf asked me if I would pay the money for him—I said not unless I knew the facts of the case first—he said that Mr. Pfeiffer had had the money to execute a contract, and had failed to do so, and they could neither get the goods nor the money—I asked what was the amount, he said 160 l . and 80 l . expenses—I said it was exhorbitant to expect 80 l ., and they reduced it to 40 l .—I placed that on paper, but previously to that I said that it would be wrong for me to compromise the matter, the best way would be to ask the solicitor's opinion—I afterwards met the prisoner at the station for Nottingham, and told him I could not do it.
