MR. RYLAND conducted the Prosecution. PETER TAGG. I am a slop-seller, and live in Tooley-street, Southwark—the prisoner was in my service, on and off, for three of four years. On Sunday, the 22nd of Aug., she was there, and I gave her into custody—I then went to her lodging, I knew where she lived, as she has been with me so long—the policeman went with me—we found thirty-eight duplicates—I have seen some property of mine at the pawnbroker's—they are here—most of them are mine—five or six of the pawnbrokers gave up the goods—my daughter discovered it, and I told the prisoner to put on her bonnet and shawl, and go with me—she said, "For God's sake, Mr. Tagg, "or "master, forgive me"—my daughter told her she heard her walk in the shop, and my second daughter charged her with stealing a pair of drawers—she professed innocence—she then said she was sorry, and begged I would forgive her.
   Cross-examined by MR. ROBINSON. Q.  It was about the drawers that she said she was very sorry? A.  And also about a pair of boots and a waist-coat—I went to her house the same day—I knew it was her house—I was in the habit of sending my boy to her—I think I was once at her house, eigh teen months ago, and left some message for her. JOHN HIGHWOOD( policeman, M  102.) On the 22nd of Aug. I was on duty in Tooley-street—I went to Mr. Tagg's, and took the prisoner into custody—Mr. Tagg charged her with stealing a pair of drawers, and a pair of boots—she said, "Mr. Tagg, do forgive me this time; I will never do the like again"—I took her to the station—I went on the Monday to several pawnbrokers, and I got a great many articles, which have been shown to Mr. Tagg—the prisoner gave her address No. 34, Brunswick-court, Bermondsey—I went there on the Sunday, and saw a man, who called himself Moore—I found there thirty-eight duplicates, and from them I got the articles at the pawnbrokers—the prisoner owned to the drawers and boots, on the Monday, before the Magistrate, but said she knew nothing about the waistcoat; but of the following Monday, the 30th, she acknowledged everything—she said she could not say but what she had pawned the articles—one dress she said was her own—I should think there were forty pledges. ANN ASLETT. I searched the prisoner at the station—I found on her twenty-two duplicates, a penknife, and a seal—I marked the duplicates, and gave them up. HENRY COTTAM. I am a pawnbroker. I produce twenty-five articles, twenty-two of which have been claimed by Mr. Tagg—the prisoner pawned them between Nov., 1846, and June, 1847—the policeman has the dupli cates which I gave her from time to time.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you seen the prisoner often? A.  Very often—I have known her for the last eleven years pawning at my house—here is a coat and a shirt pawned on the 31st of May—I recollect taking them in—I began then to have some doubt about the matter, and I asked her two or three questions—that was the first time I had suspicion—there were one or two pawnings in June, but I did not take them in—these are the things pawned on the 31st of May. PETER TAGG re-examined.  These are mine—this coat was made only a few days before it was lost, for a person who never came for it.
