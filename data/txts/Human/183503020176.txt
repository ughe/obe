WILLIAM NEWMAN. I am the prosecutrix's son. The prisoners came on the 25th of December—nothing was said about the cow being for Saps worth—the prisoners were both present when the price was mentioned—it was mentioned once or twice—they must both have heard the whole conversation—I went with the cow to Kennington-lane—I was not to leave it till I obtained the money—I got there between eleven and twelve o'clock in the morning—I inquired at several houses, and waited in the lane with the cow nearly two hours—I was not to take it to any parti cular part of the Jane—the lane is about one-eighth of a mile long—a person cannot see from one end to the other, as there is a turning—I saw nobody, and came back with it—I was at home when Gorken came, about six o'clock—it was dark then—Gorken said, "Have you been with the cow?"—I said I had taken her, but could find nobody there, and had brought her back—he said, "If you will come along with me now, I will go with you and show you the place"—my mother said to me, "Mind, do not leave it without the money, either bring back the cow or money"—Gorken turned round and said, "The money is as sure as the Bank of England"—I went to Kennington-lane with him—he took me to Eldridge's house, just through Vauxhall-gate—I saw Eldridge directly we got there—Gorken went on a little before me to the house, and both came back to me together, and Gorken said, "There is the cow;" and said to me, "Help Eldridge to drive her into the yard," and then I could have the money—Eldridge said, "Drive her in," but before that, Gorken said, "I must go, you can drive her into the yard, and then you can have the money"—he then went away, leaving me with Eldridge—we both drove her into the yard—Eldridge and a young lad in the yard tied the cow up—we came out of the yard—there is a public-house nearly opposite his house—he said, "Go into the public-house, and I will come to you in five minutes, and pay you the money"—I waited at the corner of the lane opposite the public-house door, and then I went into the parlour of the public-house—Eldridge came in and called for a pot of ale—we sat there drinking it—he said nothing, and I said nothing—we were there five or ten minutes—then a man named Sapsford (I knew him before, but never spoke above a dozen words with him in my life) came in, and sat down a little while between me and Eldridge, drinking—Sapsford swore and said, "Is your father come home yet?"—I said, "No, he is not,"—he then said, "Eldridge has bought this cow for me; there is a little difference between your father and me, and here is the balance of the money"—I said I did not know any thing at all about it—I had not sold him the cow—I had sold Eldridge the cow, and looked to him for the money—he said, "It is no odds, the cow was bought for me, and here is the difference"—I said I would not leave the cow without the money—I would either have the cow or money—Eldridge was present—I could not take the cow away, for Sapsford said if I touched a bolt or gate belonging to him, he would have me punished—I went to the gate, but I could not get it away—the gates were secured—I have never seen it since.
