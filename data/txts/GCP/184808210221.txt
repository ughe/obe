Prisoner's Defence. Having been arraigned at the bar of this Court to-day
to answer to a charge preferred against me by the Gentlemen of the Govern-
ment, it is not my intention to follow them through all the mazes and
intricacies of the law, for I believe the present existing laws to be so compli-
cated and confused, as to pass my humble powers of conception; but although
I stand at the bar of this Court to-day, I trust that no man in this Court will
for a moment suppose that I stand here accused of any crime, or any act of
dishonesty that I may bave committed against my fellow-men; no, notwith-
standing all the difficulties that I feel surround me this day, it is a consolation
to me to know, that in standing here my honour and integrity as a man, are
not impeached; I am here merely for differing from the Government in
opinion; for that, and that alone, and in support of that assertion, allow me
to draw your attention to the materials out of which this prosecution has been
framed. You have heard that on the evening of the 28th July, a meeting was
held at the Chartist-hall, Webber-street, and that I at that meeting delivered
a speech which has been termed seditious. Was there ever a more diabolical,
s more fabricated statement urged on the face of an indictment than that upon
which I have this day been accused ? I have been accused of having mali-
ciously and wilfully excited her Majesty's subjects to rebellion, and so forth.
Now I contend that the remarks I made use of at that meeting are support-
able by historical facts. You have heard from the evidence of Wm. Counsel,
the Government reporter, that I felt a pleasure and satisfaction in expressing
my opinion of the policy of the British Government towards Ireland, and in
moving the resolution condemnatory of the base and unconstitutional acts of
the British Legislature I stated that it was necessary to refer to history to
support the assertion which I then made, and on referring to history, what do
we find? why, that up to the period of 1782, Ireland was free from English
legislative thraldom, and it was not till that date that the Act 6 Geo. III.
passed, which claimed and exercised the power of British legislation over
Ireland. Now the mere passing of that Act, even of itself, admits the pre-
existence of freedom, and leads us to inquire how a nation, that long before
1800 possessed its own legislature, came to give up to a foreign power the
right of self-government. What do we find? Why, that the Irish parliament,
backed by the Irish people, exerted this power of independence in legisla-
tion for their own country. But passing over that period, and coming down
to the time of the American war, when that people revolted against England
for attempting to trample on their legislative rights, and insisted upon perfect
legislative independence with such an ardour and force of arms, that England
became alarmed and proposed a final adjustment, which was entered into
But England never yet preserved or performed a treaty with Ireland ; and
thus we perceive one encroachment after another, until we come down to the
period of 1800, when the Act of Union was carried by the most diabolical
and contemptible means that was ever resorted to for a class legislation ; which
atrocious acts of plunder has at last plunged the country into all the horrors
of insurrection. Such has been the policy of the English Government towards
Ireland. Will you tell the world, by your verdict this day, that it is sedition
for a man to sympathise with Ireland's wrongs? To tell them that they have
right to use any constitutional means which they have in their power to
remove the tyrants from power who have brought them to death's door by
their acts of tyranny! Will you tell the world, by your verdict, that it is
