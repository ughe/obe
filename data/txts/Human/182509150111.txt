 MR. BRODRICK. Q. Was the  coachman your Lordship spoke to, the driver of the carriage which drove furiously in? A. Yes - I immediately followed it.
 COURT. Q. What distance had the carriage to go from the gateway to the house? A. Perhaps thirty or forty yards. The gateway is nearly sufficient for two carriages.
 Q. When two carriages are jammed in, is it not the proper method for one to go on, or they would get entangled? A. I cannot say.
   JAMES PORTER. I was attending Lord Westmoreland. His Lordship came out about half-past twelve o'clock - he came from the front door towards the gate; when I got to the gate I observed two carriages jammed together, in the gateway - an officer was called for - one came through the gate - he went to the front of the horse, with his staff in his hand, and desired both the coachmen to move back from the gate; he then took hold of the reins, and put them back. I cannot say whether he took hold of the reins of one or both carriages - one of them gave way, the other advanced on, rather furiously, at a fast trot; I did not see the coachman do any thing - there was hardly room to get out of the way, as the carriages came on in that way - I went with my back close against the wall - the carriage passed close by me, and struck the officer, who was on the right, and he fell; I went to his assistance, and found his leg broken - he was taken to St. George's hospital - the carriage passed within three or four inches of my feet.
 Cross-examined by MR. LAW. Q. The officer's right-hand, with his staff, would be in the direction of the carriage on the left of the gateway? A. Yes; I cannot say which carriage backed; if his staff was in his right-hand his left would be at liberty to seize the reins. I moved to the right hand going in - there are two gateways - this is the western one. I heard no intimation of the accident given to the prisoner.
 COURT. Q. Suppose the man, who backed, had been in the right line with a string of carriages behind, could he get back without endangering the carriage? A. I cannot say; I did not see the officer cling to the carriage pole.
   SAMUEL DAVID DANCE. I am porter to Lord George Cavendish. On the 23d of May, between twelve and one o'clock at night, I saw two carriages in the middle gateway, which is the general entrance - there are three gates only - two were opened that night - the center is westward of the other - the two carriages were jammed together, and the coachmen were flogging their horses.
 Q. Were both flogging their horses or only one? A. I cannot say, but I observed the horses flogged - Lord Westmoreland came out, and desired the assistance of an officer, who tried to force them back - he tried to persuade them to go back to Picadilly - I did not exactly notice what he said - I saw him go towards the carriage, and persuade them to come in one at a time; one backed, the other came in with a rush, the officer was forced down - I stood at the end of the gate, the officer was down a little beyond where I stood four or five yards from the gate - I did not particularly notice in what way he fell; he was in front and fell; the carriage wheel went over him in a moment - it was Dawkins, whom Lord Westmoreland called up - I did not leave the gate to go up to him - I afterwards saw him taken away on a shutter.
 Cross-examined. Q. You did not go out to see the line of carriages? A. Yes; this was the proper gate to enter; I found two in possession of the gateway, one made way which liberated the other, and it came in and drove the officer on, he was down in a moment, he fell four or five yards from where the carriages were entangled.
 Q. Whether the horses went forward of their own accord, on feeling themselves at liberty, you cannot say? A. No; it was certainly an accident.
 COURT. Q. What occasioned the accident? A. I do not know; I did not see him hang by the reins or the pole - I cannot say whether the horses forced him forward; he fell a little way beyond where I was - whether he held by the people or reins, I cannot say.
 THE REVEREND  SAMUEL CHARLES GOODWIN, D. D. On the night of the 23d of May, or rather early in the morning of the 24th, I was in Piccadilly, and at the western gate of Burlington House (it was a very light night) I saw a great multitude of carriages reaching to St. James's-street - they were not in order, not in a line; when I came to the western gate two carriages whipped furiously up together - there was no line; both came in at the gateway, which stopped my passing on the path; they were violently contending for priority of entrance; the gateway being insufficient in width to admit two, they were jammed together - both persevered in whipping their horses. I addressed them in a loud tone, saying,"For shame - you cannot both get in together;" I then exclaimed much louder, "You scoundrels - you will spoil your master's horses and carriages too." I was disregarded; or possibly not heard; they persevered in whipping, though they could not stir. I was very much cheered by the people, saying, "Bravo, Sir - we want some such as you;" I then exclaimed, "Constable - officer." Some one within the yard, whom I could not see, but took for an officer, called out "Put back, stand back" - both carriages then backed a little; one of the drivers renewed his whipping, and darted in furiously; I then heard a cry of, "A man knocked down, run over." The multitude closed on the gate-way, and prevented my seeing what had happened. I passed on, and further I depose not.
