TORN Wells. I live in Union-place, Paddington, and keep a stall in
Paddington-market. I was at the Adam and Eve on the evening in ques-
tion, in a room where some persons were ging-Foulkes was in there.
I was getting a little too much beer-there were many people round me,
but who I do not know-Foulkes had caught hold of me by the arm, and
made me sit down by the side of him-I remained with him ten or twelve
minutes--we had a pint of half-and-half together, and then I left the place
-I left Foulkes behind me.
Witnesses for the Defence.
WILLIAM George. I am a newsvender, and live in Duke-street, Broad-
way, Westminster. I know Foulkes by sight-I should be very sorry to
be sequainted with him-I was at the Adam and Eve on Monday night,
the 15th of February-I went about eight o'clock, and staid till a quarter
to twelve o'clock-Foulkes came in-Norris was there that night-be goes
by the nick-name of Billy Button—the prosecutor asked him to toss for a
crow-I am positive of that-Norris would not toss with him, as he had
got no money-he gave that reason-Foulkes seemed rather out of temper
he called me a thief-_I had only asked him to keep quiet, as a
gentleman was about to sing-Roberts was sitting in the same box with
me, and the prosecutor was in the next box-Roberts offered the prosecutor
his pint to drink to keep quiet, and he received a blow in the face for it,
and he called him a counterfeit-on my oath, I saw him strike him
-1 was elevated in the room, as I was in the chair. Roberts left his seat,
but I went in and kept him in the box to prevent a disturbance the land
lord came in and ordered Foulkes out-be said, You must leave my
room; it is not to be annoyed by you," and be left the room and got to
the bar—there were several persons there I understood they were about to
tous—I did not see any thing of that it was after the prosecutor had been
there some time I left the parlour and went to the bar, and he there chal-
lenged Mr. Cordwent to toss him for a sovereign-he said he would not
tous-Foulkes said, " Then I will toss you for 108.," and he put his hand
in his poeket to see if he could find money to toss with, he pulled out some
silver, and Cordwent declined then to toss-he then said to me (I was next
to him), “I will bet you half-a-crown Mr. Cordwent has not 33. in his
possession" - it is a thing I am not in the habit of doing, but I certainly
did bet that night-Cordwent was desired to produce 5s. if he had it, to get
the wager-he did produce more than 50.--I did not get the money-I
have pot had it at all the house was at last cleared by the landlord he was
obliged to expel Foulkes from the bar-he ordered him out when he was
going out he said, "Never mind, I will lay wait"-he was drunk, but
knew what he was about saw him again that night, about one o'clock,
with three women-two of them I knew to be bad characters, girls of the
towa, and one I did not know-he said he would give thein some gin it
the house was open-1 came up just at the time he made use of those
words.
