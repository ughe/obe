   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  How long have you known the prisoner? A.  Two years—I have known hit parents nearly seven—I sold a clock to them two years ago—he came to my house occasionally—I let him have the clocks for the people, as I thought it was all right—I knew he lived with his father—I have seen him there many times—I have not dined or drank tea there—I have been in the habit of doing business with his father for even years—I never kept my money-drawer locked—I ex pected nobody would come to my room—Hofmire went to the room when ever he liked, and so did Rachael Davies, the landlady—there are no ser vants—I make my own bed—I have two rooms—I keep my parlour-door locked, and put the key in the landlady's kitchen—I had seen the prisoner on the Thursday morning—be never came to my house before Christmas eve, and three times after—his father has had two clocks and a watch since I have known him. JOSEPH HOFMIRE. I am a clock-maker, and live with Ferderer—we occupy the same room to work in, and pay the rent of the front-parlour between us—I have a bed-room up stairs—I was there the second time the prisoner came, when he asked for a Christmas-box, and heard him say be should like the money very much—on the "Thursday I met him at the end of St. Andrew's-row, about half-past four o'clock—he said, "How do you do?"—I said, "Very well; how is it you have been over here?"—he said, "I want to see Mr. Ferderer very particularly, about them  clocks he sold"—I said, "You have been in our place"—he said, "Yes, Mr. Ferderer is not at home, I will go back with you and wait till he comes home"—the lodger up stairs let us in—I went in, fetched the key from the kitchen, and let him into the workshop—I found the door locked—I lighted the fire, and the prisoner sat down close to the prosecutor's drawer—I knew there was money there—I put the kettle on the fire and began working—a woman in the next room called out to me, "I have got a good fire, if you want to put your kettle on"—so I went into the other room and put it on—I then went up stairs, leaving the prisoner in the work-room—I came down in a few minutes, and found the prisoner at the street-door, ready to go out—I said, "Are you not going to stop till Mr. Ferderer comes home?"—he said, "No, I had rather go to a public-house and have a pint of beer and smoke a pipe, and be back in half an hour"—I said, "If you want some beer I will get you some, you can smoke a pipe here, or you can have tea with me"—he said he had rather go to a public-house, and be back in half an hour—he left about five o'clock, and did not return—Ferderer came in about half-past five—in consequence of what I told him he went to his drawer and missed his money—I had seen the paper containing the money that morning, when I took a gimlet out of the drawer, after Ferderer went out—it had the usual appearance of having money in it—when I went out I shut and locked the workshop—there is no door opens from the workshop to the other room—you must go out into the passage—
