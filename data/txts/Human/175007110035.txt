the gentleman asked me if I wanted a careless husband, I told him I could not get one; we went into the prisoner's house and had some punch, he asked for a bed, he gave two shillings, he was not sober, which he owned at his first coming in. After we had been in bed both together for about an hour, my landlady came up and took the breeches from under his head, she pulled out the watch and put it into her bosom; then she pulled out two pieces of gold, and some silver I saw, there was a candle in the room. Said she, if you'll not say any thing of this I'll rub out your score, and buy you a pair of shoes; I said I would not agree to it; she called up another girl, and called for a bottle of wine; she shewed the money to the other girl. In the morning the gentleman put on his waistcoat and other things, he said where are my breeches? then he said, where is my watch? said I, it is all safe, and your money too. I went down and called to the prisoner, she did not come, I called on the stairs again, then she came up and brought the watch in her right hand, and asked if it was his; he asked for the money, she said she knew nothing of it upon her honour. Said he, I had so much money and I lost it in your house; if so, said she, you have given it to my sister, and she is gone out. He went out to the Black Dog and staid there, and came over every now and then. At last she went to Covent Garden market to buy some victuals, and bid me go up and lie down upon the bed, and while I was asleep, the man that goes for her husband came up and awaked me, and asked me for my ring, and bid me go out and stay till every thing was quiet, and then come home. She came home and said, make the best of your way up Drury-lane for the gentleman is gone for a search warrant, and if he should search the house and not find you, we will make oath you was a stranger, and made off with the money. And my landlady was to say if he came to take me up, he wanted to have to do with me in an unhandsome manner. I went directly into St. James's Park, and found an acquaintance, I told him the whole thing; the gentleman was coming by, I was in a surprise; he asked me what was the matter, I told him that was the person; said he, I'll go and speak to him. While he was going after him, I said I would speak to him myself, I'll face him as the d - I did the cat. The gentleman said, if you'll tell me who has got the money I'll make you some recompence; I said my landlady was the person that took it out of his breeches. I went to the justice and took my oath to what I have said now. He was fast asleep when she came up and took it. She took a burning glass out of his pocket, and wanted me to put it in again, and I would not, so she put it in herself.
 Cross examined.
 Q. Did you ever frequent a brandy shop in the Butcher-row, near Temple-bar?
 Ann Hall. No, I never did.
 Q. Was you not there the third of July to change a thirty six shill. piece of gold?
 A. Hall. No, I was not, I never was possessed of a piece of gold of that fort ; I know a guinea, and that is all I know of gold coin.
 Prisoner's defence. I know no more of it than the child unborn. She brought the watch to me and I delivered it to him again in the morning. Because I threatened to trouble her for the money she owes me, she has brought this thing against me.
   Catharine Messinger. I was servant to Mr. Little, a brandy shop in the Butcher-row, by Temple bar, this Ann Hall came to our shop on Friday was seven night betwixt ten and eleven o'clock, and asked for a quartern of gin; there were two women with her, I served her, she wanted me to change a piece of gold, I asked her what it was, she said she could not tell, I took it out of her hand, it was a thirty six shill. piece, the gentlewoman of the shop was asleep and I could not change it, so I returned it again; she offered me another, if I did not like that, and she offered me six-pence to change it; I trusted her for the gin because I could not change it.
   Hannah Cordosa. I know Ann Hall, I saw her last Tuesday was seven nights coming out of this gin shop, it is the Black Dog in Butcher-row, I was going in for a half-penny worth of small beer, she had money in her hand, I saw three broad pieces.
 Q. Where do you live?
 Cordosa. I live as far as Westminster. I had been in the city to buy some thread to work with. I am a sempstress.
 Han. Matthews. I go to the house where she lived, next door to the Greyhound, I help the maid there to do the work, I never saw Ann Hall with any money at all.
   Mary Mitchel. I never saw Ann Hall with any money, I saw her with the watch in the prisoner's house when the gentleman was in the house. She brought it down to the prisoner between three and four o'clock, and desired her to keep it for him. I am a servant in the house.
   Patrick Reynolds. I keep a chandler's shop and sell coals, I have known the prisoner two or three years, she behaved always very honestly, I never heard her charged with theft.
