Court. Q. Surely you did, for you have just said that you made a
memorandum on the draft, because you had been informed that it was on
the morning of the 30th it was executed, the day before Mr. Constable
died ? A. Yes; but they are asking me whether I heard it was a for-
gery before then-- Mr. Hale had not said anything to me about its being
irregular-he had not said anything about it before the new deed was
executed.
Mr. Clarkson. Q. And you had no notice, through Mr. Hale, or any-
body else, that the deed was a forgery? be cautious what you swear,
A. No, not that I recollect, not till about the time I told you; I do
not recollect that it came to my knowledge before the confirmation deed was
executed.
Court. Q. This is a very important fact; nobody could mistake whether
when they were about to get a woman to execute a fresh deed, they had notice
that the former was a forged one? A. No, I had not at the time this was
drawn.
MR. CLARKSON. Q. Do you mean to pledge your oath to that? 4. Yes,
positively - 1 did not know by information from Mr. Hale that there was
anything wrong about the deed till after the deed of confirmation was exe-
cuted- I did not know at the time I took the conveyance to the prisoner and
his sister, that, with the exception of this property, they were paupers ; I did
not inquire-I did not know how they got their living-they were not able
to pay me, except through Newhall, who advanced the money for the purpose
-I do not think we had required to be paid for what we had done before
that time: I will not swear we had not, without referring to my memoran-
dum-I did not at that time know that they were in such very needy cir-
cumstances; I knew the prisoner himself was in difficulties- I knew that he
had been obliged to quit the house at Camberwell, that there were proceed-
ings at law against him, and that there were overdue and unpaid bills against
him; I knew that, at the time of the execution of the deed, but previously I
had known nothing whatever.
Q. Did you, under these circumstances, knowing him to be in difficulties
and necessities, and this woman to be unprotected, and acting for both, think
that he was a fit man to have so important a trust as that reposed in him?
A. If their statements were right, and their figures, she was giving him very
little; there was very little surplus made out.
COURT. Q. Why was she to give him anything? A. There was the
mortgage ; in fact, the property was worth very little; in fact, she knew 80
much about it that she wished to get rid of it; there was the possibility of her
being made a party to a Chancery suit; there was a mortgage of 3,0001. odd,
and a legal debt of 3001. odd, on this very property, and a Chancery
suit going on at the time, and Mr. Kirk had been endeavouring to find her
out to cite her, and make her a party to the suit, which she was desirous of
avoiding; therefore we considered that the prisoner was taking but very little
under this deed.
