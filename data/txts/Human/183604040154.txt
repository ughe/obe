   Q.  How long was it after Mr. Barnes said he had the letters that you gave him the money? A.  About half an hour, to the best of my recollec tion—I gave him the money about twenty minutes after five o'clock, about forty minutes before I went away—I paid him the 8 s . 10 d . after I looked into his hat, directly after I copied the list—about ten minuted after I looked into his hat—I was ten minutes writing the paper—I believe I did not awake him to give him the money—I do not know—he was asleep while I wrote the list, and I instantly gave him the money—he was awake then.
   MR. SHEPHEAD . Take these letters in your hand—look at the stamps on them—compare the stamps on the letter with the stamps on your list, and see if they are the same? A.  Yes—each receiving-office in London has a different stamp.
   MR. PRICE . Q.  You personally expect hereafter to become a Presi dent? A.  If I remain long enough in the office—the presidents have not been called deputies since I have been in the office.
   MR. ADOLPHUS . Q.  Whether the prisoner is convicted, or removed from his office by the post-master, without conviction, will that make any difference to you? A.  Not in salary it will not—if he is removed, it will benefit me afterwards. WASTELL ARROWSMITH. I am a clerk in the Foreign Office at the Post Office. On Friday, the 4th of March, I was on duty at the window in the Foreign Office—I came on duty there about five minutes after six—the prisoner came to me at seven o'clock, and demanded 7 s . 10 d . for some letters which he said were to go to the Inland Office—I gave him that sum—he came about five minutes afterwards, and desired to have 4 d . for two newspapers addressed to Mauritius and Madras—I paid particular attention to what he did and what he demanded that night—I was present at Mr. Peacock's office, on Saturday, the 5th of March, when the prisoner was pre sent—Mr. Peacock asked him whether he had any letters about him—he said, no, he had none—he asked if the letters for which Mr. Young had given him 8 s . 10 d . the night preceding were forwarded—he said, "Yes; undoubtedly"—he was asked if he had any letters in any part of the Post Office, or in any of his drawers—he said, "No."
   Q.  Suppose these letters to have arrived at the office under a mistaken impression that they should come to the Foreign Office, how should they be conveyed to the Inland Office? A.  They would go through the tunnel, according to the usual course of business; but if, instead of sending then through the tunnel, the President comes and asks for money, I am bound to give it to him—it is not the proper practice orfont the President to come himself for the money—he ought to send the letters by a messenger—Mr. Barnes has done it—the messenger should take the letters to the bag clerk to receive the money, and the money and letters are forwarded immediately to the Inland Office by the tunnel.
   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS Q.  Was not that Friday night a foreign post night? A.  Yes. it was not a particularly busy night—I have quite a distinct recollection of it—there were no mails made up in addition to the ordinary mails that night, as far as I can recollect—I cannot swear it, but it can be proved I do not recollect any.
