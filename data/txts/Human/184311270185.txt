GEORGE STEAD, tailor, Magdalen Circus, Bermondsey.  On the night of the 24th of Oct. I was at Mr. Havill's public-house, at Green-bank—I went there between eight o'clock and half-past, and saw the prisoner sitting in the tap-room—I had never seen him before—a person I understood was his wife was with him—they were falling out—he had a baby on his knee, and offered it to the mother—she would not take it, and he put it on his knee again—I walked up to the fire, and a woman I understood to be the mother-in-law called him a Greenacre and murderer, and he called her a w—she then seized a quart pot up to strike him, or throw at him—I took it out of her hand—Havill came in, and turned them out—Spelling also prevented her striking him—after they were put out a woman took the baby from the pri soner, and gave it to a girl fourteen or sixteen years old—the prisoner sat down, and he could not help dropping a tear or something—he was sitting by me—the mother-in-law came in, and made the girl give the child back to the prisoner—he took it, and put it on his knee—Havill came, and turned the mother-in-law and girl out—he then told the prisoner to go out—he went out quietly with the child in his arms, and returned again in about five minutes, and sat down by my side with the baby—he said the child was his, and be would do for it, and with the same breath he said he would be the death of it before the morning—he said; Stood sprinkled on a brick wall would look funny in the morning—I tapped him on the shoulder, told him to take a fool's advice, and not revenge himself on a poor innocent baby—he made no reply—he appeared vexed—he sat a while, then said he would go out—the company in the house said he should not—they gave no reason—he called Mr. Havill as a witness that they would not let him out, and then they let him go out—he went out of the tap-room to the bar—I drank my beer off, and went—the prisoner was standing at the bar—I stood with my face towards the bar—the child was then in the prisoner's arms—he took the child, I cannot say whether he took it by its legs or clothes, as it had long clothes, but he took it, and struck it on the counter of the bar.
   Q.  Did you observe the effect of that blow on the child? A.  It was a blow on the head—he then said, "I have done it; I have left one behind, now take me"—he then went into the tap-room, and sat himself down—I thought he was crying—he said he always stuck to his word—I remained there till the constable came, and Mr. Havill gave him in charge.
