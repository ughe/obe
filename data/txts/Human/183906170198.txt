   Q.  Does he keep any thing else? A.  Allow me to explain—I am his brother-in-law—Mrs. Fiestell is my own sister, unfortunately—I say un fortunately, because he has brought me into so many scrapes—he has brought me here to-day to lose my day's work—I have lost my situation in consequence of coming here—I lived at Stiles's, a ginger beer shop—I suppose I had seen Moss at the coffee-shop three or four times alto gether before I saw him with Lewin Caspar—I never knew him to take coffee but once—I think he came to see Fiestell—he went up stairs—Fiestell was there sometimes—one night I recollect he came late—that was the night of the handkerchief—it was about eleven o'clock—he might be there four or five times—I think he was in Fiestell's company once when he had coffee—I think that was in March.

I am a widow, and sister to Moss, I live at 111, Petti coat-lane. I remember hearing of the robbery which took place on the 25th of March—I was in Mansel-street on Tuesday the 26th of March—I saw Moss there in the evening—I saw him in New-street on the morning of Tuesday, and in Mansel-street in the evening—on the Thursday fol lowing he gave me some directions—I was in Mansel-street on the Thurs day morning—he gave me some directions—in consequence of those direc tions I went to his apartment in Mansel-street, to a little, back-room—I found in that room two parcels wrapped in coarse brown paper tied with packthread—I took them to my own lodging by his direction—I did not take them both at once, they were too heavy—I first took one, then the other—I know a person of the name of Davis, he lived in Coventry-court—I saw him on that day about twelve o'clock—I had some conversation with him, and then he came to me again in the evening—I gave him the parcels, and he tied them one at a time in a handkerchief—he took them one at a time, and then came back afterwards and took the other—I was present at Moss's wedding—Old Caspar was there.
