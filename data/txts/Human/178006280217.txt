 Walking like other foolish spectators? - Yes.
 You saw hundreds and hundreds in the same situation? - Yes.
 Had they sticks in their hands? - Not a stick.
   JOHN PITT sworn.
 I am servant to Mr. Akerman; I saw the prisoner come up to the prison gate just after Mr. Akerman's house was broke open. While the mob were burning the goods he reached his hand over with the key in it, and said, D - n your eyes, here is the key of Newgate, open the door. I refused to open the door. He went down and I did not see him afterwards.
 Jury. Where were the keys kept? - In my master's house.
 Are you certain it was the prisoner? - I am positive to the prisoner.
 Can you guess what time in the evening this was? - I believe between seven and eight o'clock, it was just after they begun burning the goods, but I cannot speak exactly to the time.
 Cross Examination.
 This was a very terrible night? - Very terrible.
 You was of course a good deal alarmed and agitated? - Yes, we were.
 There were a great number of people? - A great number.
 You never saw the prisoner before in your life? - I cannot say that I did.
 A person came up and said here are the keys of Newgate? - The prisoner came up with one single key, the main key of the gate.
 Were these keys thrown out, or how did they get at them? - I cannot say how they were got out of Mr. Akerman's house.
 Did the prisoner do any thing else? - No, he demanded the gates to be open; he said he had got the keys of Newgate and he would have the prisoners out.
 Jury. Were the gates opened afterwards? - Yes, between eleven and twelve o'clock we opened them; the prisoners were all out then. We opened them to get out ourselves.
 - CLARKE, Esq. sworn.
 You are in the commission of the peace? - I am.
 You are one of the magistrates whom the prisoner was brought before to be examined? - I was. The prisoner on the first examination was committed solely on account of what passed respecting his having the keys of Newgate. On his second examination the other matters came out relative to what the prisoner said. As to the keys, as far as my memory will serve, he said, he picked them up. He said when he found them to be keys of consequence, he went to Westminster Bridge and flung them into the Thames. I then mentioned to him that I thought that was acting exceedingly wrong, because the reason he gave for flinging them into the Thames was the very reason why he should have kept them, for he might make the parties they belonged to very uneasy on account of the loss of them. He did not say much to that. I asked him whereabouts he threw them in, and whether he threw them in together or singly; I think he said he threw them through the ballus-trades on the right-hand side into the middle of the stream, and he thought that they fell singly. There was a talk of endeavouring to find them, but I believe they have never been found.
 Mr.  RICHARD AKERMAN sworn.
 How many sets have you of the keys of Newgate? - We generally have two of the main great keys; I kept one of them locked up in a bureau, in my one-pair-of-stairs room.
 PRISONER's DEFENCE.
 Curiosity led me as many other spectators to Newgate, hearing there was a riot there. I found these keys in the street. I did not offer to do any mischief, nor to destroy any thing.
 For the Prisoner.
   MARY CONSORT sworn.
 Do you know the prisoner? - Yes, he lies in the house where I live.
 When did he go out in the morning of the 6th of June? - I cannot say.
