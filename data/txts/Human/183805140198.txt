   Q.  Upon your giving an answer like that I have mentioned to you did not the counsel say, "Up stairs into the room," and did not yon say "Yes?" A.  No—I had been standing a short time in the lobby before they came—we had not stood there long—it might be ten minutes, more less—I am quite sure I did not swear that they came up stairs into the lobby-and I do not know where they came from, any more than seeing them pass me—I never said that I heard Mr. Miller and Mr. De Horsey make speeches that evening—I did not hear them myself, but I not go to a poor man's house to solicit him for his vote, but what he was upbraided for things left unpaid on his former canvass—that was the sum and substance of the matter—I believe that was the first thing I heard—I will swear it was-when Mr. Miller had said that, Mr. Mason said he had expended 900 l . that Mr. Miller had already advanced on electioneering purposes, that was the observation of Mr. Mason in return—Mr. Miller then asked Mr. Mason what he thought of it—Mr. Mason said he should want 700 l . to buy seven-score of burgesses, or else he would lose his election-upon that, Mr. Miller said he would not lose his election for the sake of that sum of money, he should have it—and then he said he was Sorry he entered into the coalition, he said he wished he had not entered into the coalition with De Horsey, or the election would not have cost him so much money—that was the substance of the conversation, and the order in which each of them spoke—I am sure of that—I swore before the Committee of the House of Commons, that Mr. Miller asked Mr. Mason what he had done with the 900 l . already advanced in that election—I swore that, and it is true-what I have now stated is true—Mr. Mason said he had expended 900 l . in election purposes—Mr. Miller asked him what he had done with the 900 l ., and then Mr. Mason said, in reply, that he had expended every farthing in electioneering purposes.
