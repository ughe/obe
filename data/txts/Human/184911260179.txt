179. WILLIAM GIGGS , stealing 36 trusses of hay, value 3 l  .; the goods of John Mason . MR. MELLER  a conducted the Prosecution. THOMAS WELLS. I am in the service of Mr. John Mason, a hay-dealer of Bushy; on 14th Nov. he sent me to London with a load of hay, to sell. I went to Charing Cross—I saw the prisoner there—I had not known him before; he asked if I would sell the hay, and what I wanted for it—I told him three guineas; he said that was too much—I said, "Well, then, 3 l ., and a shilling for myself?"—he said, "Well, I will have it"—I said, "I hope you are not going to take me far?"—he said, "No, only just over the water"—I Said, "There is another thing; I must not go home without the money, my master gave me orders not to go home without it"—he said, "Do you think I mean to do  you out of the money?"—he said he would buy it—he said, "Let us go over and have a glass of gin;" and asked me if I had any halfpence,—I said, "No, only a few, but I have a sixpence"—he said, "You may as well let me have that till the hay is sold, and then I will give it you back again"—I was to have the money as soon as the hay was unloaded—he took me to a butcher's shop at Walworth, where he talked about selling it—ten of the trusses were taken in there, by order of Mr. Williams, the butcher—he bought the hay of the prisoner—the prisoner told me to stop there—no one carried it in—we put the cart up, and put the hay in the loft—he bought a quarter of a load, but there was one truss more put in by mistake, and there was one truss he did not like; it was put down and we put another one up—the prisoner went to get the money, and I was to stop round the corner—the prisoner came back and offered me five shillings—he said, "You had better take that to bind the bargain"—I said, "I cannot take that, I must have it all"—a cowman came along and bought the remainder of the hay of the prisoner, not of me—I do not know what he was to give for it—we went down to his house—I do not know where he lived—I am no scholar—when we got there he helped to take out the remainder of the hay—I asked for the money—the prisoner said he could not pay me—we came along, a little way from the place—there was one man with him; he gave the man a half-sovereign, what change the man gave him I do not know—the prisoner then said I must stop in town all night; he put the horse up, and said he would pay the expense; he said he had to take 13 l . on Friday morning and he would pay my money and the expenses—I was forced to stop, for I could not go away without the money—I followed him very closely—he said, "I wish you would go home."
   Prisoner. Q.  I bought it, at Charing Cross, for 3 l .? A.  Yes; and a shilling for myself—after we got over Westminster-bridge you left me, perhaps half an hour; you and I together unloaded the first load—you took the money for that—when you sold it, I took some of it off and you took some—I knew you had got the money—you asked me if I would take fifty shillings—you never showed it to me—you said you could not pay me until Friday morning—you gave me five shillings for myself, and you gave me the sixpence that you borrowed of me—I did not get half-a-crown of you, you threw down two half-crowns—I slept at the Swan, and came to your house next morning to breakfast—I had nowhere else to go—I was waiting for my money.
