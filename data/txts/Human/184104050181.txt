   COURT to CHARLOTTE COOKS . Q.  What did you mean by representing that you had seen your father but once; that he had called but once, when it now turns out that he is actually living in the house? A.  Be cause he told me not to mention about it; he said, he did not wish the lady to know where he was—he did not tell me not to tell the Court where he lived, but not to tell the prisoner.
   Q.  But when examined on oath, how came you to presume to tell the Court and Jury that you had not seen Mr. Valentine since the day he railed on you, and that you did not know where he lived, when he is living in your own house—how do you reconcile that? A.  I do not know, Sir. NOT GUILTY.

SARAH READ was indicted for stealing, on the   5th of April , 1 shirt, value 4 s  .; and I flannel-shirt, value 2 s  .; the goods of John Matthew and another: and JANE MELON , for feloniously receiving the same, well knowing them to have been stolen; against the Statute, & c. JAMES FREDERICK SYMONS. I am in the service of John and Benjamin Matthew, pawnbrokers, in Liquorpond-street. In the afternoon of the 5th of April, the prisoner Read came to our shop and pawned an apron—about an hour afterwards a policeman came and produced a linen shirt and a flannel shirt, the property of my employers, which I had seen banging up in the shop close by where Read stood, about a minute before she came in—she came alone—I did not miss them till the policeman pro duced them.
   Read.  I found them on Eyre Street-hill, and there is mud on the shirt, where I trod on it. Witness.  I do not observe any mud on it.
   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Did you observe any one watch ing or waiting outside? A.  No. GEORGE WATERLOO HOLLAND( police-constable Q  23.) I took the pri soners into custody—in the station they were both sitting together, shuffling about, and during the time I took Read to be searched by a fe male, one of them, I cannot say which, put this linen shirt on the table—if I have said it was Melon, I cannot stick  to it—there was no one there but the prisoners to put it there—the sergeant on duty asked Melon if it was her property—she said no, she knew nothing about it—I was ordered to take Read to the cell to be searched by a female—on coming back I found this flannel-shirt under the seat on which they were sitting, which put it there, I cannot say—when the sergeant asked Melon if the linen shirt was hers, she said it was not, it was Read's—Read said it was hers—the sergeant asked whether it was marked—Melon said it was her sister's husband's shirt, and Read said it was hers—Read said it was not marked—Melon said it was marked G. B.—the searcher is not here—it was the flannel-shirt that Melon said she knew nothing about. JAMES HAHMAN. I am shopman to Mr. Watt, a pawnbroker, in Ex-mouth-street. In the afternoon of the 5th of April the prisoners came to our shop in company together—Melon offered this linen shirt in pledge, but would not take what I offered on it—I observed a flannel under her arm, rolled up, which I believe was a flannel shirt—I did not look at the linen shirt enough to identify it again. NOT GUILTY.
