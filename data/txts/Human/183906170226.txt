   Q.  Did you see any thing that Alice Abrahams brought to your house that day? A.  No—I did not see the gold melted—I saw it after it was melted—it had been melted in my absence—I had heard of the gold dust rob bery two or three days before—it did not occur to me at that time that that gold was part of the gold stolen—it did at the time I had the summons—I was in the Strand at that time—I do not know where the great mass of the gold was at that time—I never knew my father melt large quantities of gold, and put it into Dean's clothes-box—I never knew where these five masses of gold were—I never saw it till I saw it at Lambeth-street—I did not suppose it was the gold dust until I was summoned to Lambeth-street—it did not strike me as strange getting the notes and handing them over to Mrs. Abrahams—when I came back from the City Mrs. Abrahams was in the drawing-room—I do not know what she did with the Bank-notes and gold—I left the room—Mrs. Abrahams and my father were in the room when I left it.
   Q.  You heard a good deal of this affair while concealed in the Hay-market, I suppose? A.  I did not hear any thing—I only saw Mr. Abrahams one evening—we had money with us to live there—it was my brother's money, not my father's—I never wrote to my father or communi cated with him in any way while I was in the Haymarket—my sister was in the Strand at that time.
   Q.  Had you any manner of doubt, when that gold came into your father's house, that it was part of the stolen gold dust? A.  No; I did not think at all about it—I never knew any body bring such a parcel as that at once and have it melted—I did not see any more brought in that day—I have known persons bring considerable quantities of gold to melt at once—not 100 oz.—perhaps a person in the trade has brought it merely to be melted and have an assay—I did not see this gold—I only saw one bar—I never knew 500 l . worth of gold brought to my father's premises, and left there—I have been living in Coventry-street lately—I eat, drink, and sleep there always—my mother is living in Charles-street—I do not live with her—a servant lives in the new place in Hanway-yard—I have seen the place—it is not fitted up with furnaces—there is no place for them—there are no furnaces in Coventry-street—I have never known people come in and put things into the crucibles immediately—I never knew such a thing before—it is the first thing that ever occurred to my knowledge, with the exception of dealers who come to sell—I mean by dealers, shop-keepers who purchase broken gold and silver—I never knew of any body else—not gentlemen that walk about in the evening—my father melts up watch-cases and seals and things—he is in the habit of doing that—he has melted up such a quantity as would nearly make one of these wedges—I do not think there was such a stock in the shop the day Mrs. Abrahams came as would make up such a mass as that.
   Q.  Therefore if any man has sworn that the quantity of gold then made up was made out of watches and things in the shop, that man is wickedly perjured, is he not? A.  Yes; I should say so.
