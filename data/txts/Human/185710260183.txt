   A Jury, composed half of foreigners, was then sworn, and the prisoner was given in charge, both upon the indictment and Inquisition, for the wilful murder of Robert Henderson Robertson. RICHARD THOMPSON. I am an attorney at law. In 1856, I was acting as attorney for the prisoner—he had not consulted me in July last—I spoke to him—I was written to by Mr. Robertson to see him—I did see him on some business—on 8th July last, I went to the Queen's Prison, with the prisoner and Mr. Gower—I had spoken to De Salvi as to an arrangement being made for the discharge of Mr. Robertson, who was at that time a prisoner in the Queen's Prison—De Salvi was one of his detaining creditors, Mr. Gower was another, and there were others—it must have been about 2 o'clock when we went to the prison—we went to Mr. Robertson's room—he was not there at the time, he came in afterwards—there was Mr. Robert son, the prisoner, Mr. Gower, and myself in the room—a conversation took place between the different parties—Mr. Robertson took his seat near the window, I was on his left hand side, the prisoner was standing about four or five, or six feet in front of Mr. Robertson, and Mr. Gower was standing a little further off—I spoke to Mr. Robertson as to his making some proposal to the prisoner and Mr. Gower for his discharge—Mr. Robertson, addressing himself to Mr. Gower, made an explanation as to a debt of 200 l . to De Salvi, he said, "The 200 l . is owing by a Mr. Brown, it is not my debt"—on that the prisoner said, "I let you have my money, you owe me the money"—Robertson said, "No, Brown had it"—he denied it, and then there was an angry feeling between the prisoner—he seemed vexed at his denying that he had received his 200 l .—the angry feeling was expressed by the prisoner—he said, "You had my money"—I cannot recollect exactly the words used by him, but I saw that he was angry with him for denying owing him the 200 l .—he repeated the denial more than once—after he had so repeated it, De Salvi got nearer to him, and I saw him strike Robertson with his right hand, placing his left hand on his shoulder, and striking with the right—Robertson was sitting in his chair at the time—he struck him on the right side, in the neck—I should think there were four or five blows given—I could not see anything in the prisoner's hand.—after the blows were struck, Mr. Robertson got forward about four feet, and went to the ground—I immediately jumped up from my seat—De Salvi left the room, and I saw that Robertson was bleeding from a cut in the temple, and I im mediately took hold of him, and supported him on the ground—De Salvi left the room immediately after the blows were struck, and Mr. Gower also—I called for assistance—some one came into the room, and I said, "Stop those men"—the deceased was afterwards placed on a bed, and attended by a surgeon.
