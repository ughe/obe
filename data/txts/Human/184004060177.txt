HANNAH PERKINS. I live with the last witness. I went to the King William public-house between eleven and-twelve o'clock, to tell her that supper was ready—I assisted Pearson out of the house—I did not observe the prisoner till she struck Pearson, which she did with a key, and re peated the blow—Thomas got the key from her afterwards—Pearson fell on her back afterwards with the blows the prisoner gave her—the prisoner was still beating her when I and Thomas went and took her from her.
   Cross-examined. Q.  The prisoner struck the blow without saying any thing? A.  She did—it was a violent blow—Thomas took the key after the blow had been repeated twice, as far as I recollect—the prisoner then caught hold of Pearson's hair, dragged her into the second house, knocked her down in the passage, and beat her—I took her from her, and said, " Liverpool Poll,  don't hit her down"—I did not see the prisoner when I went into the public-house, not till I came out with the prosecutrix—I did not see her as I went in at the door—there was a scuffling going down to Tolbot's house—they had no fight, Pearson was too much in liquor to strike at all—she did not lift her hand to the prisoner—Pearson fell twice, the first time was in the road, on her back, and the next time in the pas sage of the house—she did not fall on the curb-stone—I did not hear her say, "Never mind a little blood, it won't hurt me"—I certainly should have heard it if she had said so—I will swear the did not say so—I did not hear it mentioned. GEORGE HALE( police-constable K  54.) I apprehended the prisoner on the 9th—she said it was not done with a key—there were a great many girls there at the time, and they said, "Oh, you did not do it, she did it in falling"—I said, "I think it was not done in falling"—she said, "Yes, it was," that the prosecutrix was very drunk, and did it herself in falling.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you find the prisoner immediately you went after her? A.  No, I did not, because they go from one house to another so—I took her in one of the houses, not the house she lived in. JOHN WILLIAM BIRD. I am a surgeon, and live in Ratcliff-highway. The prosecutrix was brought to me on the night of Wednesday, the 8th, to have her face dressed—her nose was cut across, at least it was divided—the bridge was broken, and her lip divided in two places—they were contused wounds, not such as a person would receive by a fall in the street—there are three distinct wounds—the one across the nose is a transverse one, the other longitudinal, and the other oblique—the cut on the upper lip was oblique and fiat over, so that the force must have been applied upwards, and there is an excoriation on the nose upwards, which would not occur in a fall—it was certainly not done by falling on the ground—this key would produce such appearances—it must have been applied with considerable violence—the wounds correspond with the key, and the key is stained with blood now.
   Cross-examined. Q.  If a person fell against the curb-stone, might not that cut the lip, and break the teeth? A.  Not in that way—such a thing is possible, but the wounds were all in different directions—I was not told what had been the matter when she was brought to me—she and the two young women came, and they were all enraged of course.
