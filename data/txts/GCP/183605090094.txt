Cross-examined. Q. Was it at the foot of the hill you first saw the four
or five teams? A. It was some distance before I came to the brow of the
hill--they were going very slowly along till the driver whipped the horse
-I think that was the second team, but I am not sure I should think
three teams might go a-breast on the road-I did not see any body in the
road-there is a foot-path-I cannot say whether it was in consequence of
the trace becoming loose that the horses started off-the wagon passed the
driver directly the horses started off-he followed it directly the horses
did not stop the other wagons appeared to be loaded with soot.
GEORGE COLLINS. I was servant to Mr. Spelt. On Saturday night, the
9th of April, about ten minutes or a quarter to seven o'clock I was oppo-
site the door-1 saw a wagon coming down the hill some of the horses
were trotting and some were galloping--they went at about six miles an
hour-I saw a person on the top of the wagon-he got from the top on
to the fore-ladder-he then got on the shaft, and made a spring off on to the
ground-I saw him fall—the wheel went over his leg and head and killed
him on the spot-I assisted to carry him across the road to Mr. Spelt's
house I do not know who he is or his name I saw a man run by with
whip in his hand- I cannot say whether he was the driver of the wagon
I cannot say whether it was the prisoner the man I saw was all over black.
Cross-examined. Q. Where did you first catch sight of the wagon?
Nearly at the top of the hill-I observed that the trace was loose-I did
not see the wagon stopped the man would have been safe where he was
had he not attempted to get down.
JAMES Frost. I am a constable of Finchley. I did not see the acci-
dent-I understand George Dawson is the name of the person killed
never saw him before I saw his dead body that evening his father and
brother were at the inquest-the prisoner came in about eight o'clock at
night and wanted to see his boy-I went with him to the place where he
was lying-the instant he saw him he nearly fell into hystericks he was so
hurt at seeing him-he was very much hurt and wanted to go to his master,
Mr. Titmus, at Wellington, in Hertfordshire he said he was the wagoner
belonging to the wagon-be did not tell me the name of the boy who was
killed.
There being no proof of the name of the deceased the prisoner was
ACQUITTED.
