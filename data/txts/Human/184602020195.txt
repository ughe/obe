I am a journeyman gold-beater, and work for Mr. Seymour, at Peckham—on the 21st of Jan. I went with the prisoner to several public-houses, and afterwards went home with him to the factory, about half-past twelve—he said to me, "Will you work all night? I am going to work"—I said I would—we went into the shop, and he said, "I shall melt my stone-dirt"—he melted it, and there was 3dwts. 6 grains—he said, "That won't do, I must have more"—he then went to his box in which his shruf  was kept, took some gold out, weighed it, put it in, and melted it—there was 13 dwts. 7 grains, to the best of my recollection—he said that would do to pay for the funeral of his child, and get his wife a black gown—he told me on the Wednesday that he had asked Mr. Seymour to lend him 2 l ., and he had refused, and he then remarked, "Well, he has refused to lend me the 2 l ., I will have 2 l . worth out of the old b—"—he went to town at four o'clock next day with his wife, and said he was going to sell the gold—I saw him again on Friday morning, and he asked me to go to Nunhead cemetery with him about a piece of ground—at we were coming back he told me he had sold the gold, he had taken it to Mr. Groser first, and he refused to buy it, as it was between the lights, and he said, to the best of my recollec tion, that he had sold it to Mr. Collingridge—I could not swear to the name, but it ended with a ridge , and was in Wilderness-row—he said he got 2 l . 7 s . 6 d . for it—he said to me, "I hope you will keep it dark , for if you come it  I shall get lagged , and I should look pretty in chains"—I did not say anything about it on that account till I was on my oath before the Magistrate.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you worked for a Mr. Laws? A.  Yes—I cannot tell why I left him, it is so long ago—I belive something was said about some silver concealed behind the weights—I was discharged on suspicion of that—I was discharged by Mr. Seymour's son on account of the work not turning out well—I did not take advantage of the prisoner being partially intoxi cated, and melt this gold myself—I swear that—I sat down and smoked my pipe for about an hour, and had some coffee, and then went to bed—the dead child was in the next room—I was not astonished to hear such language as keeping it dark , and bring lagged , because I have heard it often before among gold-beaters—Mr. Seymour took me before the Magistrate—he said, "I want you to speak the truth, and nothing but the truth, and I hope you will answer"—I did not go till I was compelled.
