COURT . Q.  Then what do you mean by his appearance, if you could not see his face? A.  From his height, manner, and actions; seeing him running backwards and forwards from the stable to the tomb—I had observed his style and gait before—I cannot say that there was anything peculiar about it—I was familiar with his mode of moving quickly.
   MR. CLARKSON . Q.  At that time had you any doubt that he was one of the three? A.  No; on seeing this, I immediately went, hurried on what clothes I could, and went down stairs with the intention of going into the street, see whether any of the men went out that way—when I got into the passage, I heard something very heavy put down in Bridgman's passage—I made the remark at the time, that it was very heavy—I heard it distinctly, and called my father's attention to the fact—I proceeded to the outer door, opened it, and heard Mr. Bridgman's door open about the same time—I then observed a man come from the door of Mr. Bridgman's house—there is a wooden screen between the two houses, to prevent any one seeing the entrance in and out of the other—a man came from Mr. Bridgman's house, and went as far as the gate—the screen near the gate is not so high as it is near the door, and I saw him when he got near the gate—he looked over the screen, saw me, returned into the house again, and shut the door—I went into our house again, left the door ajar, and listened for a few minutes—in about five minutes Mr. Bridg man's door was opened again—I went to the door, and saw the man return again—that was certainly not young Bridg man—I heard the man say to the party who let him out, "Good night, ma'am "—(I had observed lights through the fan-light over the door, between the first and second times of the man's coming out)—he then went away, and walked towards Kennington—after he had walked about fifteen yards, he turned his head and saw me, as I had gone into the street, and then he ran as fast as he could—I ran after him for some time, but lost sight of him—he was the man that was dressed in plain black clothes—Mr. Bridgman kept a horse and chaise—whilst the men were at the tomb, I saw the horse come from the stable—I also saw a dog in the chapel-yard, while this was going on—I had seen it about the place before, it was quite quiet, it did not appear to treat the parties as strangers—I was not aware whose dog it was—I had to go to business early next morning—I re turned about half-past seven in the evening, which was half an hour earlier than usual, for the purpose of following out this proceeding—I went to the same window from time to time, for two or three hours, to see if I could observe anybody; and a little before ten I saw two men at Mr. Tawney's tomb—they had a lever—(it appeared to me that the stone top of the tomb had been imperfectly put on the night before)—they used the lever in the same way as on the previous evening, to adjust the tomb-stone—they then came in the direction of Mr. Bridgman's house—young Bridgman was one of those men—I observed, when I saw him at the tomb, that he had a grey coloured coat on, cut in the shape of a shooting-jacket; and when they had completed what they were about at the tomb, and came in the direction of Mr. Bridgman's house, I went to the front of the house, looked through the win dow, and observed this party, dressed in the same way, come from Mr. Bridg man's house—I immediately ran down stairs, and followed him into the Wal worth-road, without losing sight of him—I then passed him twice, and saw his face distinctly—I also heard him talking to somebody, and am sure it was young Bridgman—I then consulted my relatives, and thought it my duty to examine the tomb, to see to whom it belonged; and, on ascertaining, I ex amined the directory, to trace the relatives, which led me to Mr. Tawney's office, to whom I disclosed all I had seen.
