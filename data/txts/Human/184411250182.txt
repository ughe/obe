   Cross-examined. Q.  How many more persons were in the tap-room? A.  Seven or eight—I was not drinking—the prisoner was sitting close by the prosecutrix—they were very confidential together—I did not see anybody pick up any of the money but the prisoner—I had seen him once before—I had never seen the prosecutrix before—I did not pick up any of the money—it was not scattered about the floor, it fell all in a lump—I did not see the prisoner offer the prosecutrix the money, nor hear her say, "Keep it till to-morrow"—the prisoner went out, and the prosecutrix was carried out nearly immediately afterwards—there were seven or eight of us there—we saw the prosecutrix in this condition, but none of us inter fered.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  Who carried the prosecutrix out? A.  A soldier named Ward carried her into the passage—I was lodging there. MARY ANN WARD. I am cook at the Castle—my husband is a gunner in the Artillery. I remember the night of the 19th of Oct.—I saw the prisoner sitting on the bench next the fire-place in the tap-room—Mrs. Arnold was sitting next the fire, and the prisoner was on her right hand—I saw the prisoner with a handful of money, more gold than silver—Mrs. Arnold was the worse for liquor—she was trying to get the gold and silver from the prisoner's hand, and in doing that she rather slipped off her seat, and the rest of the money fell from her lap on the floor—there was more gold than silver—I saw the prisoner pick it up.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What time was this? A.  It was between ten and eleven—I am servant at the Castle—I saw the prosecutrix in that condition, and I saw a large sum of money—I took no notice of it—I cannot say how many persons were there—some coal-porters were there very drunk. MARY ANN WALLHOUSE. I am the wife of Thomas Wallhouse, a pen sioner, living at Woolwich. The prisoner was a tenant of mine, and paid 3 s . 6 d . a week for his room—on Wednesday, the 16th, I lent him a six pence, as he said he was short, and on Friday, the 18th, I lent him another sixpence—he always behaved remarkably well, and paid his way—I am a laundress—if he had been disposed to be dishonest he could have robbed me. JAMES STOCKEY. I am the prisoner's brother. On the Tuesday after Saturday, the 19th of Oct., he gave me sixteen sovereigns and two half-sovereigns to take care of for him—he is a blacksmith in the Arsenal, and his wages are 24 s . 6 d . a week.
   Cross-examined. Q.  I believe he has been in the habit of sometimes depositing money with you? A.  Yes—the largest sum he ever deposited with me at one time before this was 6 l . WILLIAM JAMES. I am a coal porter, and live at Woolwich. On the night of the 19th I was in the Nile tavern, and the prisoner came and left 5 l . with me—he came to me on the Thursday following, and got it back.
   Cross-examined. Q.  How long have you known him? A.  Fifteen or sixteen years—I never heard anything dishonest of him.
