Fraser account on September 14, 1903, when £10 was paid in then only had a credit balance of £17 3s. 9d. When I had the £206 7s. probably H. Smith owed me more. The verification was in Smith's books. On February 19, 1906, in an old letter-book of Smith's is a letter from the "Financial Guardian" to Garnock. The previous letter is April 10, 1905, from Herbert Smith, 27, Sun Street, to Nicol, stating that "ill-health has caused me to give up business." Then follow letters from the "Financial Guardian" "p.p. H. K. Ablett, manager." From July, 1902, to June, 1904, there is £23,800 paid into my account. I have had speculative dealings on the Stock Ex  change, dealings in property and real estate, dealings in foreign stamps, and other things, by which I have made money. I have no account which will show what amount of my income from my dealings in land was during the two years from 1902 to 1904. I can tell you what they were and show you the cheques. I have obtained them on the undertaking of my solicitor to return them to the drawers. In a great number of instances I turned the cheques into notes. I have produced £11,000 worth of such cheques: a great portion of it did not go into my bank, but was turned into notes and paid to Smith or others, have not taken out how much went into my account as the profits of property dealing. It can be done. I dealt in stamps. I dealt with Messrs. Plumridge. I do not remember whether I paid the £160 in notes (produced) to Plumridge for stamps. It is very likely they came out of Alexander Fraser's account; if so I got them from Smith. I could tell pretty well what profit I made from dealing in stamps. As to the remainder of my income in those two years, amounting to £23,000, from dealing and speculating in shares, I have already said you cannot call income what appears on the debit side of the banking account. You must take both sides of the account. I might draw £10,000 for shares to-day and sell them to-morrow for £11,000, and the profit would be only £1,000. You cannot call the £10,000 income. I know of two actions brought against me in 1904, alleging that I ought to pay debts incurred in the name of Herbert Smith. I account for the drop that occurred in my account with the London City and Midland Bank from several thousands to £820 by the fact that the account was not used so much; there was no business doing—bad times. This was just about the time the Herbert Smith business was started, and that explains it; the Stock Exchange trans actions were done through him. I had to pay or receive every fort night. The moment the Stock Exchange dealings are shut down the account loses in volume, because there are no fortnightly pay ments. Markets were bad, and I stopped dealing for the time being.
