 1568.  WILLIAM SIDNEY SMITH was indicted for feloniously assaulting Charles Waters , and with a certain sharp instrument, cutting and maiming him with intent to murder him.
 TWO OTHER COUNTS, stating his intent to be to disable or do him some grievous bodily harm.
 CHARLES WATERS. I  keep the  Three Tuns public-house, Brook-street, Holborn. On the 16th of July, between eight and nine o'clock at night, the prisoner and several others were in my tap-room - several bricklayers and labourers were there - one of them made a complaint - the prisoner at that time had his head down on his hat, apparently asleep; I roused him, and told him a man had lost his handkerchief, and they had seen him take it; I insisted on having it - he said, he had not got it; one of the labourers said, he thought he had given it to another man - I insisted on his leaving - he got further on to the box - I went to turn him out by force, and he struck me. I struck him again - my wife and servants came and moved the table from the box, and I lifted him out of the box - he stood up in the middle of the room; I changed my hand, and caught him by the left hand collar with my right hand, to lead him to the door - he instantly threw himself down on his back, and began hitting me; I stood over him, holding his collar; I did not know that he had a knife, but he stabbed me three times in my arm - people called out, "He is killing you, he is stabbing you;" I then saw the blood running from my arm; I let go of him, and went to the doctor's - he was not sober, but knew what he was about. The Court ruled, that as, if death had ensued, this offence would not have been murder, it did not come within the meaning of the Statute. NOT GUILTY.
 Before Mr. Justice Littledale.
 1560.  EDWARD MASON, WILLIAM CROOK, ANN GABLE, and SAMUEL CROOK; were indicted for stealing, on the 13th of September , at Christ-church , fourteen pieces of manufactured silk, containing in length five hundred yards, value 150 l, the goods of Thomas Waterlow, the younger , and George Sawyer , in the dwelling-house of the said Thomas Waterlow.
 Messrs. ADOLPHUS and LAW conducted the prosecution.
   JOHN CHAPPELL. I am apprentice to Messrs. Waterlow and Sawyer - the prisoner Samuel Crook was also an apprentice, and slept up stairs. On the 12th of September, about seven o'clock at night, he went out, and said he was going for a candlestick; they were kept in an another house; he came home about twenty or ten minutes before ten o'clock. I opened the door to him - he came up stairs, and sat down - he then went down - called the dog down stairs, and went out with it; he stopped out about five minutes - I opened the door to him; I did not see the dog come in with him, for the light went out. I had not seen the dog go out, but I did not see it after he called it down, till half-past eight o'clock the next morning - it then came up the court - it was outside the house. I fastened the shutters before he came in; Beechey was in master's employ, and held me a light while I fastened them. I went up stairs to bed, and fell asleep while Samuel Crook was undressing himself, and cannot say when he came to bed - but when I awoke in the morning he was in bed. It was twenty minutes before seven o'clock when I went down stairs, he was then in bed - I was the first person down stairs. I found the warehouse door wide open - it had been locked the night before, and the key left in a room under where we slept, in a table-drawer near the window. I had put both the warehouse key and the key of the door facing it there - the back door was open - but not the street door. I ran up, and called out "Samuel - all the house is wide open;" he asked if it was true? I said "It was" - and he said he should have all the blame of it. He put on his stockings and trousers, and came down with me. I found the counter-doors open, and a piece of umbrella silk laid on it; the slides of the counter had been broken open - and the desk also. I told him I thought he had taken the dog out, and if he had not taken it out, it would not have been done. The dog used to sleep by the warehouse-door, or up stairs; he said he had not taken it out; that it went down stairs.
 Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS. Q. Whether the dog went out with him or not you cannot tell? A. No; we generally go to bed about ten or eleven o'clock - we both went up to bed together, and he brought the light down - one of us always bring the light down. I was undressed and in bed before he came up again - he sat on the bed, and began to pull off his clothes - we both slept in the same bed. I did not awake till morning, and then got up directly. I found him in bed when I awoke, and had not felt him get out in the night. We work in the bed-room in the day time making silk; any person belonging to the house goes in and out of the room in the day time.
 MR. LAW. Q. Did you hear him call the dog down as he went out? A. Yes; he was not in the habit of calling it down. I generally took the light down, but he said he would take it down that night. I said I would take it; he said No - he would take it. I had it in my hand to take down - he took it out of my hand.
 JURY. Q. Were you going to take it down before he was undressed? A. Yes; he was making the bed, and when the bed is made, the light is taken down. I never knew the dog absent on any other night; I saw it come in next morning at the back door, which was open an hour after I got up. I locked the warehouse door, just at dusk; the dog was in doors then - the key was up stairs.
 MR. PHILLIPS. Q. You found the back door open; the dog might have gone out there in the night if it was open? A. Yes.
