THOMAS GRIGG. I am superintendent of the Dock Company's Police—on 28th September I received information and sent for Sinclair and Bird, and saw them separately—I said to-Sinclair "I learn you were at our warehouse last night," and told him it was a serious matter, and cautioned him—he said "It was arranged with foreman Ryan about a month ago that I, Bird, and another man by the name of Carr should be in the warehouse and make up some bales of wool; I and Carr remained in one night, about the 13th, and made up two bales: on the following night we stopped in again and made up two more, making in all four bales, which on the following day were put into the scale; Ryan told us to mark them A. M. and a diamond No. I to 4, which was done"—Sinclair then said he had received about 30 s . for that transaction, and afterwards received a few shillings at the time, about 10 s . more—he said "Nothing more transpired until the previous day, when Ryan came to Sinclair and said 'You had better stop in again to-night with Bird and Carr and make up four bales more, get them marked A. M. diamond No. 5 to 8,' which we did; the bags were brought to the scale and there they remain ready to be—weighed"—I asked Bird what he had to say, and he said he knew nothing about the wool whatever; that he was working in the wool warehouse on the previous day, and that having left his jacket in the foreman's box he went there to get it, and on his return he found the door locked, and rather than make any bother in reference to his being locked in, he remained there all night; but he admitted having seen Sinclair there about five o'clock in the morning—I afterwards brought them to gether, and read each other's statements so that they knew what each had said—I then sent for Ryan, and told him what Sinclair had said—Ryan said "It is no good denying facts, it is true; I have done it to make too ends meet"—I said "It is a very serious matter, and whatever you
