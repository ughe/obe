HENRY HARLAND. I am fourteen years old. On the night of Sunday, the 5th of July, I went to bed with my brother in my father's house—I went away at four o'clock the next morning—I had been living with Nosworthy and Wells, in Coleman-street, for about seven months—I found the prisoner there—when I had been there about six weeks, he asked whether I had got a pair of trowsers I could let him have—I told him no—he still kept asking me and bothering me, and I took a pair of my own trowsers from home, and sold them to him—he said he would give me 1 s . for them, but he did not—he gave me 4 d .—some time after he asked me for another pair—he said these were almost worn out, he wanted a pair for Sunday—I took a pair of my brother's, and sold them to him for 4 d .—he paid me for them—these tort of things went on several times—I took various articles—I gave him some and kept some—some nights before I left home I saw the prisoner—I bad a sove reign of my master's, and he was afraid I should confess—he advised me to divide the sovereign with him—he said it would be better for me to come away from home, for if Mr. Nosworthy found out about the sovereign, I should be transported—he advised me to go away, and go to sea—he told me to get plenty of things, and plenty of money, and he would try and dispose of the things, and give me some of the money, and keep half of it—it was in consequence of that I took all these things on the Sunday night and ran away—I went to the prisoner's lodging at half-past five in the morning—I found him getting up when I got there—I had got ail these things with me—I gave him some of the oldest things—he went out to work, and alter that hit wife pawned a coat for 14 s .—the prisoner came home, and said there was a piece of work, a policeman was after me, and I had better be off—he advised me to go to Brighton—I carried some of the things away with me—I took the watch and a coat of my brother's—I went to Brighton—I pawned the watch and coat in Ship-street, Brighton.
   COURT . Q.  Do you mean to swear that the prisoner excited you to do all these things? A.  Yes, it was in consequence of his advice I did it. JAMES KERNEY( police-sergeant P  36.) I heard of this robbery, and on, the 11 tb of July I went to the prisoner's lodgings—I told him to be particular in what he said, for I was sure if Harland was not there he knew where he was—He said, "I know nothing about him, I have not seen him for six week"—I found on Henry Harland this powder-flask—he said the prisoner gave it him—the prisoner said he knew nothing about the powder-flask, be never had such a thing in his possession—I told him I would take him into custody on suspicion of receiving this property—he said he knew nothing about it—he told me he gave his wife a coat to pawn, which was pawned at Mr. Telford's for 14 s .—I went there and found this coat—it is the one that John Harland has identified. JOHN HARLAND re-examined.  This is my coat, it is one of the things my brother went off with that morning. MARY ANN ROBINSON. I lived with the prisoner as his wife—I pawned this coat—Henry Harland came and said he had got no money—that his father and mother sent him out to go to sea—he gave me the coat to pawn—I gave him the money and the duplicate—he came twice to our house—he wid he would ask his mother for some old clothes, and I believed it—I took the scarf and the cloak for myself, and said I was very much obliged to him"—I have seen this powder-flask—it belonged to my husband—he gave it to Henry Harland for the sake of his giving me the scarf and cloak. GUILTY. Aged 19.— Confined Eighteen Months .
