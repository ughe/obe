   MR. JONES . Q.  Where do you carry on business? A.  In Brudenell-place, New North-road—this is my first quarter—I have got the lease of the house—I came from Shoreditch—I have been six years in this country—I have known the prisoner three or four years—he has never been in my service—he is no relation of mine—when I met the prosecutor at Dover, I did not tell him it was not safe for him to go to his counting-house—I went to Dover because the prisoner had a letter from the prosecutor to meet him—I paid the expenses—I went down with the prisoner—I said, "I will pay my expenses, but you must pay for yourself "—he said, "Mr. Albert will pay you again,"but he has not—I left them at Dover—I did not hear the prisoner tell the prose cutor it would not be safe to come to London—Valentine said, "What did you write for me to come here? you might as well have left me at home"—I then asked what was the matter, and he said the prisoner had left the office, which he has no business to do—he blowed  him up  for not putting on his best clothes—the prisoner said, "Don't you know I have pledged everything you left at home; but was that sufficient?"—the prosecutor said he should have managed somehow to have kept the office open. FRANCIS HILL. I am an attorney's clerk, and have been an attorney—I have been employed by the prisoner to negociate some misunderstandings between him and the prosecutor—I had instructions to meet the prosecutor at the latter end of Feb. or beginning of March—I went to his lodgings, and saw him—he did not expect me—we had some conversation—he appointed for me to come on the following morning at a quarter before nine o'clock—I went—he said, "What have you to say?"—I said, "I think you had better settle it; you claim money of him, and he does of you; if you will meet him at Oxford-court to-morrow, and give up some letters, all will be settled"—he appointed for me to call again, and I did—he was gone out—I told him the prisoner was willing to waive his claim to his salary, if he would give up his documents and the receipt for his goods.
   MR. JONES . Q.  For whom did you act? A.  As a friend—the prisoner had the goods either in his possession or under his control—he did not tell me he had pawned some—I did not tell the prosecutor he had pawned any—the prosecutor was looking at a list of the articles when I called on Sunday morning—he said, "These are the things that I left"—I said, "You are aware that he parted with some"—he said, "I don't know; all I want is my things back."
   MR. WILDE . Q.  Have you been to the prisoner's lodgings, and seen the clock over the mantel-piece? A.  Yes—the prosecutor has been there—and seen the trowsers and things about.
