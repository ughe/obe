It is possible that the deceased might have been drink ing immediately before his death and his stomach show no indication of it; but not strong spirits—if he had taken beer two hours before, it might have passed away—I think alcohol in a concentrated form would probably have remained in a sufficient quantity for detection—there was no trace of red wine—there was a complete absence of anything in the shape of alcohol, either in colour or smell—if it had been taken on an empty stomach within an hour of death, it might have disappeared; but if taken with food it might be seen—there was a considerable quantity of food; it was all in a pulpy state, nearly half a flint of half-digested food—I think the wound in the neck was probably done when he was standing up, because it went quite transversely from one side of the neck to the other—that would not be necessarily so; that is my opinion—it was more likely to be done while standing; of course, the man might lie on the ground and be stabbed in the neck—there was nothing to indicate with any degree of certainty either way as to any of them—they might all have been produced while lying down or while standing up, assuming that the assailant was the same size and height as he was. PHINEAS PITT LANGFORD. I am M. D. and M. B. C.
