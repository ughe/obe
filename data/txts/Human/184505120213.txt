   Cross-examined by MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  You were utterly surprised at Mrs. Hunt's information? A.  I did not suspect anything of the kind—the prisoner lived with me about eight months—I do not know that he had been a master baker—I have heard say so—I gave him no right at all to sell any bread to Mrs. Hunt. MARTHA HUNT. I am the wife of Edward Hunt. We live in George-street—I have known the prisoner about nine yean—in the beginning of Sept., 1843, he said Mr. Bush allowed him a halfpenny a loaf for the bread he sold, which made it better for Mr. Bush and for him, and he asked us to take bread of him—my husband said it did not matter who he had hit bread of, as he paid for it—we had not dealt with Mr. Bush before, nor did we know him—from that time we dealt with the prisoner—we had sometimes a quartern and a half of bread in a day, some times less, about six or seven loaves in a week—I took more on a Saturday than other days—I always had new bread—he brought the bread in a morning, and on Satur day, between four and five o'clock, he brought a quartern of flour—he continued to supply me from Sept. till the 26th of April following—these are the bills—on the 13th of April here is a charge for a loaf of bread and a quartern of flour—I had them of the prisoner—for about the first three weeks I paid for the bread and things as I had them—the prisoner then wished me to discontinue it—he said it would serve Mr. Bush, and he would have the overplus for himself—from the time I began to deal with him till I ceased, I never saw Mr. Bush—I paid the prisoner on account 1 l ., and 3 s ., and 8 s ., and 3 s . again—last Friday week the prisoner had summoned my husband to the Court of Requests—I had not the money to pay it, and I went to Mr. Bush to see if I could talk to him to inter cede with the prisoner to get time—the prisoner had summoned us in his own name.
   Cross-examined. Q.  There is an execution out against your husband, is there not? A.
