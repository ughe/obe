There was a meeting on March 19, at which a resolution was passed as to bonus, but it was not due until April 19. During the time this transaction was going on the shares would be quoted cum. bonus. Mr. Lever was not right when he worked out the difference of 2 3/8 as £118 15s. It should have been £23 15s., as the shares were in dollars instead of pounds. It was 2 3/8 on 5,000 dollars. Mr. Lever would only be entitled to £23 15s. and £30 deposit, even assuming the £10 on Rio Tintoe ran off. With regard to Mrs. Butcher's case, the shares were quoted ex dividend on February 22. There was an interim dividend on April 3, 3 per  cent. The dividend would be known a long time beforehand. The shares were bought on February 16, at U 7 £, the highest price of the day. In Mrs. Butcher's case the matter ended in dispute, and there was never any sale of the stock. I do not, therefore, know what price to take. The price on March 1 dropped to 173 3/4, on the 2nd they were 174 1/4, and on the 6th 1734. If Mrs. Butcher's transaction had been opened at 177 1/2, the price given in the contract, and suppos ing it was closed on March 1 at the price Ablett contends she had run off at, there would have been nothing owing to her except her deposit. With regard to Mrs. d'Altera, that was a purchase of £1,000 stock at 176 5/8. The contango notes are not in the proper form used on the Stock Exchange. I do not think it is the proper way to reckon, contango; in this particular case it is simply a statement of interest. If the "Financial Guardian" had opened the stock themselves on the Stock Exchange at the price of February 17 (176 5/8) they would, in fact, have had to pay contango. If Mrs. Butcher was to be credited with the dividend, the other people would by entitled to charge in terest in some shape or form.

On the amount of the stock, at a rate; but that is exactly what I complain of, that when the final account is rendered to this lady three dividends have passed, and not one has been credited.  Mr. Leycester contended that the "Financial Guardian" had a right to charge interest in a transaction which was kept open for an in  definite period, where the client was to be credited with dividend on the larger amount supposed to be invested—not in this case neces sarily, but that was the proper way of drawing up the account between the parties. Mr. Justice Lawrence saw he could not see any right accruing to them to make the charge unless they actually made a contract with someone else, and opened the stock in their own name. They must show in the circular some bargain by which a sum was to be paid, in order to entitle them to charge their client with the interest.
 Witness. I should like to point out that the circular deliberately contemplates the purchase of the stock, otherwise, the dividend can never be collected.
