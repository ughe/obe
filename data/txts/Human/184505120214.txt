   MR. CHARNOCK . Q.  Is your husband here? A.  Yes—the floury brought in a bag on Saturdays between four and five o'clock—the bag on left with the flour—the name of William Bush, and a bunch of wheat was on it—a quartern of flour was brought—the prisoner left these bills him self—I saw him write when I gave him the sovereign. WILLIAM MORTON( police-constable P  289.) I took the prisoner into custody on the 29th of April—I told him he was taken on a charge of felony on account of Mr. Bush—he said, "A charge of felony! I will soon settle that."
   Witnesses for the Defence. WILLIAM TROUGHT. I live in East-lane, Walworth, and am a green-grocer—I have known the prisoner for years—he has been a baker—I was a baker in 1843 and 1844—I supplied him with bread from the 11th of Sept. till the 23rd of April, 1844—I allowed him a halfpenny a loaf profit—I have supplied him, according to my recollection, with a couple of French loaves and a quartern of flour a week, which I left at his bout—it was on such terms as would enable him to sell it—it is a general custom of bakers to give their men bread—the prisoner has children that are bakers.
   COURT . Q.  Do you know Mrs. Hunt? A.  No—I have heard she lives in George-street—she did not live nearer to me than to Mr. Bush, but it would be handier for her to get a supply from me—I served two customers in the same street. JOHN STANFIELD. I live in Earl-street, London-road, and am a baker—I have supplied the prisoner as a baker during 1843 and 1844—I have frequently supplied him with bread in my rounds, sometimes four times a week.
   COURT . Q.  What do you mean by your rounds? A.  I mean my bat where I go round to my customers—he was employed within the limits of my rounds—when he was first in the employ, he met me and asked me several times whether I could let him have a few loaves, and I let bin have it at a price to sell it again—he might get a penny a loaf, but always a halfpenny—a halfpenny is what we allow to persons we know nothing of to sell again, but, knowing him, I would willingly allow him a penny a loaf.
   MR. CHARNOCK . Q.  When did this supply take place? A.  It began about this time two years as near as I can guess—I have no books here—I wanted no books, it was ready money—we never book it—I supply customers near to the prosecutor four times a week, on Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday—I met the prisoner several times, and he took a few loaves—I should say he had about two loaves a week. NOT GUILTY.
