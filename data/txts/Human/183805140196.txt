   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  I believe you were one of Mr. Miller's committee? A.  Yes, or Mr. De Horsey's—I have not been an active man in the elections at Newcastle—I have never been a canvasser for Mr. Miller before—I am not aware that one Joseph Bailey went with me—I will not swear he did not—I am not aware whether Sampson Bai ley was with me—I will not swear that he was—I will not swear whether I gave any money to the men or not—that was not the first time I gave money to persons during an election-l am not in the habit of giving money at elections—I have done it perhaps a dozen or more times during the elec tions, in many days, 6 d . at a time—I will swear I have not given a great deal more than 6 d . a time at an election.
   MR. KELLY . Q.  What are you at Newcastle? A.  A silk-manufac turer—I have a house in town—I have never given more than 1 s . at a time.
   MR. CLARKSON addressed the Jury on behalf of the Defendant, and called the following Witnesses for the Defence . SAMUEL PLANT. I am a shoemaker, living at Newcastle-under-lyne. I have resided there a great part of my life—I am a voter for that borough, as a freeman—I know Mr. Miller—I voted for him and Mr. De Horsey at the last election—I also know Mr. William Mason—I remember meet ing him on the first day of the canvassing—I remember going to the castle on the evening of the nomination day, between nine and ten o'clock-Gal limore and Wall were with me—we went up stairs to the first floor, and staid there a short time—I saw Mr. William Mason and Mr. Henry Miller there—I saw them go into a small room—I got dose to the room-door, and I heard something said by the persons inside the room—Mr. Miller made an swer to Mr. Mason, and said he could not think the reason why he was so ill treated on his canvass: that he could not go into a poor man's house but what he was upbraided of things that were left unpaid at the former elections.
