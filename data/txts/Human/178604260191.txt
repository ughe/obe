What was the objection he came to you to remove? - He said there was an objection at the Pay-office.
 Did he say what that objection was? - I do not remember that he did, he came to me to clear up the execution of the will.
 Then did he state to you that the objection was, that it was not a genuine will? - No, he did not.
 How did you know that that was the objection to their paying this man? - I looked into my list of wills, of which I had given intimation at the office, and found this will to be among the number.
 Why, had this will ever come into your consideration before? - I had seen the will.
 Where? - At the Commons.
 How had you seen it there? - I had inspected it, I go regularly once a week to search the whole bundle of seamens wills?
 The ground of your objection was the doubt of this being genuine? - Yes.
 You had given notice of this as one that you had doubted? - Yes.
 Upon what fact had you grounded your suspicion of the authenticity of the will? - On the face of the will.

 Court. I shall be glad to hear the Counsel for the Crown: it seems to me that this charge is for personating  John Murphy, the executor of the will of Banks, in order to obtain the wages; I think you have given sufficient evidence that there was a man of the name of Banks that was entitled to wages, and I think the probate is prima facia, though not conclusive evidence that there was such a will, but the git of the charge, the substance of the indictment is, that the prisoner personated that executor in order to obtain the wages; now that is a fact capable of direct proof, it is not a fact, like others, that must necesrily be collected from circumstances and inferences; the direct and proper proof is to call some of the parties, whose business it was to pay the wages, to prove that he had so done; it therefore seems to me that the circumstance, the accident of not being proved by that evidence which is the best, from the forgetfulness of witnesses, is no reason why the Court should receive evidence, which is not the best; therefore the inclination of my opinion is, that this fact is not sufficiently proved to put the prisoner on his defence.
 Mr. Baldwin. I do not consider this is a matter of law, but as a matter of fact, whether this man did or did not personate  John Murphy, the executor; I submit, that the fact may be proved as incontestibly as if the man himself had been here to say so. My Lord, I say this is the best evidence; when a man robs another, the man that is robbed may prove the fact; but, says he, I do not recollect, and I will not positively swear that is the man; but, my Lord, is not the confession of that man every day made evidence against him; now, here the man produces a probate of this will, wherein John Murphy is made executor; he says, my name is John Murphy; he takes Mr. Harris to the office with him; Mr. Harris does not go in with him, but he comes out and tells him: is not that just as good evidence as if the man was present? But it does not rest here: when he goes to Mr. Knight, in consequence of the order from the Board, he states to Mr. Knight the business that he had been to the Board upon; he states the purpose of demanding these wages, and that before these wages could be paid, he was desired to go to Mr. Knight.
