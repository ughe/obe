I have known the prisoner about three years—we both lived at Mr. Swainson's together, and I have known him since—on the evening of the 10th of March, between six and seven o'clock, I saw him at the corner of Black more-street, where Mr. Swain son lives—he asked me if I had been to Mr. Swainson's lately—I said no, I had not, but I had heard Mr. Swainson had a bad headach, and Mr. Temple was living there—I went on as far as Chancery-lane, and left him in Fleet-street, as the left-hand side of the way—I do not know in what direction he went—I went down the Temple.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What time was it after you left Fleet-street? A.  I cannot say nearer than between six and seven o'clock—we walked dirces—from Blackmore-street, past St. Clement's Church through the Bar—I was with him about ten minutes—he had a female with him—be met her is Drury-lane—he went away with her in Fleet-street, and returned alone to the corner of Chancery-lane—he was alone when I left him. JAMES SELWAY( police-constable F  11.) I took the prisoner into cus tody—he asked what he was taken for—I told him for taking a tab of butter from Mr. Rea—he said he never did.
   MR. DOANE called ELIZABETH COMPTON. I am single. I know the prisoner—on satur day, the 10th of March, I was in his company—I do not know the witness Smith—I did not see him that evening—it could not have been me he saw with the prisoner—I met the prisoner at the corner of Blackmore-stree, by an oil-shop, as I was going to market—I spoke to him as I passed—I went to inquire the price of some meat, and returned and saw him again by the oil-shop—I went home to my mother's, who was ill, and came back again to purchase a neck of mutton, and I saw him again—it was from seven as half-past seven o'clock—I am confident it had struck seven—he had no butter with him when I saw him.
   Q.  If they say at the time you saw him by the oil-shop he was at the prosecutor's, it must be a mistake? A.  It must—I went with him into Drury-lane—he said to me, "Betsy, can you stand the market penny?"—I said, "I am not quite so poor as that"—we went to some wine-vaults is Drury-lane and had half a quartern of gin, for which I paid 2 d .—it was then twenty minutes past seven by the clock there—he had no butter with him up to that time.
   COURT . Q.  What time did you first see him? A.  I saw him from seven to half-past seven o'clock—we went to the gin shop before I took the mutton home—I have no idea How long it would take a person to go from Thames-street to Drury-lane.
   MR. JONES . Q.  Might it be a quarter past seven o'clock when you first saw the prisoner that evening? A.  No, I do not think it was—I have known the prisoner between six and seven years—he is no relation of me, only a neighbour—I had not seen him for a year and a half, I dare say, till I met him on this night—I know it was on the 11th of March I met for ferring back to the four Saturday nights for what I went to bought when I heard of his being taken, I found it was on the very night I brought the mutton that I saw him—he was with me when I went for it. GUILTY. Aged 29.— Confined Twelve Months.
