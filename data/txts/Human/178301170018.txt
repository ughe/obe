I have no other thing to say, I must submit to the laws of my country let it be as it will; but I truly acted in my own defence: if it be innocence to act in my own defence, I am innocent; but if I must die for having defended myself, if it be the will of the court, if it be the will of the jury; I cannot dictate: I am in the hands of my King and country, let them do whatever they think proper with me: but I solemnly protest, that I did not go out of my room to seek to give any offence to that man; but that he came up to me. It appears from the circumstances and from the evidence, that he was the aggressor, if the jury did recollect; they might recollect at least from the evidence, that he came to provoke me, and that I did not go to provoke him.
 Mr. RECORDER then passed Sentence as follows.
 Mr. Macginniss.
 After a trial, conducted with all possible attention, and with every degree of tenderness to the unfortunate situation in which you stand, you have been convicted by a Jury of your country, of a crime which the laws of all civilized countries have thought it necessary to punish with death, it remains only for me to pronounce that sentence which necessarily follows that conviction; the  laws both of God and of man have decreed that whoso sheddeth man's blood by man shall his blood be shed. If there have passed in this instance any circumstances of personal danger or violence, and of that great provocation which will mitigate the degree of your guilt, I hope you will feel the benefit of them in your own reflections; but you have not been able to substantiate them by circumstances in evidence, sufficient to induce a Jury to mitigate or change the denomination of your offence. God forbid, that one word should drop from me, to aggravate the feelings of a gentleman, which must already be insupportably severe! - No occasion has ever occured to me, in which I have found it more painful to discharge a duty sufficiently painful in itself; the character that has to day been given of you, must interest every man of common feeling and humanity respecting your situation: but a jury having pronounced upon the nature of your crime, the sentence of the law must follow. I hope in God that those circumstances which you have stated in your defence, and which, if you had been able to have proved to day, would have changed the description of your crime, are truth: I hope it for your sake, on an account infinitely more important, than even that of the sentence which is now to be pronounced upon you. I trust that the little time which the law allows in a case of this nature, will be employed by you in a manner suitable to the situation in which you stand, in a manner suitable to your education, in a manner suitable to the character you have received: of that you will receive the benefit: and I pray God it may be so effectually employed. It remains only for me to pronounce upon you the sentence of the law, which is, that you Daniel Macginniss be carried from hence to the place from whence you came, and from thence on Monday next to the place of execution, there to be hanged by the neck till you are dead, and your body to be delivered to be dissected and anatomized according to the statute: and may the Lord Almighty have mercy upon your Soul!
