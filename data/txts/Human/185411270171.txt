MARY ANN RUSSELL. I went into Hazle's room on 30th Oct., between 3 and 4 o'clock—I saw the man, Nelson, on the floor, and the prisoner holding him by the hair of his head with his left hand, and a dagger in his right hand—I tapped him on the shoulder, and said, "Bridle your temper, and don't spill blood on a shipmate"—he did not say anything—Nelson was then lying on his back, with his head against the door—I saw blood upon him—I tapped him on the shoulder, and said, "Mate, you are stabbed," and found that my hand was covered with blood—I saw a wound in his neck—I did not see any weapon in the neck—the prisoner had it in his hand—I saw no blow struck—fie was bleeding from the neck—I went down stairs, and met sergeant Davis at the door of the house—I saw no weapon used.
   Cross-examined. Q.  You went before the Coroner? A.  Yes—I was before the Magistrate, but was not examined—both the prisoner and deceased were very drunk indeed—I do not think the prisoner knew what he was doing, for he was very much excited in drink-—he never spoke a word—I had never seen them before. MARY READY. I live at No. 3, Harrow-street, Southwark, in the same house as Hazle. On 30th Oct., I saw the prisoner and two other men there—I did not notice them when they came in, but I saw afterwards that they were drunk—I saw them standing up against the mantel-piece, and I thought they were fighting—I heard the deceased say to the prisoner, "You son of a b—;" (that was after Hazle was gone)—he said he was no son of a b—; he said, "I will fight you; I will fight the best of the two, but don't let the two of you come on me together"—I saw the deceased come up and hit him with his right hand in the left breast, and knocked him on the ground—the deceased afterwards stooped down to hit him in the face, and the prisoner caught hold of him with his right hand and said, "Don't hit me while I am down," he then pulled a dagger out of his left hand pocket, and stabbed him in the neck—I only saw him do it once—I then ran into my own room, rose up the window, and screamed out "Murder!" and "Police!"
   Cross-examined. Q.  Was the deceased using the prisoner with great violence? A.  Yes; and calling him very bad names—he hit him three times before he chucked him up against the bed. WILLIAM DAVIS( police-sergeant, M  15). I was called to the house, and saw the deceased and prisoner there—they were both very drunk—the deceased was bleeding—I asked him what was the matter—he said to Brown, "You should not have stabbed me"—Brown said, "You struck me first"—he said, "I know I struck you first, but you should not have used the knife," or, "dagger"—Wilson gave me this dagger in the prisoner's presence, and said, "This is what he done  it with, policeman"—the deceased gave the name of Charles Nelson—when I first went into the room, I found the prisoner and deceased there, and another man was coming down the stairs.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Are you in the habit of seeing many of these mari ners? A.  Not particularly; I have seen some of them carry a weapon similar to this—it is a dagger, sharp at both sides—they generally carry a knife with a string; and sometimes carry these things at their side. WILLIM TIMEWELL( police-sergeant, M  6). I was at the station when the prisoner was brought there—he was very drunk—I read over the charge to him, but he did not seem to understand it—afterwards when he became sober, I called him out from the cell, took him into a room by himself, and read over the charge to him, and he appeared very much surprised—he said, "I know no more about stabbing him than a child born this day."
