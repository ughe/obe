THOMAS ARNOLD (Police-sergeant, K 32). About half-past 8 on this evening
I was on duty in Wick-lane– I heard a cry of Police," and ran to the spot,
and met the prosecutor-I ran with bim, and overtook the cart in which
were the two prisoners, and a third man was running by the side endeavour-
ing to get into the cart-when I stopped the cart, one of the prisoners, I
believe Blackin, threw an overcoat into the road-Cox said, "Give him his
by coat "-Cox took off the hat which he had outside his wide-awake, and
threw it at the prosecutor-I took them to the station-I found this waist-

Thomas Howes (Police-sergeant, N 46). About half-past 7 on this evening,
I was on duty in Lower Clapton, and heard a cry of Police "-I ran to the
spot, and saw Cox holding the prosecutor by the arm-he was trying to get
him to go back and ride with him; he said he promised to take him to
Shoreditch, and not to leave him halfway at the Fountain the prosecutor
would not go, and asked me to protect him from these men-he said, "I
wish to get away from them;" I said, “Very well, you can get away, and
if you go away they won't follow you now"-be went on his way, and they
turned back-I told them they must go away from him—that was about
200 yards from the public-house I saw Cox back to the public-house, and
when I got there I saw the two other men with him for the first time-
stood about there for more than five miutes, and saw that they appeared to
be settled, and with no intention of following him-I went about fifty yards
further on, and presently I saw the cart going on with the three men they
were all dressed something like they are now-they followed in the direction
which the prosecutor bad taken, and I took a short cut down Clarence-rond
towards Hackney, and thought I should arrive there in time, but I saw no
more of them till at the police-court.
Cox's Defence. The prosecutor's friend hired me to fetch a horse and cart
I ran to Stamford-hill-gate as fast as I could go, and got them out of the
stablo; and when I came back he had hired another man to take his trap,
and gave him the 48. I said, "You have hired some one else;" he said,
“Yes, you won't bave anything to do with it now, you may take your horse
and cart back again." He went into the parlour of the public-house, and
the landlord said he would not draw him unything; he said to me, " That
man will have to pay you 48. for going for the horse and cart." I went into
the parlour, and asked him if he would give me the 4s; he said he was not
going to give any 48. I said, "You will have to pay the 48." I did
not abuse bim; I spoke very civilly. After a great deal of persuasion, a
gentleman said, "You had better pay him;" and at last he pulled out about
ll& in his hand, and the gentleman gave me a half-crown, a shilling and a
sixpence, from it, and said, "Now Cox, you see about getting home and out
of the way, away from him." The prosecutor was very tipsy. On going
outside he said, I have paid you the 46 ; you will have to take me part of
the way home, down to the Fountain at Claptou," I drove him there, and we
all had some beer there, which I paid for. The prosecutor came out of the
bar and ran off, and Rowsell came into the parlour. I asked him where the
other one was gone ; be said, "Oh, he is outside. I went out, and asked
