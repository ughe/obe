innocence of the motive with which he published it, but the test is
this: Was the language used calculated, or was it not, to promote
public disorder or physical force or violence in a matter of State!
And I need hardly say that anything in the way of assassination
would be comprehended in that definition. That is the test; and
that test is not for me or for the prosecution; it is for you, the jury,
to decide, having heard all the circumstances connected with the
(860. In arriving at a decision of this test you are entitled to look
all the circumstances surrounding the publication with the view
of seeing whether the language used is calculated to produce the
results imputed; that is to say, you are entitled to look at the
audience addressed, because language which would be innocuous,
practically speaking, if used to an assembly of professors or divines
might produce a different result if used before an excited audience
of young and uneducated men. You are entitled also to take into
sccount the state of public feeling. Of course, there are times when
sspark will explode a powder magazine; the effect of language may be
very different at one time from what it would be at another. You are
entitled also to take into account the place and the mode of publica
tion. All these matters are surrounding circumstances which a jury
may take into account in solving the test which is for them, whether
the language used is calculated to produce the disorders or crimes
or violence imputed. It is quite true, as the defendant has put
before you, that a prosecution for seditious libel is somewhat of a
rarity. It is a weapon that is not often taken down from the armoury
in which it hangs, but it is & necessary accompaniment to every
civilised Government; it is liable to be abused, and if it is abused
there is one wholesome correotive, and that is a jury of English men
such as you. Having said this much, I should like to say by way
of nomment upon a good deal that has fallen from the defendant in
the speech that he has addressed to you—that the expression of
abstract academic opinion in this country is free. A man may
lawfully express his opinion on any public matter, however distaste-

tory matter, or if he avoids anything that can be characterised
either as a blasphemous or as an obscene libel. Matters of State,
matters of policy, matters even of morals-all these are open to
him. He may state his opinion freely, he may buttress it by argu-
ment, he may try to persuade others to share his views. Courts
sad juries are not the judges in such matters. For instance, if he
thinks that either a despotism, or an oligarchy, or a republic, or
even no Government at all is the best way of conducting human
tairs he is at perfect liberty to say so. He may assail politicians,
