MESSRS. CLARKSON AND PAYNE conducted the Prosecution.
WILLIAM DRUMME. I am fifteen years old. I am in the employ
of William Betts-her son employed me on board her barges-she has
one called the William Henry-Long went on board that barge on
Wednesday, the 11th of March-he did not say any thing to me
it was lying in the road opposite Mr. Nash's, at Pickle Herring-wharf
some clover and straw which belonged to Mr. Vandervorde was in
the barge--Long came on board shortly after me, and took her up to
the Giraffe steamer-about three o'clock in the afternoon we got to the
Giraffe, and took in thirty-nine quarters and four bushels of tares--we
had finished the lading about half-past six-I did not see any thing of
Vandervorde at that time, but soon after I saw him come on board to Long
-he said to Long, "If I come and help you, don't you think we could
get her up there, for the horses have not a bit of straw to lie on"-Long
said, “ Yes, if you come and help me I think we can"-the straw was to
be delivered at St. Mary Overy's dock-I was behind the barge in the
boat-Long brought the barge to Pickle Herring-wharf-(this was on the
Wednesday night)- I went to another part, leaving them both on board,
and they took her up to St. Mary Overy's dock, and left her there to un-
load the straw—that is on the same side of the river as Pickle Herring-
that was about half-past seven o'clock-I came away in the boat-I saw
Long again next day on the barge, about eleven o'clock in the morning,
and the straw was then out of her-I said, “Why don't you bring her off?"
he said, " Because she is aground"-(there was plenty of water then
to have brought her to Mr. Nash's wharf)he then said he was not going
to the wharf, he had to go over the water to fetch some seed-he took her
over at flood in the afternoon-about ten o'clock that night I was on
Pickle Herring-stairs, and I saw Long in the barge--I said, "Why don't
you bring her in ?" he said, “You can see there is such a tide on her
head, I can't get her head in; I must let her lie alongside of the roads"
-when I found he would not bring her in, I got a man in a boat to give
me a cast-off to the barge-Long was then bringing her round to Vander-
vorde's barge--he then went on board of Vandervorde's barge, and took the
chain with him, made her fast to one of the timber-heads of the stern of
Vandervorde's vessel-Long then went on shore, and left me on board the
barge all night-the next morning, about seven o'clock, Long eame again
on board, and he said to me, 4 Jump up, and let us haul the barge a-head,
I ought to have been here before we hauled a-head, and laid alongside
of Vandervorde's barge, and Long told me to hold on, which I did with my
hands—there was a man in a smock-frock on board Vandervorde's barge-
Long said to him, " Come and give us a heave up with this," which was a
sack of tares--the man came and lifted it on to Long's back-he carried
it off the William Henry barge, and put it on to Vandervorde's vessel,
close to the fore hatchway-Long then came back to the William Henry
-when Long hauled her round he went across the other vessels, and went
on board Vandervorde's, vessel again I then saw Vandervorde and him
quite close together, and they appeared to me to be talking--this was after
he put the sack on board—I did not hear what passed between them
Long came on board the William Henry, and took her to Mr. Nash's
wharf-he got there about a quarter to eight o'clock-he then went away
