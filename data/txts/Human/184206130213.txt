   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you known the prisoner? A.  From a child—I never knew him to be quarrelsome. CATHERINE MULLINS. The deceased was my husband—he left home at five o'clock the morning this happened—he was quite well then—I saw him next at half-past eight or nine at night, at his brother's—he was not insensible then—I saw a cut on his head—it was bleeding—he got his speech, and came to his senses on the Thursday following, but lost them again on Friday morning—he died last Wednesday week—his name was John—Mr. Beale the surgeon saw him. WILLIAM WELLER. I am a watchman, at Plaistow. I was at the Coach and Horses there on the 3rd of May, and saw the prisoner and Mullins in the tap-room—Mullins told the prisoner to come out, and he would fight him—I afterwards saw them fighting in the road—I saw the prisoner strike one blow, and the deceased fall.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you see whether the deceased struck too? A.  No—I cannot tell—I think he was provoking the prisoner for about a quarter of an hour in various ways, using very had language to him—he stopped in the house some time after the deceased went out, and I saw the deceased push up the window, still provoking the prisoner—I have known the prisoner from a child, and never saw him in any row  or trouble before—he is no fighter. JOHN EVANS BEALE. I am a surgeon, and live at Plaistow. I saw the deceased first on Monday, the 6th of June—he had then been ill a week—he had symptoms of effusion of blood, or fluid on the brain, pro ducing convulsive spasms and fits—he was sensible, but only at times—when I first saw him, I told him to put out his tongue, which he did—he appeared sensible then, but that morning he had two or three fits—he died on the 8th—I made a post mortem  exam ination—on removing the skull cap, I found lying on the left hemi sphere of the brain, from one to two ounces of coagulated blood, between the pia mater,  and dura mater,  which accounted for his death—he had a small wound at the back of his head, it was nearly well—there was a bit of plaster on it, which I took off—I did not apply anything fur ther—there was not much concussion, and no external swelling—there were evidently several other blows on the back of his head—I apprehend that concussion was the cause of the extravasation of blood—I consider a blow or fall was the cause of it—I do not know that a blow under the left ear would produce it without a fall, but accompanied with a fall it would—such a blow and fall as has been described would be sufficient to account for what I saw, and the man being partially intoxicated.
   Cross-examined. Q.  You could not trace the extravasation to the wound at the back of the head? A.  It led me to know that he had had a severe blow on his head—there was no fracture—the blow was between the hemispheres of the brain—the blood was all in one hemisphere—ex travasation may occur without violence—it is more likely to occur during intoxication—I do not think he could have recovered under any circum stances—the extravasation had most likely existed for some days, but I cannot state positively how long.
 (The prisoner received a good character.) GUILTY. Aged 38.— Strongly recommended to mercy.  — Confined One Week.
