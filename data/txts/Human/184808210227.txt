I hope we shall hare many such numerous meetings as we have now, and as peaceable as the last time we met in this Hall. With these remarks I will sit down, and call upon your old friend and warden, Mr. Keene, to propose the first resolution; and he will also read you a letter from an individual you will be happy to hear would have attended this evening, were it possible; and also, I may state that even the Government hirelings of this country are beginning to use the victims at present in their possession rather harshly; although the Defence-fund has paid for the maintenance of Mr. Vernon in prison, to exempt him from labour, nevertheless he has been forced to pick oakum until his fingers were blistered. This is no flying report; I have had it to-day from the lips of Mrs. Vernon herself; and also I have seen a letter from Dr. M'Douall, which you will soon have the miserable pleasure of reading to  morrow in the 'Star.' That gentleman was thrust into a cell through which the hot water pipes passed, for the purpose of conveying hot water to other apartments of the prison, and there they kept him stewing, I cannot give it any other name; for hoars, until he refused to complain, because it only gratified them; and this gentleman was obliged to take off his coat, waist coat, and trowsers, and stand up in a corner of the cell to keep life, soul, and body together—(Loud cries of shame). That is the state of your Govern ment, and the resolution therefore calls upon you loudly to sympathize, par ticularly with your Irish brethren, who no doubt are suffering similar acts of outrage and violence."
   Q.  How was that speech received? A.  With cheers—a person named Keene then spoke—in the course of his speech he said: "Mitchell saw the misery his country was suffering under; Mitchell knew he must be a sacrifice, but he was willing to be a sacrifice, if by—that sacrifice he could rouse his countrymen to a perfect knowledge of what their rights were"—(three cheers for Mitchell were then given)—the resolution that was proposed by Mr. Keene was, "Resolved, that this meeting views with feeling of distrust and alarm the measure adopted by Her Majesty's Government, and sanctioned by the Im perial Parliament, for the repression of public opinion in Ireland, as tending to the final severance of all amicable relations between the two countries; and this meeting is further of opinion that the Irish people are fully entitled to a free and independent parliament, and therefore resolves to render to their patriotic brethren in Ireland their cordial sympathy." After Keene, a person named Pedley spoke—the following is a passage in his speech: "I believe, Mr. Chairman, the fact is this, that Ireland is in arms; that they are fighting there; I trust they will speedily gain a victory, and God speed them in their cause!"—that was received with cheers—the resolution was then put by the defendant to the meeting, and carried unanimously—Shell then spoke—I took his speech—Mr. Counsell also took it—we each took the meeting throughout—Shell proposed the second resolution—it was this: "Resolved, that this meeting do solemnly protest against the base and unconstitutional act of the British Legislature, as committed by them in the suspendin? of the Habeas Corpus Act in Ireland; and, further, do hereby tender to Feargus O'Connor, Esq., M. P., and to those gentlemen who supported him, our best thanks for their manly and patriotic conduct on that occasion; and, further, that we do hereby solemnly pledge ourselves that we will render in the hour of trial our full aid and co-operation to our Irish brethren"—I did not take that part where the defendant introduced Mr. Rooney to second a resolution—I have Rooney's speech—he said, "Mr. Chairman, Englishmen, and Irish men too, I don't know that I am in my place to-night in seconding this reso lution;
