   Cross-examined. Q.  You told him to do all that he has done? A.  Yes—I gave him directions to give the prisoner the money in the way he has done—I do not recollect that I told him to write the letter, but I told him to call upon the prisoner and renew the connexion—I first heard from him about this some time in the month of Oct.—I cannot say the day—it was the day the prisoner communicated with him—it was between two and three months before the letter was written—I had not an opportunity of having anybody placed to hear any conversation between him and the prisoner, but after the first con versation I felt an anxious wish to bring the prisoner to justice. HENRY BERESFORD. I am an inspector of the C division of police. On the afternoon of the 9th of January I saw the boy Lincoln come out, and the pri soner was standing at the corner of Compton-street—I went on the following evening to the house—the prisoner came in—I searched him, and found on him five shillings and some copper—the next morning he wanted to have some coffee—I said, "No, I must keep this money"—he said, "It is my own money, I brought out six shillings, and I changed one shilling"—when I first seized him I found the money in his pocket—I showed it to Mr. Davis, and he identified four shillings of it as his money—the prisoner had just got to the door, when I seized him—he was not out.
   Cross-examined. Q.  And in fact he had got the money in bis hand? A.  It was in his pocket—he had his hand in his pocket—I took his hand out and put my hand in, and took the money—I cannot say whether his hand was closed—I told him to take his hand out—I watched him all the time—when he received the money from the boy he closed his hand upon it, and put it into his pocket—I found on him the five shillings and 6 1/2 d . in copper.
   MR. DAVIS re-examined.  These are four of the shillings I marked and put into the till with six others, which I have in my pocket. GUILTY  . Aged 30.— Judgment Respited.

 772. AMELIA GILLINGS was indicted for obtaining goods by false pretences. JANE ANN SHEPHERD. I am barmaid at the Dover Castle, in St. George's in the East, which is kept by Mr. Abraham Watson. The prisoner came there on the 6th of Jan., about half-past eight o'clock (I never saw her be fore)—she said, "I want half a pint of gin and a pot of porter for Mr. Mace, next door to the baker's shop"—I served her—she came again, and said she wanted another half-pint of gin and a pot of porter for Mr. Mace, who was out, and as soon as he came in he would send over the money, and she had it—she came again for another half-pint of gin and another pot of beer—I let her have it—I saw Mr. Mace afterwards—the next morning I saw the prisoner come with the empty pots—I asked her how she came to come for the gin and porter for Mr. Mace, when he did not send her—she said she knew Mr. Mace did not send, but a gentleman whom he knew did. JOHN MACE. I am a lighterman, and live at No. 27, Church-row, oppo site the Dover Castle—I deal there for beer—I don't know the prisoner—I did not send her there for gin or beer on the 6th of Jan.
