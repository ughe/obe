I am a fully qualified medical map, of Broadway, Tooting—I first saw the deceased child on February 22nd—it then had symptoms of bronchial pneumonia, and had several sores, due to bad nutrition and neglect, one on the chest, and one on the shoulder—I gave it some medicine, and told her to come the following Monday morning and let me know how it was, and I would go and see it—the ointment ought to have lasted three or four days, I told her to apply it to all the sores—she came again on March 1st, and brought the child, the bronchial pneu monia was better, but the sores were still bad, and in my opinion they had not been dressed at all—I asked her if she had used all the ointment—she said, "No"—I gave her some further remedies, and told her to bring I it again in a day or two—I saw it next on the day of its death, February 20th—she said that the child had a fit about three a.m., and she thought it was going to have another—I told her I would go in the course of the morning, but in half-an-hour some one came, and said that the child was lead—I called about 12.15 and saw it, and saw some bruises on its face—I declined to give a certificate, and communicated with the Coroner's office—I made the post-mortem  examination—the child's weight was only 17 lbs. 14 ozs.—I noticed a bruise on the face, another on the chin, one on the right foot, several others on the body, and both feet were swollen—it had suffered from rickets for more than three months—that might be due to artificial feeding—I think it had been suffering before November 19th—want of care would make the rickets worse—it would be obvious to the mother, as it would get bow legs—I noticed sores on the point of each shoulder, and on the scapular , which I attribute to bad condition of the skin, and the sores to bad nutrition of the skin—there was an abrasion on the side of the head, and considerable bruising all round—it was a severe bruise, and considerable force would be necessary to cause it—there was slight bronchial pneumonia of the right lung, but it was not likely to accelerate death—there were no diseased organs, and nothing whatever to lead me to believe that it was unable to assimulate food—the cause of death was weakness from mal-nutrition, and not being taken out into the air, especially in a ricketty child; and being treated severely, and occasion ally slapped and shut up in a wash-house and coal-cellar would reduce its condition, which was obvious to anybody—it could not possibly have forced its head between the railings of the bedstead because it was in too weak a condition to do so—mal-nutrition, want of air, and inattention on the part of the mother would accelerate death.
   By the COURT . I saw the bed—the injury could be caused by the child putting its head between the rails, but I think it was not strong enough—if it was in a fit it would not shove itself about like that.
