   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Were you hired along with your boat? A.  No, it is not usual to have a waterman in the punts when young gentlemen go fishing—it is occasionally—if the stake which held the end of the boat had been pulled up, the action of the boat might have been to I swing with her bow down the stream—I was examined before the Coroner.
   JURY . Q.  Was the punt in the same place at five o'clock, as where you placed it at nine? A.  The punt was, but the stern-pole was left—the hind pole was about twenty yards from where it had been—the punt had been shifted in the course of the day, but not the poles, and the punt had been replaced in the position it was in before.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  When you saw the Vivid,  she had got rid of her passengers, and was going up by the Duke of Boc cleugh's? A.  Yes, just above the pier—the pole of the punt had been shifted in the course of the morning by the wherry coming alongside—it had broken the rope, and then she swung down the stream—if I had been on board, the steam boats pass so frequently, I should not have looked out for any accident, and I Bright have been doing something to the tackle—if I had seen the Richmond  coming, and had lifted up either of the poles, the punt would have swung round, and cleared the steamer altogether—a man at the wheel of the steamer could see about fifty yards before the vessel's bow—if there was any thing between the stern of the vessel and the paddle-box, I do not believe he could see—I believe that is the reason why a person is on the paddle box to guide him how to steer.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  Was there any thing to prevent a person at the wheel seeing there was room enough on the Surrey side of the steamer? A.  Not that I know of—the stern-pole to which the punt was fixed was removed at the flood tide, about half-past three o'clock—I put it to rights at four, before I left it. CHARLES LANGSTON. I am a constable of Richmond. I know the place pointed out by Howard, where the punt was moored—it is in the parish of Richmond—I measured from the Duke of Buccleugh's grounds on the Surrey side of the river, to a pole pointed out by Howard, and the dis tance was forty-three yards and upwards—I have known that part of the river all my life—I have seen punts moored there repeatedly, and have fished there myself—they are generally moored across the stream, fastened with two poles—I measured the distance from that place to the Pigeon landing-place, and it was one hundred yards and upwards—there is a jetty at the Pigeons, which projects into the river about thirty-four feet—I mes sured it since I gave evidence before the Magistrate—a vessel lying there when it comes in to land passengers would lie outside that thirty four feet.
   Cross-examined by MR. CLARKSON . Q.  What was the length of the Vivid? A.  I do not know, it was a very long boat—I think it was low water the following morning, when I measured.
   MR. BODKIN . Q.  The distance would be the same, whether high or low water? A.  It would.
