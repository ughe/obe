On the night of the 24th of Oct. the prisoner came in about eight o'clock, went into the tap-room, and called for a pint of ale—I afterwards saw some women come to him—I heard the mother-in-law call him a Green acre and a murderer—I saw the wife hit him right and left it hard as she could, abont the face—the child was on his lap at that time—I turned the women out—the mother-in-law called him a Greenacre, a murderer, a man eater, a woman-eater, and another expression almost too bad to mention—every thing she could say against him she did—after I had put the women out, the prisoner remained in the tap-room with the child—I did not see the women again till after the child was killed—about a quarter of an hour after they were gone I said to the prisoner, "You had better leave my place"—he said, "Master, I won't hurt you nor any one else," and went out very quietly indeed with the child for about five minutes, then came back very quietly, went into the tap-room, remained about five or ten minutes, and the people would not let him go out—he applied to me—I was called, and said, "It is all right, you had better let him go out"—he came out of the tap-room in the front of the bar with the child in his arms—I was inside the bar—I had occasion to leave the front of the bar for not a minute, and went behind a curtain which is acrost the bar, sad when I had been there about a minute, I heard a woman scream out that the child was murdered—I went in front of the bar, and the prisoner said, "I told you I should do it; I am always as good as oy word; I shall be hung at the Old Bailey; I hope every one will come and see me hung"—he went into the tap-room, laid himself down on the bench, and began to cry—I went for a policeman—I remained at the tap-room door till the policeman came, and gave him into custody—the counter in front of my bar is a kind of shelf, about eighteen inches wide, made of pewter—I considered him sober when he came into my house—I think I had teen him two days before, he and his wife came together for a pint of ale—I have known him five or six years.
   Cross-examined. Q.  When did you first repeat the expression which you say the prisoner used about being hung at the Old Bailey, &c.? A.  I men tioned it at Union-hall—I think you will find it in my deposition—I will swear I recollected it when I was at Union-hall.
   Q.  Did not be say, "I hare done it, I have done what I said I would, I will go in and sit down, and I will give myself up?" A.  No, not those words exactly—I am positive he said what I have stated—I will swear it—I was sober—I should say a dozen peeple heard him say this, customers—Donovan, Spelling, and Stead are not regular customers. GEORGE TOMPKINS( police-constable M  166.) I went to Mr. Havill's on the 24th of Oct., about half-past nine o'clock at night—I was not sent for—it is on my beat-—I went in, seeing a mob round the door—the prisoner was sitting down, leaning on the table—he was gives into my custody by Havill—I took him to the station—in going along he said to me, "I shall go to the Old Bailey for what I have done"—he was sober, but appeared very much agitated—I took him part of the way, but he got so very violent, I was obliged to call the assistance of another constable—I had hold of him.
   Cross-examined. Q.  He talked extremely loud, did not he? A.  I do not know about talking loud—he plunged himself about, and kicked about, and flung his arms about—he did not kick me, nor tear his hair. MARY ROBINSON.
