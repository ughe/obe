SAMUEL MULLETT was again  indicted for stealing 1 penknife, value 1 s  . 6 d  .; 2 bags, 6 d  .; 1 purse, 1 s  . 6 d  .; 25 sovereigns, 19 half-crowns, 37 shillings, and 20 sixpences; the property of Samuel Smith . HANNAH SMITH. I am the prosecutor's wife, and was found in Drum mond-street with the prisoner—before I left my husband I had some silver, partly new silver, which was kept in my bed-room—part of it accompanied us to Ireland—I fetched the money out of my bed-room about five months before I left home—I said I had no money—the prisoner said, "Yes, you have"—I said, "I have some up stairs, but I cannot let you have it"—he said, "Fetch it down"—I fetched it, and placed it in the bar drawer, telling him at the same time not to touch it for God's sake, for fear Smith should find it out—(I surrendered my person to the prisoner about two years before)—he said, "D—d nonsense"—he took the money way—I do not know what he did with it—to the best of my recollection there was 10 l .; or 11 l .;—I am not certain—he had it in his possession in Ireland in the bag—the bag bore some initials—I cannot say what they were—I went to Drummond-street on the Sunday—my husband turned me out from home—the prisoner had the money in his possession in Drum mond-street—my husband came and found us together—the money was then found in the prisoner's box—I saw it pro duced—the prisoner claimed it—I am sure that was the money I brought down stairs—there were twenty-nine sovereigns in a white purse—the purse was the prisoner's, but the money was mine—he claimed it—he put them in some time before, because I was in the habit of giving him money—he promised if I got a certain quantity of money he would take me to Ireland—he took the silver out of the drawer, and I gave him the gold at home at our house.
   Cross-examined by MR. WILKINS . Q.  Did your husband turn you out of the house? A.  Yes; he told me to go out—he did once beat me—we never had any quarrels until I became acquainted with the prisoner—my husband has frequently beaten me—he never kicked me—I have been living with my sister in the Borough—my husband has not visited me there—he has been to the house—I have seen him, but he has not seen me—he has seen the child—Icame here on my own account—I met Mr. Warboys here, a friend of my husband's—I was not here yesterday, or the day before—I was here the beginning of last week—I came in case I should be wanted—no one told me I might be wanted—I volunteered my evidence here—I said when I was in Dublin that all the property was my own, and the prisoner knew nothing about it, that I was turned out, and sought protection from him, and he gave me that protection, that I sold him the knife found in his pocket for a half-penny, and he had committed no robbery, that it was me who got him into trouble, and I deemed it my duty to try and get him out, but I did it by the prisoner's persuasion—I fetched the silver down because he wished me—my husband allowed me to take it—I am not aware that I stated in Dublin that nothing occurred between me and the prisoner till my husband turned me out—upon my oath I did not say so—I know Mr. Jennings, the attorney—I told him that every shilling of the money I had given to the prisoner, but I said it by the prisoner's persuasion—my sister has been supporting me—she keeps the Black Bull in the Borough—I have not told any one what I had to say to-day—this purse I bought with my own money and gave to the prisoner.
