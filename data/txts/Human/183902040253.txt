   JURY . Q.  Did your brother's legs swell much before his death? A.  No; my father got drunk when he got work, but he would get us in some victuals first, and then go out—he would get us about two quartern loaves, or a quartern and a half, and coals and potatoes, and a bit of dripping—he would then go to the public-house, and come home about eight or nine o'clock, sometimes. FRANCES BANTLIN. I am the wife of William Bantlin, a traveller. I have known the prisoner ten years—we rent an apartment in the house, in Counter-alley, Newington—we moved there on the 19th of November, and the prisoner came to help us move—he then lived in Ann-street—the prisoner asked me, as it was so late that sight, to let him stop there, and he made shift on the floor—the children were afterwards brought there, while I was away, not to the same house as me, but the next house—I believe he gave his children part of what he had himself, as far as laid in his power—I believe he was out of work about the time of the child's death—I do not think he neglected them through getting liquor, but one and another person, who knew him in his trade, I believe, gave him liquor—I do not think it was at his own expense—I do not think, while he was there, he had sufficient to support the children—I have seen him give them food—they never complained to me that he neglected them—I understand that the children's mother, on her death-bed, requested he would not put them in the workhouse—I saw the child the evening before he died, and he looked very bad—(I went in with Mr. Boulter's boy)—I had not seen him for two or three days before—he used to come up, and sit by my fire-side, when the father had not power to get a fire—he had not the hooping-cough, but he had a consumptive cough, and he had the measles—I think he caught cold, for the girl brought him out the day after he had the measles out on him, and I think they struck in—as far as I saw, the prisoner brought them bread and meat, or whatever he could get, and gave them the same as himself—I never saw any neglect—he was particularly kind to the boy—I believe he was out of employ a fortnight or three weeks before the boy's death—I know he had nothing to do in the Christmas week, for my husband made him come and dine with us, and the child also—I advised him to get an order for the parish doctor, who would give him a note to go to the Board for relief—I cannot recollect exactly what he said at that moment, but he said at times that all the doctors in the world could not save him, for he had had a complaint from his infancy—the child had had the small-pox about four months before—I am not at all aware of any in tentional neglect.
