Cross-examined by MR. BALLANTINE . Q.  What was the nature of this scuffling, in your opinion? A.  I think he wished to cohabit with his wife—that appeared to be his endeavour—she never complained of any blow being struck—there was no cry out, nor anything that could lead me to believe any blow had been struck—she seemed to be struggling, and not to be inclined to allow him—she was an obstinate temper, and I could tell she refused his purpose, and he was endeavouring to effect it—this was six weeks after her confinement—I attended her once before when she miscarried—the prisoner is twenty-two years of age—she was always ailing ever since I have known her—he has been a most excellent husband to her as ever lived—I do not think a better man ever lived—I know what a✗ good husband is—I have got a very bad one myself—I go out at a✗ monthly nurse—the prisoner always behaved with kindness to hit wife, and waited on her—at one period he was in very comfortable circum stances—after his marriage his circumstances somewhat declined—when I first went there, till the last two months, during her confinement, she had two feather-beds on her bed, six pillows, two bolsters, and about seves blankets, not on the bed, but under it, and when she was confined she had a hard flock bed and flock mattress, which, she said, frequently made her bones sore—the things were pledged, as they were not so well off—it was in consequence of their reduced circumstances—they had been pledged to procure necessaries for himself and wife—for her comforts, of course, as well as his own—he was too fond of his wife, I am sorry to say, a woman that drank so—a Dr. Fisher came to see her—at the time he came she was not sensible—she was never sensible, during the sixteen days, from the morn ing it happened till she died—I was with her when she died—her husband always behaved kindly to her, as kind as a man could be—at the time she miscarried she was in a state of insensibility very much resembling this state, and the landlady, who is outside, can say the same.
   MR. HAKE . Q.  Have you seen Mr. Brenton since he has been in custody? A.  No, only at Union-hall—I had no conversation with him there—Mr. Brenton was an excellent husband to his wife ever since I knew them—I recollect Mrs. Brenton having a black eye, but it wai not given her by Mr. Brenton, it was by being intoxicated, and that very day I fetched her seven quarterns of gin, and then, before Mr. Brenton came home, I fetched a jug of hot water to bring it up again—she got the black eye by falling down the stairs—he never struck her in his life in my pre sence—on the 3rd of Oct. Mrs. Brenton had made no complaint to me of any sore on the lower part of her back.
