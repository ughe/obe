JOHN VORLEY. I live in John's-court. The prisoner came to me with some leather—I do not recollect when—it was a wet night—he wanted to know "whether I would buy it—I said, "No," they were of no use to me—they were not worth more than 9 d .—he took them away, came again in about ten minutes, and said, "I suppose I must have it, for they have been in the box long enough, and we want something for supper"—I went to my leather-cutter, Mr. Penton, and sold them for 1 s .—I got 3 d . on them—I considered the pri soner an honest young man—I knew him as a customer—in fact, if he had come to me, I would have given him employment—they are what they call cuttings, the fragments that are cut off chairs—you can buy it at 20 d . per lb., and I had given after the rate of 2 s . SAMUEL CHESTER( police-constable D  103.) I found the prisoner at the corner of Henrietta-street, St. James's-street—I told him Mr. Palmer gave him into custody—I said, "I suppose you know all about it?"—he said, "Yes, his father spoke to me about it yesterday"—I took him to the station—Mr. Vorley came to me with the leather.
