Cross-examined by MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  Is 1838 the last time you speak of Stanley, within your knowledge? A.  It is—he did not go about with ragged clothes like a beggar, but his children and his wife did—he generally went out decently dressed, like a respectable person.
   MR. CLARKSON . Q.  Had you any idea how he got his living at that time? A.  He told us he was a wool-stapler.
 ----  YACKNEY . I am an engineering surveyor, and live in London-road, Southwark. I knew the late Joseph Stanley from eighteen to twenty years—in 1836, I dined with him at one of his son's, Mr. John Stanley, in Her cules-buildings, Lambeth—I knew him, and saw him in the years 1835, 1836, 1837, and 1838—I think I saw him up to about the latter part of Sept, 1838—he did not appear to be a person of property, on the contrary—the first time I met with him he appeared very needy, and said he was travel ling, selling cigars and tea by commission—I should say his circumstances were very indifferent—there was every appearance of poverty.
   Q.  Did you ever know him to be in a condition to lend 800 l . or 1000 l . A.  I should rather question such a thing, certainly—I should say certainly not.
   Cross-examined by MR. CHAMBERS . Q.  What period of 1838 do you speak of? A.  That was the last time I saw him—it was in the City, at the latter part of Sept., I should say about the 20th—I never had any dealings or transactions with him, beyond sometimes giving him a glass of stout, and a sandwich—that was the last refreshment we had together—I never let him go by without speaking, because I was always soliciting him to do something for his son—he was engaged in Chancery matters at that time. EDMUND MESSENGER. I am a tailor, and live at Camberwell. I know the prisoner, and have often seen him write—the signature, "F. C. B.    Cross-examined by MR. BALLANTINE . Q.  How long have you known him? A.  From the year 1839—I did not live with him then—he was intro duced to me by a person named Smith—I was arrested for debt in 1840—the prisoner did not become bail for me on that occasion—I drew a bill on him about that time, for money which he owed me for clothes, and it was never paid—I went to live with him at Croydon, and assisted him in his shop there—that was two years after I drew the bill on him—he has never paid me the money, I could not receive it, for when I failed I made an assignment of my property—he did not make any charge against me after I had been with him about three months—he did not give me into custody—he did not charge me with robbing him of 4 l . 16 s ., nor did he give me into custody in the presence of one Thomas Clack—that I swear—he never threatened to give me into custody—he said there was a balance of 4 l . 16 s . or 4 l . 18 s . due, and we proved afterwards that there was no deficiency whatever—he did not, to my knowledge, say that I had appropriated it to my own use—he did not threaten to give me into custody on account of that deficiency, or any thing to that effect, to my knowledge—this is the very first I ever heard of it—he did not charge me with embezzlement—I did not leave immediately after that—I remained two or three months after he mentioned about the 4 l . 16 s .—I assisted him when I was in business—I have often seen him write.
