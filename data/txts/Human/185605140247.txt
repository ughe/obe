Cross-examined. Q.  Were the prisoners drunk? A.  Quaid was not very drunk, Peters was rather drunk, but he was sober enough to say, "It is mine," when he had got the staff. GEORGE TAYLOR. I am a waterman at the cab  stand in Moorgate-street. I was there on 10th March, in the evening—I saw the prisoners coming towards Moorgate-street, and Valler was standing on the pavement—Peters put his hand round his neck, and slapped his face—Valler came I beck to speak to them, and Quaid shoved  Peters on one side, and struck Valler—there was a struggle, and they both fell on the pavement—I did not see much more—I saw Peters interfere, and they all went on the pave ment, and one of the bystanders pulled Peters from him. JOHN MILLER. ( City policeman , 99). I was in Moorgate-street, and saw Ginn struggling with the two prisoners—I took Quaid into custody—he kicked me a violent kick on the leg immediately I laid hold of him—we struggled violently together, and got on the pavement; he got up again—I got assistance and got him to the station—both the prisoners were very violent indeed. EDWARD MITCHELL. ( City policeman , 126). I was called to assist Ginn—he was very much exhausted, and had lost his hat in the struggle—I assisted in taking Peters—he was very violent indeed, I had to strap his legs with my belt, and while doing so he kicked me on the head—it took four men to take him. CHARLES SWALET SMITH. I am assistant to Mr. Child, the surgeon to the police. I first saw Ginn on the night that this occurred, he was in bed, and his legs were in a very inflamed state—he had been kicked very much about the shins, and was suffering acute pain, so much so that he could not bear the clothes on his legs—the skin was off in several parts—he has been prevented from going on duty till now, and will be for some time.
 (  The prisoners received good characters. )
