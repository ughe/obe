   Q.  Was it said for what purpose he should leave the coach? A.  Young Caspar said some bills were to be printed on the following morning about this matter, offering a reward of 500 l .—he said Mr. Allen and Mr. De Mole were to have them printed—I said I should like to see Mr. De Mole and Mr. Allen before they were printed, and upon that Lewin Caspar told his father to go to Mr. De Mole about it—it was for that reason he left the coach—Lewin Caspar only went with me to the steam-wharf that day to take inquiries—in the evening he went with me to St. Paul's coffee-house, to inquire if any person had come by the Falmouth mail—I had been there before in the early part of the day—we went to see if a person had come, answering the description Lewin Caspar gave of the man, sixty years of age, with grey hair and whiskers—we heard of a person who appeared to answer the description—Lewin Caspar said he thought the description given by the proprietor of the coffee-house, resembled the person very much—he described the person as having grey hair, and about the height and age—there were one or two waiters, and the landlord—three persons had arrived that morning from Falmouth, and this descrip tion was given of one of them—Lewin Caspar said it appeared to corre spond—he said nothing to me about that house at that time; but a day or two after he said he thought I could not do better than stick to the per sons at that coffee-house—I saw him on two or three occasions after, and he asked How I was getting on—three or four days after, I succeeded in tracing the cabs  to New-street, and was led to a house in which the wit ness Moss was stated to have resided—I think it was No. 1—it was the first house on the left side—I have never been there with Moss—I saw
