 COURT. He did not mention that you were to take part in it - A. Not at the time. I said, what is it? He said, next Monday they were to choose assignees, and they wanted to have their own friends chosen for assignees. Mr. Grick was then sitting in my room, by the fire side. Mr. Grick was in the room before Nugent and Folkard came. Nugent said, do you think the young man will go. That was Grick, to go and prove under the commission. I said, I did not know. Mr. Nugent then said, do you think the man of the house will do it; I said, I did not know; you had better ask him; you had better ask him yourself. Nugent then explained the business to Grick. Grick said he would do it if there was no harm in it. I went down and asked Mrs. Bond. Mr. Nugent said, he would have a dinner and a one-pound note. Mrs. Bond said, if it was to hail any body her husband should not go. The way it was to be done Mr. Folkard was to give bills.
 Q. Was that the way Folkard or Nugent explained it - A. They both explained it was to be done that way. Mr. Folkard was to give bills to Grick and Bond.
 Q. What were these bills to be for - A. That Mr. Folkard owed Grick and Bond so much money, to go and prove them at Guildhall on the Monday.
 Q. Had Nugent said, at that time, whether he had applied to some of his own friends - A. Yes; he said he had some of his own friends; he did not say how many; but he wanted five or six more.
 Q. What were you to have - A. I was to have a one-pound note and a black set of ornaments, and I was to persuade Grick and Bond to do this, and get any body else I could persuade to go and swear a debt.
 Q. On the next day did you see Nugent again - A. Nugent went, and said, he would come the next day. That was Friday. On the Friday he came, and brought  William Folkard with him. I had never spoken to William Folkard in my life. I had seen him at a trial, in a Court. Nugent introduced him as usual; here is a friend of mine, who is a brother to the gentleman that was here before. He said, he is a very good friend to prisoners; his name was Folkard. I asked William Folkard if he had had brought the ear-rings. He said, I should have them; his brother and Nugent were to come the next day, and to bring them in his pocket, with the bills. I told Nugent I should not be at home on the Saturday. Then he said he would call on the Sunday. On the Sunday afternoon I, Mr. Bond, Mrs. Bond, and Mr. Grick, we all went out to avoid them.
 Q. When you came home in the evening did you learn that any body had been looking after you - A. Yes, on the evening, when we returned about eleven o'clock I received that note.
 Q. Whose hand-writing is that note - A. Mr. Nugent's. (Read.)

 Q.Shortly after you had been at home did Nugent and Folkard come to your house - A. After the little girl gave me the note I opened it, and read it, and gave it to Mr. Bond, and while I was sitting there Nugent and Folkard came into the house, and went up into my room. Nugent said, how do you do, and put a bit of paper into my hand. That was a one-pound note. I told him I did not think that Bond and Grick wished to go to-morrow to Guildhall. Nugent said, let me speak to them. Bond and Grick came up stairs. He began explaining the business to Bond and Grick in my hearing. He said, it was nothing but just to go to Guildhall, and to say that Mr.  John Folkard owed him so much money. They sent out for something to drink. William Folkard took his pocket-book out, and took some stamp bills out. He wrote on paper for how much the bills should be drawn.
 Q. Did Nugent take the bills up and look at them. Do you recollect who it was that wrote the bills on the stamp paper - A. Mr. Nugent. I will not be sure which it was. Nugent looked at it. When the bills were wrote out, he said, Bill is it all right.  William Folkard said, what? Nugent said, are they old stamps. William Folkard said, yes, he had taken care of that. I asked him, why they ought to be old. Nugent said, it would not be right to have their bills drawn upon a new stamp at such a time, as the Stamp-office gentlemen altered. their marks every month.
 Q. Was there any bill drawn upon Bond or Grick - A. One was filled for Bond and one for Grick.
 Q. Were there any that were not filled up given to you - A. Yes, three stamp bills; they were accepted by Folkard, in blanks. I was to try to get some of my acquaintance to fill them up, and come to Guildhall on Monday to prove them.
 Q. Were you acquainted with a painter and glazier in Bloomsbury - A. Yes, a Mr. Priest, in High-street, Bloomsbury. I was to make him come and prove on Monday. I applied to Mr. Priest. I was desired to apply to him. He was to prove to the amount of two hundred and fifty pounds. I went to Mr. Priest, and shewed him the black acceptance, and told him he was to go and prove on Monday morning. He refused to do it.
 Q. After they left you when did they call again - A. About eleven o'clock on the Monday they called again. Nugent called in an hackney coach, and  William Folkard with him. Nugent said, he would take us in the coach to the Butler's Head in Bell-alley, Coleman-street. We were to meet John, and then, at twelve o'clock, to meet the creditors. We were to meet John Folkard, to get what was promised, the one-pound note and the ear-rings, and such things. I told him I would not go with him, but I would go afterwards. I went there with Grick.
