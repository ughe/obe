ELEANOR GIRLING. I am the wife of William Girling. I remember the prisoner coming home on Thursday—he has two children—the eldest little girl was in my room when he came home—he spoke from one room to the other to the child, and asked her what business she had with me—I said I took her in out of good nature, as he had left her with a fire in the room—I afterwards went into the room when my husband was there—I began to remove a chair or two from the room—when I came back, I laid my hand on a tea-board—I did not see the prisoner come towards me, but I heard my husband say, "Don't strike my wife!"—I saw my husband standing still—the prisoner took up a hammer, and struck him one blow—I screamed and threw my arms round my husband's neck—he seemed to reel, and put his hand to his head—he did not fall—the prisoner stood still—I took up a little chair, and tried to strike him with it—he threw up the window, and told the people outside what rent he owed—he remained abont the place a little while, and then went away—he said he would not strike me—I could not see whether my husband's head bled, for my sight seemed to leave me, I was so frightened. GEORGE WESTON( policeman ,) I was sent for to Mr. Dry's about a quarter past two o'clock—I found him dressing Girling's head, which was bleeding very much—I went home with him—I was shown into a shoe maker's room by his wife, and found this hammer up in one corner of the front parlour—there were four or five hairs on the sharp part of it—there was a quantity of blood about the room—the prisoner was not there. HENRY DRY. I am a surgeon. Girling came to me about two o'clock in the afternoon—he had two incised wounds in the head, over the left eye—the sharp edge of this hammer might have inflicted them—a branch of the temporal artery was severed by it, and a considerable quantity of blood lost—he has been under my care ever since, and is not well yet—I think he will do well—it is impossible to say whether he may not suffer permanently from it—the wound is of that character that weeks and months might elapse before any symptom might present itself—I do not think him out of danger—when before the Magistrate, I called it a dangerous wound—it must have been inflicted by two blows, for the upper wound is longitudinal, and the other transverse. WILLIAM MORTON( policeman. ) I apprehended the prisoner in a little street leading into Suffolk-street, Borough, on the 27th of March, about half-past nine o'clock in the evening—I told him the charge against him was for assaulting Mr. Girling—he said, "Assaulting! what, in my own place?"—I said, "Yes"—on going to the station, he said, "They cannot hang me up by the heels, and what they give me I shall be able to bear"—next
