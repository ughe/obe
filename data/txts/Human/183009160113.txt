 Cross-examined. Q. Can you, as a medical man, venture to swear what was the cause of death without opening the body? A. I have said I could not - I can only speculate on the extent of the internal injury; I have known the spleen ruptured from the blow without any external marks- Mr. Stirling was the Coroner.
 Q. Did you not state this to the Coroner? A. He asked if it was absolutely necessary to open the body - I said it was necessary, to examine the exact nature of the accident, but not absolutely necessary to enable me to say he died in consequence of the accident; I have seen one person who burst a blood vessel, without any previous appearance of disease, and which occasioned death, but that was not the case in this instance; I have read and heard of such instances - I once saw a case in which the spleen was ruptured, without any visible external appearance; there are some internal injuries which produce no external marks.
 COURT. Q. When had you attended this man before? A. Upwards of twelve months since; he had taken no medicine since as I am told - he was suffering from asthma- that would not cause sudden dissolution.
 The prisoner in his Defence, stated, that seeing the deceased near the wheel he had called out to him, and pulled up immediately, but not in time to prevent the accident, and that he had paid him every attention in his power, and carried him home - that Vernon had abused him very much at the deceased's house, and had taken great pains to injure him.
   BENJAMIN CULVER. I am a labourer, and live in Manor-street. I was at my door on the night in question, and heard some one call out - after that I heard a dismal groan; I went, and saw the cart standing still - this was within half a minute of my hearing the call; I saw two persons get out of the cart immediately, and assist the deceased off the ground - I heard one of those persons say, "Send for a doctor;" I afterwards ascertained that to be the prisoner - I saw him afterwards, and was satisfied he was the person; there was not the slightest appearance of the prisoner's being drunk - he rendered the man every assistance; I did not see Bennet for five minutes after the accident - he appeared quite drunk; the deceased had then gone home.
 COURT. Q. What is your business? A. I am a gardener; my employers live at Battersea - my brother is a market-gardener there.
   GEORGE LOSCOMB. I am pot-boy at the General Hill public-house, Chelsea. I was passing with my beer about a dozen yards from where the accident happened, and saw the prisoner and another man helping the deceased off the ground - I assisted also: the prisoner helped him all the way home, took him up stairs, and treated him with kindness - I am sure he knocked nobody down; I did not know the prisoner before.
   GEORGE TAYLOR. I am a painter. The prisoner was a stranger to me; I heard the horse and cart going along - it did not appear going fast; I am not a judge of such things, but suppose six or seven miles an hour - it was very dark indeed; I heard the empty casks rattle, which made a great noise - the prisoner and Loscomb both assisted the poor man home; I went all the way - he treated him with great kindness, and was the instigator of the doctor being sent for; there was not the slightest appearance of drunkenness about him - he remained in the room a full hour and held the candle; he appeared very sorry indeed.
   WILLIAM BOLTON. The deceased lodged in my house; Drury and these two men led him home - Drury staid in the room more than an hour and a half, and appeared to treat him with great kindness; he did not appear intoxicated in the least.
   SOPHIA LOVE. I am the deceased's widow. The prisoner assisted him home, and remained in the room nearly an hour and a half - he treated him with kindness and concern, till he was disturbed; my husband in his dying moments said he believed it was an accident - he was afflicted with deafness; he was perfectly sensible he should not get over it - he told me he was like a child when the noise was, and the horse was against his shoulder; instead of running out of the way he most likely ran into it - this was the day before he died; he might have been called to in the road, and not hear.
     NOT GUILTY.
 It was stated by the widow after the verdict, that she had expressed to the Coroner her desire that the body should not be opened if it could he avoided.

 1718.  WILLIAM BRITTON was indicted for feloniously receiving, on the 7th of June , 1 watch, value 22l.; 2 seals, value 5l.; 1 key, value 2d.; 1 ring, value 20s.; 1 watch-ribbon, value 9d., and 1 watch-guard, value 1s., the goods of George Toth , he well knowing the same to have been feloniously stolen.
   GEORGE TOTH. I am a furrier, and live in Wilderness-row, Goswell-street. On the 27th of June, about a quarter to nine o'clock in the morning, I went out, leaving this watch and appendages hanging on a nail - I returned at ten, and it was gone; I had left Charles Wells in the house - he was convicted last Session of the robbery; after his conviction he gave me information, and I went to the prisoner's house in Field-lane; I saw him and said, "Are you a pieman?" he said Yes; I said, "Are you the man who bought a gold watch and two gold seals?" he said,"No, I am a poor man; how could I buy such property? I have not 3s. in my pocket" - I said, "If you are the man, to avoid the trouble of prosecution I would rather give you the money which you gave for the watch;" he said he knew nothing of it; I went home, and in about two days I saw him again in Holborn - Richard Wells, my servant's brother, was with me, and in consequence of what he said, I said to the prisoner, "Will you tell me were the watch is? or I'll call an officer;" he said he knew nothing of it - I again said if he did not tell me I would have him taken up - he said, "Well, come home with me, and I will speak with you;" I did not go with him, for there was a row in the street - the people began to push me about; I called an officer, and gave him in charge - I have never seen the watch in his possession,
