MR. BODKIN conducted the Prosecution. JOSEPH HILL GREGORY. I am gardener to Mr. James Farquhar of Charlton. On his premises there is a tool-house, where apples and things of that sort are kept—it is within the fence that encloses the ground—you can go to it from the house without going outside the fence—on Saturday the 26th of November, I fastened it myself, about five o'clock in the evening—the apples, walnuts, and baskets were all safe—I went the following morning about ten o'clock, and found some persons had entered by taking a pane of glass out of the casement, that would enable them to force back the lock of the door—it appeared that that had been done—I missed from seven to nine bushels of apples, two sacks, some seed peas, some walnuts, and a gathering basket—these apples are the same kind that were lost—I know this sack by the particular way in which the mouth is torn—I had used it a day or two before—there is the other sack—I know it by being a very large one—I am certain it is the same—the apples correspond with what they left—I have got a sample of them.
   Cross-examined by MR. DOANE . Q.  Are these sacks all alike? A.  Two of them I know nothing of, the other two are not similar, they have no marks of my master's name on them—there was some long name on one of them—my master's sacks were in my keeping—I only swear to the one with the tear, that is the only reason—I have never seen sacks torn, in that way.
   MR. BODKIN . Q.  You had been using this sack a day or two before? A.  Yes, and one that lost, which had a wide mouth—I believe this is the same—it was shown to me on the Monday. JAMES WILD( police-constable R  141.) I went to the prosecutor's pre mises, and I saw the way the tool-house had been entered—I observed foot marks, and covered them over—they proceeded towards the place that had been entered, and towards a wood—they were plainly visible, and I covered them over—I afterwards went to the wood in company with the gardener, and found these two sacks of apples—they ware concealed in the wood, and covered over with the branches—there was a gathering-basket close to the sacks—I remained there from half-past five o'clock till about a quarter-past ten o'clock at night, when the prisoner and another man came—it was a very moonlight night—I had an opportunity of seeing the men—I am certain the prisoner is one—I and the other offered laid down, and the two men took each a sack on their backs—we got up, they ran away, and threw down their sacks of apples—we heard them fall—they made their escape—I took the basket, and left the other officer in charge of the apples—I went along the Woolwich-road towards the turnpike, and coming back I met the other man who had been with the priso ner, coming out of a cottage where the prisoner lived—in consequence of what he said I went towards the White Horse, Charlton—I followed him, and just as we got between there and Lord Howick's I saw the prisoner and the other man coming along the foot-path together—that man went to meet the prisoner, and they returned together—five or six yards before they came to me the prisoner, left him, and went to the other side of the road—I bid them good night, and walked on—I then returned to the turn pike-gate and met a young man who went with me into the wood—we were going to where I had left Smith—I heard two men talking in the garden of a cottage adjoining the wood—Smith held up his cutlass, and said, "Be quite"—the tall man that made his escape, came up a ladder, and put a dog
