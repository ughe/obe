Cross-examined. I knew Gibbs was an old employee of the bank
rupt's father, who had started in business for himself and was in
dcbted to the bankrupt in a considerable sum. A private company
was formed to carry on his business, and the bankrupt and a Mr.
Wilson were directors, Wilson taking first debentures and the bank-
rupt second debentures. Mumford never told me that Wilson, know
ing there was a heavy debt due to him, had promised that if he
gor 15s. in the pound upon his first debentures he would make up
the debt as far as he could that was owing from Gibbs to Mum-
ford. The debtor's business was a business in the Whitechapel
hay and straw market. It is a largely-attended market and anyboly
can bring goods there. Mumford told me that one of the things be
had had to contend against was a very heavy rental of £570, and
that prior to his bankruptcy he had been in negotiation with his
father's trustees to get it reduced to £250. Arbitrators had been
a pointed, one making an estimate of £250 and the other an estimate
of £320, the decision of the umpire being for £250. That would
have given the bank a very good security for their £1,900, but upon
the bankruptoy proceedings supervening the trustees would not pr
ceed with the negotiations, and the result was that the rental being
ay heavy as £570 the bankers did not stand upon their security, but
proved for the £2,000 and the Official Receiver distrained on the
lease. I know that when Gibbs wanted to enter into a contract for
the supply of stone Wilson and Mumford became joint guarantees
fer £2,000. I understand that an action was brought against them
on their guarantee and the money was paid, but Mumford owed
Wilson his share, £1,000. I know that in January and February
there were some serious failures in the corn trade-people with whom
Mumford had dealt and people in the trade began to be apore
Lensive. I agree that a consequence of that would probably be that
in the case of a man having credit transactions if there was a doubt
