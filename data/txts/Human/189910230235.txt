it is not a proper cashier's book—it does not give you sufficient information—it starts after the first moneys dealt with by Singleton—that was not Mr. Colee's fault; it does not start with that balance that should havo been in hand; and as to the keeping of the book, it is impossible to ascertain from merely looking at the book which are receipts and which are payments, because they are dealt with in precisely the same way—for instance, if the cashier receives £100 from Mr. Jones, he puts on the debit side of the account "Mr. Jones £100," and on the oilier hide of the account there also appears "Mr. Jones £100 '—if he pays Mr. Jones £100, he draws a cheque to Mr. Jones, and on the debit side appears "Mr. Jones £100," and on the credit side "Mr. Jones £100," and therefore, without tracing it through the bank pass-book or by some other means, it is impossible at a glance to see whether it is a receipt or a payment I do not suggest that it is an intentional concealment, but from the system it is impossible to trace it—I am able to get at what was done with the pass-book, and so on—the cash-book is not a record of cash received by the company, but only of cash received bv the cashier: there are omissions from that cash-book of money paid by the public for shares—I have noticed from time to time in that cash-book a I the initials of the directors, including those of Mr. Wain and Mr. Lambert, and also in the petty cash-book;
