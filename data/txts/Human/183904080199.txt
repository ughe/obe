STEPHEN BURTENSHALL. I am a watchman, and live in Little Britain. I was on duty at the Castle and Falcon, between eleven and twelve o'clock on this night—I went into the tap-room, and they were all drinking toge ther in good humour, when I came out—I went in again to call the policeman out, and said the sergeant was going by—the prisoner was then talking along with them—the deceased was laughing and joking with them also—I turned, and came out, and Childs, the deceased, came out laughing directly after-wards—the prisoner followed him with two or three pots in his hand—the de ceased came running down the yard, and the prisoner after him twenty-five or thirty yards—he then overtook him, and struck him in the left side of the ear with a quart pot—he then up with his hand to strike him again, and the policeman rushed in between them, but did not reach him to hit him—the policeman asked him something, and he up with both hands to strike the de ceased again, but he hit the policeman instead of him, with one hand on the breast, and the other on the top of the shoulder—they stood there talking a little while, and then went in to have something to drink, all of them, but what passed then, I do not know—I saw the deceased at the same public-house next evening, with his head tied up.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What was he doing there? A.  Nothing—I did not see him drinking or smoking—it was about nine o'clock—there were only about five or six people there—I had not seen him at all in the course of the day. JOHN STEPHENS. I am a surgeon. The deceased was brought to St. Bartholomew's hospital on Wednesday, the 6th of March—he had a wound on the left ear, which divided the ear, and a slight bruise behind the ear—they might have been done by a pot—he was not drunk, he had been drinking—I dressed the ear, and did what I thought necessary—I pro posed his stopping in the hospital, but he wished to go home—he seemed a respectable young man—I desired him to come again, but did not see him again alive—I assisted Mr. Lewis in making a post-mortem  examination—he died from erysipelas on the head—that might have been occasioned by the blow on the head—he had no signs of erysipelas about him when he came to me.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Would not drinking raw brandy be very apt to bring it on? A.  Not likely to bring it on, but very likely to increase it, it would not cause it without a wound—I should think it a very im proper course to pursue after a wound however slight—it did not appear to me a severe wound when he came to me—I put a suture in to unite the two parts, and gave him some opening medicine—he was quite sen sible, and did not appear stunned by the blow—if he had taken proper care of himself, and abstained from liquor, and done as I desired him, it is probable erysipelas might not have come on.
