   Q.  Do not you think it very difficult to distinguish whether this was a drunken attempt to molest the prosecutor or to rob him? A.  I had seen them insult several people before—I said to Parr, "You call yourself drunk?"—he said, "No, I am sober"—I said, "You have been drinking." FREDERICK NOYES. I live in Clerk's-place, Bagnigge-wells-road. I was with Matthews, and saw the prisoners insulting every person they met—I saw Parr throw the prosecutor down and draw his watch from his pocket—I and Matthews stepped up at the time, and he let go of the watch—I saw him drop it—we went up to the prosecutor, and saw him pick the watch up from the pavement—we followed Parr, and gave him in charge of a policeman—Snell was about ten yards distant at the time, staggering about.
   Cross-examined. Q.  This taking hold of the prosecutor, taking his watch, and throwing him down, must have taken a great while, I suppose? A.  Not a great while—I suppose it took half an hour. HENRY JOHNSON. I am a policeman. I was going home from this Court on the 4th of March—I saw Parr pass me, and the prosecutor, and two witnesses—about a minute afterwards they gave him in my charge—I took Snell at the work-house about an hour afterwards—the prisoners were in liquor, but not so drunk as not to know what they were doing.
   Cross-examined. Q.  When you found the prisoner had got nothing, but that the prosecutor had got the watch, you might have taken the man before a Magistrate, and had them committed as rogues and vagrants, might you not? A.  Certainly—there was no bruise on the watch—Parr told me he belonged to the workhouse, and said where we could find Snell. NOT GUILTY.
