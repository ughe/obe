   Cross-examined by MR. ADOLPHUS . Q.  DO you know Vincent at all? A.  I never remember seeing him before he was at the office—I never saw Sullivan before—he said he came for butter—he did not say twenty casks—he did not mention the quantity in my hearing—I assisted in the deli very into the cart—it was a common town cart—it was not covered with any thing when I saw it—there was a mat or two on the copse, which is very common—I think there was not sufficient to cover the whole—I saw nobody about, but the carman who took them in—nobody assisting him at all—nobody drawing mats from the copse over the butter.
   MR. CLARKSON . Q.  I believe you only remained while the casks were loading? A.  Certainly not—there is a very considerable thoroughfare in Thames-street—my attention was confined to the cart. SAMUEL CHITTLE. I live at No. 3, Cumberland-place, North-street, Whitechapel. On the 4th of December I was at Bow—on coming home I called at the King's Arms public-house—I think it was between three and four o'clock—I saw Sullivan and Vincent there, drinking a pot of beer toge ther—I saw Hill's town cart standing almost close by the door—there was nothing in it—I did not go up to look into it—I was there about half an hour—they left before me—to the best of my knowledge they went out of the house together—I did not see which way they went—they took the cart with them to the best of my knowledge, but I do not know—I did not come out with them—when I came out it was gone—I came away perhaps a quarter of an hour after them.
   Cross-examined by MR. ADOLPHUS . Q.  There was but one cart, I pre sume? A.  I saw but one, that was Hill's—it might be a few yards from the door—there was nothing in it—there could not be twenty casks of butter in it without my seeing it—I cannot say whether Sullivan and Vincent met there accidentally or not—it is common for two carmen to drink together.
   MR. CLARKSON . Q.  Did you look into the cart to see whether there was any thing in it? A.  I did as I came up, but I saw nothing in it—it was empty—I am certain there was nothing in it. JAMES HARRINGTON. I am a publican, and live in Tower-street. I know the prisoner Gregson—I saw him at my house, I think it was on Thursday, the 6th or 7th of December, the day before he was taken up—he asked if I wanted to buy any butter—I said I did not know that I did—he asked what I was paying—I said, "A shilling a pound"—I asked if he had got any—he said he had not himself, but he knew somebody who had—I asked him the price—he asked me 80 s . per cwt. I think, and said I was to give him some thing for himself provided I bought it—I said it would not do for me unless it was very good, and I should not buy it unless I saw it—he said they should bring me one in the afternoon, about three or four o'clock, to see if I liked it—as he was going away I said, "There is some butter stolen from Mr. Barber's quay, it is none of that, is it?"—I do not exactly know what he said, but it was "Oh no," or something to that purpose—he said nothing about the quality—I did not ask him about it—I did not see him again that day—I went out, and left word at home if any body called with any thing, to leave it, but nothing came—I saw him about eleven or twelve o'clock
