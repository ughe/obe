Cross-examined by MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  Do you mean to swear you had either of these firkins in your possession? A.  Yes—I know the large one by its being red inside and the name of M'Adam on the bottom—this firkin was sent to M'Adam, was returned to Pilcher, and then sent to me from them with putty, and I had in my shop a firkin marked like this—I do not mean to swear to that other one, not marked with a name or a mark—firkins have no mark on them, only the weight—I never saw a name on a firkin before in my life—the name is put on this firkin in ink—this is it—it is a direction to him, not as a mark of ownership—there are no other marks on it, except the lead that is contained inside—I cannot say exactly how long before I had seen this firkin safe on my premises—I have seen it within six months—I never heard before that the prisoner had threatened one of the persons in my employ to make a charge against him to me for ill-using a horse, but for not cleaning him—I have brought both the firkins here—I found them in a closet at the back of a room in Little Earl-street, Marylebone—I know the prisoner lodged there—I did not see him there—I took Mead to point out the house, and I also found some work which I had given to the prisoner for his wife to do—the larger firkin stood in front of my shop, and the smaller one at the back—the prisoner was principally employed in the factory, but he was employed in the shop sometimes. MB. CLARKSON. Q.  Had the prisoner access to the place where these firkins were? A.  Yes, he was there most of his time—I have lost a great quantity of fat. JOHN MEAD. I am in the prosecutor's employ. About two months ago there was a barrow outside my master's shop—about a quarter to ten o'clock at night I was sent to take it down to the factory—as I went I trod on a bundle of fat outside the factory-gate, about the middle of the court—I put my hand down to the bundle, and found it was fat—I took the barrow into the factory—after the prisoner had shut up that night, I saw him come down the court and take up the bundle of fat—he put it on his shoulder, and I fol lowed him with it to a little court facing his house, in Little Earl-street—I then went home—I knew he lodged in the front room on the first floor, at No. 6, Little Earl-street—I think there was about half a hundred weight of fat in the bundle—the prisoner was at the time employed in the factory in melting—there was fat in the factory.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Why did you not mention this to your master? A.  I mentioned it to one of the apprentices, who asked me what it was about, and what I had seen the prisoner do, and what I had to say against Marks—I had not said anything against him to anybody then, but the apprentice knew it, because he was in the factory one Saturday afternoon, about a fort night after, when Marks blowed  me up, and said the horse was not half dressed; and I said if he did not mind, I would say something to stop his mouth—I did not see any firkins. MB. CLARKSON. Q.  You did not speak of it for a fortnight? A.  No, not till he blowed  me up—I was afraid be would knock me about. CHARLES COWDERY. I am apprentice to the prosecutor. I was in prison for three weeks on a charge of misconduct—I saw the prisoner come into the factory with the horse, and he said to Mead, "You have not half cleaned the horse, I shall inform your master"—Mead said, "If you say anything about me, I shall say something about you"—I was not with my master when the firkins were found—I know the smaller firkin—it is my master's—I saw a cloth in the firkin, and about a quarter of a pound of fat in it.
