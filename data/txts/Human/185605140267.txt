THOMAS SMITH. I am manager to Mr. Hopcroft, a printer, of Mincing-lane. On the afternoon of 23rd, I counted the paper over, and gave it to Bentley to damp—I have seen the three or four sheets ( produced )—they are not in good order now; I should not think they were spoiled before they were doubled up, but I do not know how they were when he had them—I have examined the paper found at the prisoner's house, it is worth 7 s . or 8 s ., and is Mr. Hopcroft's property; they are not spoiled sheets—we do not allow paper to be taken, but it is the custom in the business—the prisoner has been six weeks in the service.
   COURT . Q.  You do not allow it? A.  No, but it is the custom at other houses for pressmen to claim the paper which they do not spoil; they are entitled, as a reward for their care, to the sheets which are not spoiled—very often there are a great many spoiled in the working—these are not fit for circulation.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Has he been working for five or six years for Seyfang and Co.? A.  Yes—the house does not wish to press the case against him.

 531. ROBERT CHISNELL , stealing 59 1/2 yards of silk, value 10 l  . 10 s  .; the goods of Edmund Spall .
   MR. W. J. PAYNE . conducted the Prosecution. WILLIAM TRIM. I am warehouseman to Edmund Spall, a silk agent, of No. 120, Cheapside. On the afternoon of 1st May, about half past 5 o'clock, I left the premises with my master, who fastened the door, and took the key—the warehouse is on the second floor—on the following morning I went to the warehouse, as usual, about 10 minutes to 9 o'clock, and about a minute or two afterwards a policeman came up to me—I found that the door had been broken open, and fastened again with a hinge; the policeman took it off, and we both went in—on looking round I missed a parcel of nlk aprons, which had been on the counter, and the cash box, which had been in a little ante-room on one side, and contained two sovereigns, and a few shillings in silver; it was safe on the Thursday evening—there were nearly twenty pieces of silk, and they were worth about 30 l .—on the 2nd I went with an officer to Mr. Duke's, a pawnbroker's, in the New Cut, and two pieces of silk were shown to me there, which I immediately recognized, as a portion of the silk taken from the warehouse—three other pieces of silk were shown to me at Mr. Cole's, in the Waterloo-road, on the same after noon, which also formed a portion of what I had missed; and last Saturday I saw two more at the Mansion House; they formed a portion of the same silk—I do not know the prisoner.
   Cross-examined by MR. LILLEY . Q.  Is Mr. Spall in a large way of busi ness? A.  Yes—there might, perhaps, be the same quantity of silk on the premises in a drawer that there was on the counter, and of the same pattern and quality, and some other patterns as well—we had about thirty pieces of the same pattern, and have about ten left; we only supply the wholesale houses—we have supplied a great many pieces of this particular pattern to the wholesale houses—Mr. Spall is agent for a manufacturer at Macclesfield.
   MR. W. J. PAYNE . Q.  I believe this particular silk was made by Mr. Sherrard, of Macclesfield? A.  Yes—that in the drawer was not taken away—I recognize this by the pattern and quality; the pattern is made exclusively by Mr. Sherrard for Mr. Spall—from the wholesale houses it goes to the retail.
