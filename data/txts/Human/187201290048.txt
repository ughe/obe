   Witness.  I should not have paid the money unless he had produced the receipt—I kept 11 l . for the pianoforte, which he said was at the Pantech nicon, and I found that he had substituted another instrument when I went to see it—that was proved in evidence.
   Prisoner. Q.  Have you got a guarantee from Mr. James? A.  I have—there is nothing owing on this deed now—I am not a halfpenny out of pocket at present.
   Re-examined.  Mrs. Dewsbury, whose money I lent to the prisoner, is my wife's sister—the actual amount the prisoner had, was 19 l . on 25th November, and he was to get 11 l .—he got the 19 l . at Mr. Harris's private office, where it was settled—I gave him the 11 l . on the 29th—I would not have parted with either of those sums if I had known the rent had not been paid—I always require the receipt when I lend money on a bill of sale—the charges the prisoner made against me were gone into before Mr. Ellison on six different days—the prisoner was assisted by a solicitor—he swore that he had not had more than 19 l ., and he had had 30 l . from me in hard cash—the claim was for 37 l . 10 s ., that was the whole money involved in that matter—the furniture in the bill of sale was not entirely his own—he described a portion of his property, as being at the Bedford Pantechnicon. HENRY HARRIS. I was present when the 49 l . 8 s . was paid for the bill of sale—after the bill was issued Mr. Follit asked for the receipt for rent, and the prisoner handed it him.
   Prisoner. Q.  What are you? A.  A solicitor's clerk—I looked at the receipt, and saw enough of it to identify it—I am a managing clerk, I have nothing to do with Mr. Follit at all—I am not his partner, but my name is up because I introduce a good deal of business—I am not admitted—I am Chancery and Common Law clerk.
