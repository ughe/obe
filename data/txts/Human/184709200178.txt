No—he walked back, and up with his fist to strike me, and said, "You b—, stand up"—I had my truncheon in my hand, and struck him, or else he would have got away form me—he put his hand in the attitude of striking, which made me strike—he went quietly with the assistance of two City policemen. CHARLES HEISCH. I am a lecturer upon practical chemistry at St. Thomas's Hospital. I have examined these five packages, two of them contain jalap, one contains carbonate of magnesia, another some pills, and another some brown sugar—the pills appear to be composed of common aloes, commonly called bitter aloes—I also examined the bottle—it had no label round it—it had apparently contained some organic matter in a state of decomposition, but it was impossible to state what it was, it was so completely decomposed—this is not the bottle that was taken from the privy—the bottle taken from the privy contained a quantity of night soil and traces of arsenic—there was certainly enough to enable me to say that the bottle had contained white arsenic ( the bottle was here produce )—it has no label on it, nor had it when it was given to me—I was not present at the post-mortem  examination—I received the stomach from Dr. Taylor—I submitted it to a chemical test, and found that it contained a considerable quantity of white arsenic—I was afterwards present when the kidneys were taken out—I submitted them to the same analysis, and they also contained arsenic—I succeeded in reproducing arsenic to the extent of about fifteen grains, some portion of which I have here—( producing several small tubes )—this is arsenic in the metallic condition in which it came from the contents of the stomach, and this is some in the state of white arsenic—at my last experiment I found this white arsenic in a pure state to adhere to one side of this tube—fifteen grains would be quite sufficient to cause death—this other tube contains fifteen grains of arsenic—it is a about the same in bulk as an ordinary pinch of snuff, that might he held between the fingers.
   COURT. Q.  Would tea hold it in solution? A.  To a certain extent it would, but it would be more suspended in the fluid than actually in solu tion—it would be held in the tea for a short time, but being rather neavy it would soon settle to the bottom—it would settle, perhaps, in the course of three or four minutes.
   MR. BODKIN. Q.  Supposing a person to stir the tea, as is usual, before taking it, it would then be sufficiently buoyant to be taken in it? A.  Yes, it might then be taken—arsenic is as nearly tasteless as possible—supposing fifteen grains of arsenic to be put in a cup of tea, sweetened with sugar, and milk in it, it would not be detected by the taste—I did not see the deceased during her life.
   Cross-examined. Q.  In extracting this amount from the kidneys and stomach, you allow, I suppose, a good deal for loss; there must have been a great deal more than that taken into the stomach? A.  Yes—it would be difficult to say whether there was double—it would depend a good deal upon the evacuations that took place after the poison had been taken.
   COURT. Q.  From what do you infer the fact of more having been taken into the stomach? A.  From the fact of her having vomited frequently.
