   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  How long is it since your first bankruptcy? A.  About seven years—I was then a horse-dealer, and in the same yard which I now keep—I purchased the premises about three months afterwards—they took bills for them at different dates—my first divident was 5 s .; there was another about 3 s . 6 d ., and there will be ano ther—I will swear that more than 5 s . has been paid—my second bank ruptey was at the latter end of 1834—there has been no dividend declared on that as yet—It is in the hands of Mr. Green—I cannot tell what my cre ditors will get—Hill asked me to give 500 guineas to buy time—I dare say he knew I was a bankrupt—when a man is unfortunate it is generally known—I hava had conversation with a person of the name of Bardell, the brother of the two Bardells who are indicated—he is a vetrinary surgeon, and a very respectable man—he asked me how I came to indict them—I said it was my attorney's advice, and I could not go from it—my attorney is Mr. Norton—he advised me to indict the Bardells—I went with Mr. Bardell to Mr. Norton's, and there was a conversation about their putting in bail. Q.  Now Mr. Bardell is alive, and is here; do you remember his asking Mr. Norton why he advised you to put his two brothers into the indict ment, when they were innocent? A.  I do not recollect it—Mr. Norton might have dropped such an expression as, That he was determined they should not be witness for the other defendants—It was not in my presence.
   Q.  Do you mean that such a villanous expression might be used, and you forget it; such an infamous expression? A.  Mr. Norton's answer was, "we have no occasion to press the indictment against your brothers"—Mr. Bardell went on to say something about the bail, and Mr. Norton said, "The indictment has no occassion to be pressed against your brothers; they have not much to fear; but you must put in bail"—he said that after advising me to include them in the indictment.
   Q.  Was any thing said about a hundred pounds? A.  Never to me, or to Mr. Bardell in my presence, upon my oath; nor did Mr. Norton say that he put the Bardells into the indictment that they should not be wit nesses, nor anything to that effect—Mr. Norton did not tell me why he put the Bardells in the indictment—I went to him to indict the Paddington committee, and he indicted whom he pleased—I appointed with Mr. Bar dell to meet him at Mr. Norton's—I went there to meet him—he got there first.
   Q.  Did Norton say that their names must remain in the indict ment; his object was to prevent their being witnesses; because, if they were ommitted, the paddington proprietors would subpœna them against Bray? A.  Never, sir; nor that their evidence would be of importance, I having commissioned Joseph Bardell to raise 100 l . to take my omni buses iff the road, and discontinue further opposition; and if he could not get 100 l ., to take 50 l ., and they to take my stock at a valuation—there was not a word said about it—we were there half an hour, talking about the indictment a little, but the most was about putting in bail.
   Q.  Upon your oath. was not the appointment to go to Norton with Bar dell to try to get his brothers out of the indictment? A.  No; the agree ment was for Mr. Norton not to oppose the bail, but take them on Mon day—there was nothing said about getting his brothers out of the indict ment—I never in any way wanted money to settle this business.
