   Cross-examined.  I have known the prisoner some time—I always found her very decent and cleanly. BRIDGET COSGROVE. I am a nurse at the Islington Workhouse—the deceased child was brought there on the 16th of July—to all appearances it was a very healthy child—it was brought to the workhouse again on 28th August, and I think I saw it on the 29th—I could not recognise it then, it was in a dying condition and was sent to the infirmary—I did not attend it afterwards—I only had charge of it as wet nurse—it had been in my care from the 14th of May to the 16th of July, when the mother went out as wet nurse. ELIZA JUDGE. I am superintendent night nurse at Islington Workhouse—I saw the deceased on 28th August; it was so weak that it could not take the breast, but after carefully feeding it for two hours it was able to do so, but very feebly—I fed it with beef tea and arrowroot and milk for two hours and warmed it, and then it was able to take the breast—it went on pretty well to the Friday and then began to sink, and it died on Satur day morning the 2nd of September—I never heard it cry, it was too feeble—it was very thin and emaciated—it had no diarrhoea then. PHILLIP COWEN, M. R. C. S. I am resident medical officer for the Islington Workhouse—I attended Eliza Onslow in her confinement, and on 28th August the child was brought to me by Mrs. Barales in a very emaciated condition—I ordered it to be taken up to a suckling woman, and also to have some beef tea and arrowroot—I did not expect it would recover, it had the ordinary signs of emaciation; wrinkled skin, prominent cheek bones, and sunken eyes—there was no apparent disease—it was then clean and there were no signs in the flexes of the thighs and such parts as though it had been neglected—there were no sores from non-washing and drying, but there was a little external redness of the anus, which indicated that there had been diarrhcea—I was told by the day-nurse that it had had diarrhoea for two days after its admission; that nurse is not here—I spoke to Eliza Judge about it; nothing was said about diarrhcea—I afterwards made a post-mortem  examination—there were no marks of violence externally; the whole of the organs were healthy, except two, although they were deficient in blood—the brain was healthy, but between the skull and the dura mater—there were 3 ounces of serum; the mesenteric glands were somewbat degenerated—the middle part of the intestine was empty, the large intestine contained fair-looking fcecal matter; I say fair, in a healthy sense—the other organs were all healthy—the enlargement and degenera tion of the mesenteric glauds is sometimes constitutional, and is pro duced by bringing children up by hand—the state of the mesenteric glands was not sufficient to account for death—the cause of death was mesenteric disease and emaciation, and the question is how that emaciation was produced—there was serum in the brain, but there was no disease to account for that; that is a symptom when the current of blood from some debilitating cause becomes more slow, and the capilaries give out serum—that is common in exhausting maladies, in children especially—that is common whenever the condition is debilitated, from whatever cause—the condition of the mesenteric glands and of the brain, was not sufficient to account for death—there was no disease of the brain to account for death—
