SARAH CLARK sworn.
 I am servant to Mr. Doughty.
 What damage was done to his house? - Every pannel of the door was broke and all the windows. The chimney-piece in the fore parlour was torn all to pieces.
 PRISONER's DEFENCE.
 I am entirely innocent of what is laid to my charge.
 For the Prisoner.
   THOMAS THOMPSON sworn.
 I am servant to Mr. Howard, in Great Ormond street, who is a member of parliament. I have lived with him almost fifteen years.
 Was you near Mr. Doughty's house at the time of the riot there? - Yes. Mrs. Newman knocked me up about three o'clock, and told me I must put lights out, which I did. I went out a little before four o'clock to Queen-square. I saw the prisoner and his fellow-servant, standing together at the top of Devonshire-street; I had never spoken to the prisoner before, though I have seen him at times during this last year and half at Sir  Henry Gray's. I went up to him and wished him good morning; I said it was a dismal scene. He lifted up his hands and said it was a dismal scene indeed. While we were talking one of the rioters came up, who had a scar in his face and was bloody; he said they would go and pull down that house in Great Ormond-street, meaning the Lord Chancellor's house. The prisoner said they had better not go there, it was dismal enough to see what had been done. The rioters said the Lord Chancellor's house was to come down; and another house; I understood him Mr. Langdale's. I staid there about a quarter of an hour; then I left him there, and came to the corner of Great Ormond-street. Some of the rioters followed me, and said these were the houses which were to come down.
 Had that man a bell? - No, he had no bell in his hand when I saw him. He had on a brown coat and round hat; he had a scar in his face he had been knocked down by a gentlemen in Queen-square.
 Where was it you had this conversation with the prisoner and the man? - At the corner of Devonshire-street, in Queen-square. I left the prisoner with his fellow-servant there when I went home.
 PETER BOND sworn.
 I am servant to Mr. Lewis, in Ormond-street. I went to Devonshire-street about four o'clock, and staid about half an hour. I saw  Wilson the prisoner there. I know him perfectly well by sight; he lives in the same street my master does. At the time I was there he was standing holding his fellow-servant by the arm; he stood by the end of Boswel-court. I did not see them touch any thing, or hear them say any thing to any body. I returned home and went again before five o'clock. Then it was all demolished and the mob were gone to a gentleman's house in Great Ormond-street.
 Mr. WILKINSON sworn.
 I have known the prisoner some time; he has an exceeding good character; when I heard he was taken up I sent my compliments to Sir  Henry Gray that I would come to dinner with him to know how this man came to be taken up. Sir Henry told me he was exceeding quiet. I always thought him a decent well behaved servant.
     NOT GUILTY.

 385.  MARY WILKINSON was indicted for stealing a copper gallon pot, value 2 s. the property of William Clark, June 12th.
   WILLIAM CLARK sworn.
 I live in Crown Court, Newgate-street. I observed the prisoner had got a gallon pot concealed under her petticoats. I stopped her. I asked her what she had got and where she was going? She said, what is that to you, I am going to hell. I said, you may as well give me my pot before you go. I went to catch hold of her, and she fell down by the kennel and said I was going to rob her. I took the pot from her and charged a constable with her and sent her to the compter.
 Was she in liquor? - I do not think she was, but she might be.
