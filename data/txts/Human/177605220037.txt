 2d Count. That he one bay horse of the price of 20 l. the property of  Samuel Whitbread, in the stable of the said Samuel, wilfully, maliciously, and feloniously did kill and destroy, against the statute.
 The witnesses were examined apart at the request of the prisoner.
   JACOB YELLOWLEY sworn.
 I am clerk to Mr. Whitbread: last winter at the time of the severe frost, when the street was very slippery, Mr. Whitbread had a horse lamed; this horse was put under the care of  John Edwards, Mr. Whitbread's farrier, for cure; he was under his hands for a considerable time and grew worse, and Edwards said he could do no more; one Mr. Buckle then had him under his care, and the horse grew better; Buckle, however, was discharged; the horse was again put under Edwards's care, and again grew worse; he was put again under the care of Buckle, and grew better, so that he was ordered to have light shoes put on, and to be put out to grass, thinking that exercise might do him good: I saw the horse on the Saturday, he seemed better of his lameness, but I observed he looked sickly, dull, and heavy about the eyes; on Monday morning I heard the horse was dead; that he died in great agitation of body; on the death of the horse Buckle was sent for; he told me the horse was certainly poisoned; I desired some people might go with him and see the horse opened; they returned with some stuff which they took out of the body of the horse; I did not see it taken out. I made Mr. Whitbread acquainted with the whole matter, and he ordered a warrant to be got and Storer taken up; he worked in Edwards's shop, which is in Mr. Whitbread's yard; I think I saw him at work in the shop on Saturday evening, but I cannot be positive.
 Cross Examination.
 Was not Edwards taken up on this affair? - Yes, he was; we did not know who to suspect; the suspicion lay mostly on Storer: the business is done in the yard, but the horse that was poisoned did not stand in the yard, but in a stable on the other side of the street.
   WILLIAM SCOTT sworn.
 I work with Mr. Edwards in Golden-lane: I heard  John Storer ask Blanden, in Mr. Whitbread's shop, What is the strongest poison for a horse? I believe it was on a Saturday in the last month; I worked but two days, Friday and Saturday; Blanden said, I cannot tell, I had like to have poisoned myself once by taking mercury; I said, if he had taken arsenic instead of mercury, it would have poisoned him or a horse either, for it was stronger than a caustic: afterwards, about nine in the morning, he asked me to go and fetch him two ounces of yellow arsenic, and he would pay me again what it cost; I went to the chymist's at Barbican, he refused to sell it me; I shewed him my apron, and said I was a farrior, and we used it as a medicine for horses; he went and asked advice, and then came and served me; I pounded it in a ladle and put it in a drawer; I said to the prisoner, Jack, there it is: he said it was very well; I never saw what came of it.
 Cross Examination.
 Did he say his master had ordered him to get it for any horse? - No, he was foreman of the shop; he said nothing about his master to me.
