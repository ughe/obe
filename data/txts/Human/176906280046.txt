I said I was not certain. She said she was going out, and I was desired to let her in. (The lock of the street door was out of order, and the key would not open it on the outside.) I did not go out. She came up between eight and nine to light a candle. She said she was in a bad neighbourhood, but she had taken care of herself. She went away, and desired I would let her in at ten. She came home a little after twelve. I let her in with another woman with her: that woman had a lighted candle in her hand, and a whole one not lighted, seemingly of about four in the pound. The prisoner took and lighted the whole candle by the other, came in, and wished the other woman a good night. She shut the door, which I bolted and double locked, left the key in it, went up stairs, and left her standing at the door to go into the parlour. I saw her no more till the Wednesday morning, before Justice Miller. I was awaked in the morning by the cry of fire: my husband was in the bed with me, and my child about three years old. He got out, and found the room full of smoke; he looked out at the window, and the people called to him to come down: he opened the door and went to the top of the stairs, and the smoke met him, so that he could not go down: then he said, We are all gone! He then went to the child that was asleep, and took and laid her out at the window in the gutter; (there was a parapet wall;) he then took me in his arms and lifted me up, and assisted me to get out into the gutter, and followed me. I then had the child in my arms. We were only in our shirt and shift. I with the fright found myself fainting, upon which he took the child, and pushed me on my side towards the tiles, so that I should not fall over; he went with the child over the top of one house and carried it to the top of another; it is a flat roof; he left the child, and came and assisted me up to the child; but we saw no way to get down. After we had stayed some time, there came somebody out at a trap-door, and we went down into the house, where we got some clothes. There was no body in the house when I let the prisoner in but she, my husband, self, and child. On the Sunday morning there stood a mahogany table by the shop window with a large feather bed upon it, and behind the door stood another with a feather bed upon it. She had two children and a niece that she kept in the house then, but when she came up to light her candle, between eight and nine, they were gone.
   Peter Gilcrest. I can give no farther account than what my wife has.
   Elizabeth Bull. I am a servant to Mrs. Ashby, next door but one to the prisoner. I heard the prisoner tell my mistress, if she was forced to leave her house, she would either pull it down, or set it on fire. This was about three or four months ago. This she spoke seriously. My mistress is now out of town.
   Jane M'Gin. I live in Crown-Street, which is at the back of Charles Street. I saw the prisoner, between seven and eight on the Sunday evening before the fire, several times standing at her own door. I saw her three children put in a coach with a trunk and several bundles; one seemed like a sheet or table cloth with things in it; and they went off I do not know where.
   Esther Fitzwater. I live in Petty France, Westminster. I saw the prisoner in King-Street, about twenty yards from her own house, on Monday morning, about a quarter after six: she was walking a great pace towards Charing-Cross alone.
   John Mills. The morning her house was on fire I looked in at the window of the shop about twenty minutes before seven; there was a basket seemingly to be used to carry greens in, some straw, and leaves of greens, and nothing else but a few old boards. I could see all over the shop.
   Clement Scot. I broke open the prisoner's door with a hammer, but I saw no goods at all besides a bit of an old basket and a bit or two of board: the place looked as if the goods had been moved.
   John Scot. I broke a pane of glass and looked in, but saw nothing in the shop except some rubbish.
   Robert Mograge. I am a shoemaker, and live next door to the Bagnio, Charing-Cross. On that Monday, near eight in the morning, a man met the prisoner at my door, and said something to her; he seemed to be in a terrible flurry. I begged of her to come in: she said she hoped no body was burned, and that she went out about five in the morning. She said she had a candle to see to pack up her goods. There were two men with her, Mr. Thompson was one of them. The other man said he was going to take her goods away for her.
