The following submitted, "Taking advantage of the foregoing cir
cumstances, we have thought it wise, entirely in your interests, to
open for you 30 Tinto shares." It is part of a business letter, and
there are one or two amendments in my handwriting. I could not
possibly say now for whom these letters were intended, as there is no
address. As to the phrase which occurs frequently in these cirou
lars "Your interests being identical with our own," it is an entirely
mistaken idea that the outside broker wants his client to lose. I have
heard it stated here, but the idea is utterly ridiculous. The outside
broker is only too anxious to pay his clients. It is to his interest to
do so because he retains his client, at the same time making money for
himself en route. If the suggestion is made here that the interest
of the outside stockbroker is antagonistio to that of his client at
means that if the outside broker gets £10 off his client and the client
loses it he has lost a client. Surely it must be the interest of the
outside broker to retain his client and to make as many more as
possible. He cannot do that if every deal is antagonistio to the
client. The deal must be in his favour or the relationship would
not continue. The outside broker is only toa glad to draw cheques
to his client in order to increase his business. It is not like the
case of a bookmaker; the client does not bet, and I repeat that
the outside broker generally protects himself.
Mr. Justice Lawrence. That is not part of the bargain. There is
nothing in these circulars that says a word about protecting your
self.
Witness. Only, on the other, assumption, if the client loses the
£10 he does not go on any more, and the broker loses him and loses
his business. Well, the business would not last.
