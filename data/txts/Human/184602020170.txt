PETER STACEY. I am the witness's husband—I was the last person up—the house was quite secured—I went to bed at twenty minutes after eleven—I fastened the back doors—I went into the yard twice after I shut the front door—the street door was quite fastened—when the alarm was given I got up—my wife said something, when I came down—I went to the back door and found it shut, but the side part of the door jamb was broken away, a few boards taken out and a hole large enough for an arm to be put through and unbolt the door—I found a pair of shoes near the door, and outside the door a chisel, which I gave the policeman—my house is in the parish of Rotherhithe.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Is that the name of the parish? A.  Yes—I keep a beer and coffee shop. CHARLES LACK. I am toll-collector at the Thames Tunnel—about a quarter-past two on the morning of the robbery, I saw a man come out of the prosecutor's house, without shoes—he had on a short jacket and cap—he ran away—I had not an opportunity of seeing his features—I have known the prisoner several years—it is my firm belief he is the man.
   Cross-examined. Q.  This was the darkest time of the night, was it? A.  Yes, but there was a gas-light within eight or ten feet of the house, so I had an opportunity of seeing him—Mrs. Stacey was calling police the moment I came up to the door. WILLIAM BRADLEY. I live in Church-street, Rotherhithe, in the same house with the prisoner—I remember having some shoes from the prisoner's mother, to take to Cuddy's—I did not see them as they were in a hand kerchief—I live in Mrs. Collins's house.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did not the prisoner come home at his usual hour that night? A.  Yes, about half-past ten or eleven—he remained there all night, as far as I know—I saw him about nine o'clock next morning in his bed-room—he slept in the same room with his mother. WILLIAM COLLINS. I am a shoemaker, I know these shoes—I half-soled them for the prisoner, my work is on them—he brought them himself and fetched them away—I have reason to believe they are the shoes.
   Cross-examined. Q.  You think there is some of your work on them? A.  Yes ( pointing it out )—I can swear to the work—they are the shoes I have repaired twice for him—I know the soles, they are nailed, as he ordered them, as he walked and I knew his wear—I was a witness in the New Court twelve years ago, against a boy for robbing my master—I was never in custody—I did not see the prisoner after the robbery, till he was taken. SAMUEL ANDREWS( police-constable N  264.) I received these shoes from Stacey.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Had you notice of this robbery? A.  I knew nothing of it, till I heard a cry of "Police"—I went with Mr. Stacey to see if a certain boy was known at home. RICHARD HILL( police-constable M  16.) I went to the Spread Eagle on Wednesday, the 21st of Jan., about half-past eleven, and found the prisoner in the tap-room—he denied the charge—I showed him the chisel—he said "It is mine," I told him to be careful what he said, as it would be used in evidence against him—and he then said, he knew nothing of the chisel.
   Cross-examined. Q.  How did you get the chisel? A.  I had it from Mr. Stacey—the prosecutor's is a well conducted house, for all I know—I did not hear that the prisoner was going to give himself up. JANE COLLINS. I took the shoes home to the prisoner, after my husband had worked on them.
 (The prisoner received a good character.) GUILTY. Aged 17.— Confined Six Months .
