Court to Mr. SMEE. Q. Is it a very common thing for persone
have stock transferred to them without accepting it? 4. Exceeding
common-I should think nine persons out of ten do not accept.
RICHARD BORD. I am a clerk in the Consol-office in the Bank,
the 3rd of Sept. I received from Mr. Sutton, a clerk in the same office
two tickets-(looking at them)--these are them-I received them from
him for the purpose of effecting a transfer-they are usually deposited i
a box, and we take them out as we are going on entering-these came
into my possession from Mr. Sutton-he was somewhere about the Transfer
office passing over tickets- I saw the prisoner in the Transfer-office at that
time-I am uncertain whether I received the tickets from the hand of Me
Sutton, or whether I may have taken them from the box, but they came
direct from him-I received them in the Transfer-office-I am employed
in the Transfer-office-the prisoner was not in the office at that time-be
made his appearance a few minutes afterwards--I happened to have the
number of tickets in my hand when he came up-there were several
other clerks assisting there, and he came up and inquired who entered
stock for the name of Oxenford--I replied that I did- I had these tickets
then in my hand-he then asked me if I would oblige him by entering
them directly, as Mr. Oxenford was waiting-I therefore proceeded to
enter them in the Transfer-book-whilst he was waiting I asked him if he
knew Mr. Oxenford, having known the name many years ago, before I
was in the Bank-he replied that he did he was an old friend of his-
said, "He must be getting an old man now"--he said he was-when I
had completed it I said, "Where is Mr. Oxenford ?" -- he said he wa
outside minding the horse--that he meaning himself) had got an early
of that day, and they were going down to Gravesend together-
supposed by that that he had done his business for the day, and had leare
to go away early-he then went out to fetch Mr. Oxenford, and shortly
returned with a gentleman, and asked me for the book--he then asked,
“What gentleman will see it ?”-it is the custom that one clerk should
witness the transfer, and that another person, in attendance with the party
transferring, should witness his identity-it must be some person know
to the Bank-the prisoner took the book and took it to Mr. Ingall,
another clerk, who was close by, sitting in the same office-at the same
time I heard him say to the person who had come into the office, “Come
along, Willey," or some such expression as that," it is all ready"-I saw
him and the other person come up together-I did not myself witness the
act of the transfer of this stock-I did not see the signature-I after
wards accompanied John Forrester, the officer, to the United States--
left London on the 18th of Sept.-We ultimately went to Boston, and
found Elder in Federal-street, No. 51 (we had been to a variety of other
places first-it was on a Thursday, about the end of Oct.- he was passing
by the name of Ellis-l recognised him as the man who had come into
the Bank with the prisoner, after his going out to fetch Oxenford, who
he said was minding the horse-I told Forrester that he was the man-
Forrester took him by the coat and said, “ Your name is Joseph Elder,
and you are lately from London"-he was afterwards committed to
prison, and I heard afterwards that he had destroyed bimself-I went
with Forrester to Nabant where the prisoner escaped from, but did not go
up to Brewster's Island.
