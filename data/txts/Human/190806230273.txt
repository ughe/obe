 Re-examined. I attended the meeting about the anonymous letter and proposed that the writer should be advertised for, which I believe was done; it was in the local papers. I afterwards saw Barker, jun., and Symes together, and thought then the letter referred to me; that made me suspect Symes had written it because I had always run young Barker down, and said it was a scandalous shame he should get that contract, being £800 higher than the lower man, through his father backing him. I thought Knight was spiteful towards me, and that Barker had been coaxing up these other things; that was only my inference. Barker and Knight were extreme friends, and as I had got at that job about the sand, about which Barker had to pay £100 through me, I thought they had said, "Now we can get our own back." That is the way I saw it after  wards. At first I thought that old Barker, or someone from his firm, had written the anonymous letter, having heard from Symes that I had called on him. The letter did not suggest my name—that was first suggested at the Inquiry. Before I came on the Board I had tried to get contracts from Mile End, but did not get any. I have also tendered at Bromley and Whitechapel. I have been the lowest two or three times at Whitechapel but I never got it. I never succeeded in getting one yet. I am very glad I did not. My business takings have decreased ever since I became a Guardian.
 (  DEFENCE OF GILDER .) SAMUEL GILDER(prisoner, on oath). I am Secretary to Friendly Societies, and from 1889 to 1902 Chief Clerk to the Soup Kitchen for the Jewish Poor, one of the largest institutions of that kind in London. In 1901, on my silver wedding, I received a valuable present. I have also taken a great part in charitable work amongst the poor and the Jews in the East End. I became a Guardian in 1904. Calcutt had then been four or five years the contractor, and was already reappointed for that year. He asked me to vote for him, and I said I should do so, all things being equal, he being the appointed tradesman and being on the spot. I was not corruptly influenced. Draper and I were at variance with regard to outdoor relief, of which I was in favour of our being more liberal; I also believed in giving local work to local men, and always insisted on Trades Union wages. With the exception of the indestructible paint I never voted against Knight. I was on the Finance and the Infirmary Visiting Committees. I had no conversation with Mrs. Calcutt about Calcutt repairing houses for Hirst. Calcutt asked me many times to spend a week-end at Southend, but I never went. In 1905 my wife and daughter went there for two or three days, and I went down on the Sunday with another daughter to fetch them home. I had not told Calcutt and did not myself know I was going until the last moment.
