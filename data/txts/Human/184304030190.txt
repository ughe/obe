THOMAS BULL. I am out of business; I live at the Horse and Groom, on Blackheath-hill. I have known the prisoner since August last—he is a bricklayer's labourer, and has worked for me in that capacity. On the morn ing of the 20th of March I was standing at my front door, at ten minutes to six o'clock, and saw the prisoner and his brother go quickly out of the yard—I have not the shadow of a doubt about the time—I had looked at the clock—the brother had a brown coat, and a bundle under his left arm—the prisoner had a sort of dirty-white frock and cap on, which is a very common dress—they were going swiftly to their work, in the direction of Blackheath-hill.
   COURT . Q.  What kind of cap had he? A.  A dirty-brown kind of cloth cap—there might be a piece of leather in front—I did not pull out my watch when they passed, but saw the church clock—they came out of the Horse and Groom yard—there is a direct road through there to the place where they lived. WILLIAM HINDES. I am a master plasterer, and live at Lewisham. The prisoner's brother is employed constantly for me, and I employed the prisoner at intervals—I employed him on Monday, the 20th of March on the Lee-road, at Mr. Russell's buildings—there is a new detached cottage there—it is from three-quarters of a mile to a mile from Mr. Meriton's—you come to the Lee-road first from Blackheath—they would not go near Mr. Meriton's in coming to their work, it would be quite out of their way—they would be nearer Mr. Meriton's when at my house, than they would be afterwards—I expected them both at work that morning—they overtook me, as I was going, at the upper end of Lee-lane—they came close behind me, walking very fast—I heard them talking together—I did not speak to them—that was about two minutes after six—I am very positive to the time, as I heard the clock strike six—they live nearly a mile and a half from the work.
   COURT . Q.  What clock are you speaking of as striking six? A.  Lee church clock—the prisoner had on a sort of dirty-white smock-frock, and a fur cap very much worn, to the best of my recollection—I dined on the premises—I noticed that the prisoner ate his breakfast, on the 18th of March, with a carpenter's chisel, and on the 20th he ate his dinner and breakfast with a carpenter's chisel—there are but few of us in the building—the master I work for ate his dinner with a lath—I had a knife—some of the other men ate with knives—the prisoner's brother ate with a knife with one blade and a half; one blade was broken. FRANCES WOODER. I am a widow, and live at Blackheath; the prisoner is my son. On Monday, the 20th of March, he was in bed at twenty minutes before six o'clock—I have a clock down stairs, and my son has one up stairs—my clock goes very nearly with the church clock on the hill—the prisoner passed through the room I sleep in, opened the door, and shut it after him—I was in bed—I looked at the clock before he got up, and it wanted twenty minutes to six—I look to see that he goes regularly to his work—I almost always look at the clock—I am quite sure I did so that morning. GEORGE SWAIN re-examined.  It was Lee church clock that I heard strike—it is not half a mile from our house—when the prisoner was brought by the officer to Mr. Meriton's premises I was there, and said I considered he was the man I saw that morning—I said so loud enough for the prisoner to hear me—I did not hear him say a word—I spoke loud enough for all in the house to hear me—the man I saw that morning had on a kind of brown cloth cap. NOT GUILTY.
