They then got his coat off his back - he attempted to keep it under his arm. He made his escape into a gingerbread stall with the coat under his arm; they rushed after him, and took away his coat. I saw him bleeding at the mouth, he walked very lame, and appeared to be hurt in the leg. They all came again, and took him from the stall round to the back part of a publican's booth; I could not see what they did there, but in five minutes I saw him without shirt, waistcoat, and handkerchief; he had nothing but his trowsers and shirt, and appeared to have been much hurt. I could distinguish several of the persons. I saw the prisoner there; I am quite certain that he is one of them. I was afraid to interfere to assist him. It was light - I could very easily distinguish a person's features. The rain had cleared up about three o'clock, and it was a fine evening. I attended before the Justice about a week after, and saw him in custody, and am certain he was one of the men. I swear positively to him.
 Cross-examined. Q. Did you go up into the crowd while this was going on - A. I was not in the crowd; I was looking at it; I heard murder cried, and went to see what it was. Every time they took anything they ran from him.
 Q. Why not run to some house and give an alarm - A. If I had run, very likely I should be attacked by some of them; I did not leave till they left him. I heard them say they would go and have something to drink; I ran up, and said,
 "Gentlemen, you cannot go in here, there is nobody there; master is not there;" the booth was pinned down. One of them was on horseback.
 Q. How soon did you give information at any place about this - A. About four o'clock in the morning. There were twelve or fourteen booths on the beath, but I was afraid to move to give information. It might last altogether about an hour and a half, but I was not there at the beginning. I was there for three-quarters of an hour, and had drank nothing that day but about a pint and a half of beer; and a gentleman, whose horse I was minding, gave me two glasses of stout.
 Q. When you heard the cry of murder, why not go and give information, instead of going to the place - A. I went to see what was the matter, and when I got up I was afraid to leave: I said nothing to them - they went down the course, and I after him.
 COURT. Q. Were you in the slightest degree intoxicated - A. I was not; I was frightened.
   JAMES BESWICK. I am a Bow-street patrol. On the 14th, I apprehend the prisoner at Monlsey Hurst - I told him it was for ill using and robbing Sheehan; he at first said he was not there, and then that he was there; I put him into the cage at Hampton-court, and took him before Mr. Trimmer, at Heston, next day. The prosecutor saw him that evening; he (Sheehan) was at that time very lame; indeed, I found him at the workhouse - he could just put his foot to the ground, but could not walk without a stick; he was examined before the Magistrate, and the prisoner committed.
 Cross-examined by MR. PLATT. Q. He at first said he was not there, and then that he was - A. Yes; I had old him of the robbery.
 Q. He said he was not at the robbery - A. No; he said he was not on the heath at all, and then said that he was; three more persons were taken, but could not be identified.
 JURY to SHEEHAN. Q. Do you know the man who was drinking with you - A. Yes; his name is Evans; he works with me; he went away about four minutes before this began.
 Prisoner's Defence. I am innocent; the officer asked me, as we went along, if I knew any of the people who were there; I said I knew nothing of them, but should know them if I saw them again, and that I had heard two men in the morning say, that two of them worked in a brickfield.
 One witness gave the prisoner a good character.

GEORGE HAYNES was indicted for breaking and entering the dwelling-house of John Blankford , about six o'clock in the forenoon of the 23d of August , ( Ann Floyer and others being therein), and stealing therein two gowns, value 15 s; a shawl, value 10 s.; a bonnet, value 18 s., and a hat, value 18 s., the goods of Ann Floyer.
   ANN FLOYER. I am a widow, and lodge at John Langford's, Little Earl-street, Seven-dials. I have the second floor front room. On the the 23d of August, about five o'clock in the morning, I awoke, and these things were then safe in my room; I fell asleep, and awoke again at seven, and they were gone; my daughter was in bed with me: my son goes out at half-past five, and he unlocked the door - the house is all let out; the prisoner is a stranger.
   WILLIAM HENRY LLOYD. I am a pawnbroker. On the the 24th of August, between three and four o'clock in the afternoon, the prisoner came to pawn a gown; but having had information, I stopped it; he ran out, leaving it behind, but a watchman was coming by, and stopped him; and in about three minutes a duplicate was brought to me belonging to Graham and Co.
 Prisoner. Q. Did I not ask you to let me go out, as the woman was outside who gave it to me to pawn - A. No; he said it belonged to his wife, who lived in Daker-street: I said I should go to Daker-street, and he immediately ran out.
   ANN FLOYER. I live with my mother, and was asleep when this happened; one gown hung by the bed-side, the other was on the table - and the other things about the room.
   LEONARD NEEDES. I am shopman to Graham and Co. On the 24th of August the prisoner pawned a gown for 3 s. 6 d. about half-past three o'clock in the afternoon, in the name of John Harris.
 Prisoner. Q. Did I not come in twice about it - A. He came in first and asked 4 s. on it; I said I could lend but 3 s. 6 d., which he refused, but came in two or three minutes, and said he would take it.
   BENJAMIN TIMBRELL. I am a constable. I took him into custody at Lloyd's.
