MR. PAYNE conducted the Proceeution.
EDWARD HENRY HATCHETT. I am apprenticed to Mr. Blake. on
his premises at Enfield on Saturday evening, 17th May—there is a stac in
the brewery, and a bopper, where the malt is when it comes from the mill
previous to its going into the mash-tub—I saw the hopper that night, between
8 and 9 0'clock—there was then about eight quarters of malt in it, and the
surface of it was level—it was to be used in business on the Monday morning
—on Sunday morning, between 11 and 12, I went with Collins to examine
it, and about a bushel was gone, and the suråce of the malt had a hole in it
—it was not in the even state that it had been before—the prisoner was in
Mr. Blake's employ, as under-stage man.
Cross-examined by Mg. COCKLE. Q. What becomes of the malt after being
used? A. It is thrown out of the mash-tub into a pit, and sold for grains
for pigs or cows—tbe men were at work on that Saturday night—they did
not leave till• about 12 0'clock—I cannot tell bow many persons were in
the brewery that night, for I was gone to bed between 10 and 11 ; about
twelve men are generally employed there—they leave at different times—some
•ere out with drays.
ME. PAYNE. Q. Where it this hopper? A. Just over the mash-tub—
the draymen do not go there at all—the prisoner and the stage-man had
access to that part.
CHARLES LANGLEY NORMAN. I am under tun-man at the brewhouse.
On Sunday morning, 18th May, I was in the brewhouse, about 9 0'clock—I
went up to the stage to weigh the beer, which is done by an instrument—the
hopper had then about eight quarters of malt in it—the prisoner was on the
stage with a man named Hummerston—l have not seen Hummerston since ;
he had been on the premises the day before to assist the prisoner—the upper
stage-man was not there that morning—be had been there late at night, till
about 4 in the morning.
