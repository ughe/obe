   Q.  Are you able to say at what time the prisoner came home? A.  Yes, I think it was past two o'clock, I am quite sure—when he came in I said, "Richard, it is morning"—he said, "It is early"—he then went into his bed room—he did not fasten the door—he had no fastening inside—on the Tuesday morning he got up at half-past seven o'clock—I had been up ever since six o'clock, and was getting my husband's breakfast—I bad not gone to sleep after the prisoner came in—I was weaning my baby, and did not go to sleep any more—my baby was so restless, I got out of bed three times—it was soon daylight after he came home—it was moonlight when he came home, it was very light—I was up three times after be came in, before six o'clock, and I did not go to sleep—I began weaning my baby that day and she was very ill—I watched the morning, and I know it was towards morning when he came in—I am quite sure it was near three o'clock—we have no clock in the house—I am quite sure it was nearer three than two o'clock, but I say two o'clock.
   COURT . Q.  How came you to say between one and two o'clock before? A.  I said "from one o'clock"—I was sure it was quite towards morning, because it was so soon daylight.
   Q.  That is no answer to why you should say between one and two o'clock? A.  I cannot speak nearer than I have—I know it was soon morning, and I did not go to sleep after he came in—it was very bright moonlight when he came in.
   MR. BALLANTINE . Q.  You say you were in the room at the time the prisoner came into it in the morning? A.
