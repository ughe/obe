SARAH MORLEY. At the time I was examined I lived in Union-street, Borough; I knew the deceased by living in the same house for seventeen weeks. On the Sunday before the Saturday on which he died, the pri soner was in my room, and our conversation began about the newspaper—I told her a lady and gentleman and their servant had been poisoned with arsenic, by their daughter—she asked me whether I knew how they sold it, I whether in liquid or powder—nothing was said about the name it was called by—I saw the deceased on the morning he died—I cannot tell what time, it was before dinner—he was sick when 1 first went into the room—he brought something up, but what I do not know—the prisoner was there at that time—she said he brought up every thing she gave him—I asked her why she did not have a doctor—she said, "We owe money"—I said, "Why, there are parish doctors and dispensary doctors; why not have a doctor?"—the deceased said, "Why not me have a doctor?"—she said, "You don't want to be physicked and tortured about"—he had his hand on his chest—I did not hear him complain of anything at that time—on the Monday after the Saturday on which he died I was conversing with Mrs. Parfitt, whom Mrs. Bowler calls her mother—Mrs. Bowler was present—I told her that he had said, "Why not have a docto?"—she said, "He did not say so; and if you say that, you will hang me, for you will be called on the Jury"—on the Sunday morning the day after his death, I was clean ing the room, after laying him out, and brought a half-pint mug off the drawers' in the room, and took it into the kitchen—there was some tea in it—I emptied it in the yard, on the stones by the water-closet—I saw a sort of sediment in the bottom of the mug, of a dirty white, like magnesia—I then took the mug up stain to my husband, to show him—he told me to take it down and place it in the kitchen, and I did so—I did not wash it out—I never saw it afterwards—a man named Dunster lodged in the house—Mrs. Bowler told me at one time, just before Mr. Bowler's death, that if he (Dunster) got married to the young woman be was paying bis addresses to, she would destroy their happiness—on one occasion, previous to Mr. Bowler's death, she told me that Dunster would stop five years for her—I never saw her and Dunster go out together.
   Cross-examined. Q.  The prisoner was of a very cheerful and lively disposition, was she not? A.
