GEORGE
George's Church, on the same side of the way-we got there a little before
four, as soon as it was open-we stopped at the coffee-stall from one till
near four, drinking coffee and eating bread and butter--the prisoner
sometimes works at Mr. Rofy's-he ought to have been at work there that
Monday-he did not go home all night-he was along with me-We were
drinking beer at the public-house till about half past five, and then came
back again to Camberwell-we got back at seven-no one was in our com-
pany at the public-house-I believe I was smoking a pipe as we came
along the Camberwell-road to the Elephant and Castle-I cannot tell
whether the prisoner was smoking or not-we met a policeman--he did not
turn his light on to look at us that I swear-we met him at the corner of
Southampton-street, near the Duke of Clarence--we did not meet one near
the gate.
MR.PRENDERGAST. Q. Did you speak to the policeman ? A. Yes-
it was Hopper-I changed half-a-sovereign at the Waterloo Arms in the
evening-I had 23. or 38. left in the morning.
MR. PAYNE. Q. Were you alone, or was anybody with you when you
saw and spoke to that policeman ? A. The other two went on--I did not
ask the policeman if he had seen a young man pass, dressed like a brick
layer, whom I called Bill-I met this policeman on the Saturday night,
and asked him that question, but not on the Sunday night-I spoke to
him, but what I said I do not know-it was at the corner just against the
Duke of Clarence, about five or ten minutes past twelve.
ME. PRENDERGAST. Q. Did you ever see the prisoner in such a cap as
that? A. Never, and these shoes are not his-I never observed him with
any like them.
Court. Q. Where did he go to his work at seven o'clock in the
morning? A. To Mr. Rofy's, in George-street-he worked there regu.
larly in the watering time-I know Wells- I do not know whether it was
at the same place where Wells was to go to work.
MR. PAYNE. Q. You do not mean to say that he did work at Mr.
Rofy's that day? A. No, he did not-he went with the intent to do so-
I saw him afterwards on the Monday- I did no work that day--we spent
the greater part of the day at the Waterloo Arms.
REED. I am landlord ot the Anchor and Hope. I have no
knowledge of the prisoner, but the last witness was at my house, with two
other young men and two females, from twelve o'clock to perhaps ten
minutes before twelve-I will not say exactly the time they had half a
pint of gin and cloves, for which Barton paid--they looked clean, particu-
larly Barton - he looked very clean-neither of them looked as if they had
come out of a chimney-I think they had caps on, and they were smoking
-Barton lighted his pipe at my gas before he went out.
- BOLTER. I keep the Waterloo Arms, in George-street. The
stable where the prisoner slept belongs to Mr. Bullock -a young man and
he slept together in a loft over the stable-I saw the prisoner on the
Monday morning, about seven o'clock-he had a cap on, and I think I
should have observed if he had been without shoes-he did not look as if
he had just come out of a chimney-he was dressed as usual-he was in
the habit of assisting my man to clean my pots, and I reprimanded him
well for not being there to do so--Mr. Rofy is a corn-chandler, opposite,
and contracts for watering the roads-I never saw the prisoner in such a
cap as this-he usually wore a blue cap.
