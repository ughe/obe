   Cross-examined by MR. DILL . I recognise Mountford. FREDERICK HENRY SNEAD. I am a boot and shoe maker of 86, Kenning ton Road—at the end of November last Sidney came there in the name of Charles Carter to take the house—I asked him to give me references, and afterwards received this letter. ( Giving a reference, R. Cramer, Esq., of Sutherland Street, South Belgravia . ) I wrote to them and received this reply: "Dear Sir,—In reply to your letter, C. Carter has rented under me and I have no hesitation in recommending him as a respectable tenant. Signed R. Cramer." Carter said that he was going to occupy it as a private house, he was not to let lodgings—he said that he would respect my wishes—I believed the reference to be a respectable person and that he had been a tenant of Cramer's for some years—when the quarter's rent became due I went, but could not find Carter—I met him in Kensington Road a day or two before he was taken in custody—I never got any rent, I have got possession of the house—I used to see Thomas Williams come up the area steps and Mrs. Williams or Dundas, they occupied the basement.
   Cross-examined by MR. DILL . They were ordinary lodgers, and I had no power over them.
   Cross-examined by Sidney.  My solicitor commenced an action against you—you made an appointment to see me on Thursday evening at 6 o'clock; you called on Wednesday, and I was not there—Inspector Marshall has some of your letters—I did not say that I did not mind what Mr. Carter was, so long as he paid me my rent—the people next day gave me information that you were coiners, and I sent a man to try and get into the house, but could not; I did get in one evening when you were away. JOHN HENRY SPENCER. I am a coal merchant, of Kennington Road—I got an order for two tons of the best Wallsend coal, in the name of Carter; I sent the coals, but did not get the money—I went a day or two afterwards and saw Mrs. Dundas, she said that she was a lodger there—I saw no one else; she said that Mr. Carter was away, she would tell him as soon as he cause in—I left a message with her that we should send for the coals and remove them, or sue for the money—about three days afterwards I knocked at the door and Mrs. Dundas came up the steps—I asked if Mr. Carter was there, she said "No," he had not come home—I heard some one in the coal-cellar; the door was locked, I went down and opened it, and saw the prisoner Williams in the cellar filling some sacks with the coals—I said "What are you doing there? you are sacking the coals"—he rushed out, and said "You speak of suing for the coals or fetching them away; the first b—that comes down these stairs I will knock his head off"—I think he was sober—after he had finished he locked the door, and turned to me and said "Now you can try if you like"—I never got the money or the coals, JOSEPH WILLIS.
