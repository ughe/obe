Its points are easy of comprehension, and its principles are honest. It does not offer concessions for one class of men, and refuse them to another; nor docs it involve the ruin of one set of men, as the necessary basis upon which another set are to flourish. Can there be any sedition in demanding for every man of sane mind, untainted with crime, and at the age of maturity, a voice in the election of the national representatives? Can it be sedition to demand security for that voice? Is it seditious to require the people's representatives to give an account of their delegation at shorter periods than they have hitherto done? or will it appear seditious to demand that a man of talent, without property, should have an equal right to a scat in the people's parliament which the man with property, but without talent, now possesses? Is it seditious to require that that man shall be remunerated for his labour? Or can you call it sedition to demand that each division of the kingdom be as nearly as possible equally repre sented? Yet such, Gentlemen, is the sum total of all the points of the Charter—the meaning; of the principles of this new philosophy, which has frightened the Sovereign, alarmed the Ministry, maddened the aristocracy, and distracted the middle classes, but now reigns paramount in the minds of the people. Depend upon it, Gentlemen, when the people understand and adopt a line of policy, neither scaffolds, nor dungeons, nor shackles, will arrest their onward career; nor will unprincipled oppression annihilate their power. You may do as Castlereagh did, thirty years ago, with the Radicals in the west of Scotland, when he emptied the gaols and bridewells of their criminal inmates, and set the felon free, that he might make room for honest Reformers: and although he brought some to the block, and sent some few into exile, yet radicalism prospered, and at this day stands high in the estimation of political reformers.  2046. WILLIAM SMITH and ELIZABETH BROWN , burglariously breaking and entering the dwelling-house of Joseph Eden , and stealing 2 shirts, 1 pair of stockings, and 1 handkerchief, value 5 s  . 6 d  .; his goods.—2nd COUNT  , for receiving the same.
   MR. CHARNOCK conducted the Prosecution. ELIZABETH EDEN. I am the wife of Joseph Eden, of Richmond. On the evening of 17th July I was the last person up—I saw all the fastening secure about twelve o'clock—next morning I came down about seven—I was the first person up, and found the house in very great confusion—the kitchen window was open, and a pane of glass broken, sufficiently large for a person to put their hand through and undo the window—I missed a pair of my son's boots, two shirts, a pair of stockings, a blue and white cotton handker chief, and a tin box, containing about 18 d . or 2 s . in money—they are my husband's and son's property—I had seen them safe the previous night—the shirts had been washed, and were wet—I know the stockings by my own darning, and the shirts are marked by me, "J. E.," my husband's initials—I know the handkerchief by the pattern, and its being a little torn.
