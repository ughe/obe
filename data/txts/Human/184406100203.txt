   Cross-examined. Q.  Was that the till? A.  Yes, it was kept there—I was present when the policeman found a paper containing arsenic—no one directed him to that drawer—it was not the first place he went to—he came to search the house—there was a quantity of arsenic in it—it looked as if it had not been untied at all—I had a violent fit of coughing in the course of the morning—it was after that I felt the burning in my throat—the coughing might have produced it—my husband is a kind man—he had flogged my son about a week before this—he frequently corrected him when he required it—my son does not remember an injury long, if he did I would flog him equally to his father—he is too forgetful, or he would not so soon transgress again—he was never flogged severely enough to remember it long—I did not swallow more than a teaspoon-full of the tea—my little boy told me that a portion of the contents of the cup were thrown on the carpet—I do not know whether the doctor was told of it. WILLIAM TIFFEN ILET. I am assistant to Mr. Cooper, surgeon of Guy's Hospital. I remember the prosecutrix bringing a breakfast cup to the Hos pital—I poured off the liquid—there was a sediment, which was analysed—it was three or four grains of finely powdered arsenic—in my judgment that would most likely not be sufficient to cause death—one or two grains more would very likely do it—I administered an emetic, but found no arsenic among the contents of the stomach—I have examined the contents of the paper produced—it is arsenic, and is labelled arsenic.
   Cross-examined. Q.  If I am rightly informed, one thousandth part of a grain, by a proper test, might be discovered? A.  Yes—not the slightest ves tige was found in the stomach—I was not aware that a portion of the con tents of the cup were poured on the carpet—if that had contained arsenic, it might easily have been ascertained—I think the tea poured off from the ar senic contained none.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  Would the skim have contained arsenic necessarily? A.  Not necessarily.
   COURT . Q.  Would there be a portion of it in a state of solution? A.  In the tea there was not the least trace of arsenic—tea dissolves a very small portion of arsenic, indeed—it would sink after being stirred, and become sedi ment. DONALD MURRAY( policeman. ) I went to the prisoner's house in Ber mondsey-street, and examined, among other places, the drawer where the till is—I found the paper I have produced in the back part of it.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Who directed you to the drawer? A.  The wife.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  Had you examined other places first? A.  Yes—there was a case of drawers in the shop—I commenced at one side, and began to search the whole; and she said she knew it was in the drawer that was locked. WILLIAM HILL. I am a chemist, and live in Bermondsey-street. I know the prisoner, and have been in the habit of selling him small quantities of arsenic, as well as soda—it is four or five weeks ago since I sold him any—he told me it was to destroy rats—the parcel produced has my label on it—it is about a pennyworth of arsenic.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you know him personally? A.  I have known him between five and six years—I never heard anything against him—this appears in the state it was when I sold it.
