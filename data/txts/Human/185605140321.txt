JOHN ROBERT REYNOLDS. ( policeman, R  289). On the evening of 14th April I saw the four prisoners in High-street, Sydenham—I apprehended Ponman and John Reynolds—Ingram took the other two—I searched them at the station; on Ponman I found a half sovereign, a piece of paper, 7 s . 6 d . in silver, and 5 d . in copper—the half sovereign fell as I went to lay it on, the desk; whether it fell from the paper I could not tell—I also found a tobacco box and a knife on Ponman; and on John Reynolds a tobacco box—when they were charged with the robbery, Ponman said he knew nothing of it—the others did not say anything in my hearing.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did not each of them say they knew nothing of the robbery? A.  Not in my presence or hearing—I recollect no one speaking but Ponman at the station—the paper may have been separate from the half-crown, for aught I know—there was a piece of brown paper in his pocket, something similar to what sugar is wrapped in—there was nothing else in the same pocket but a piece of string—the tobacco box was in a different pocket, but I have kept the paper in it since—the purse was found in a gentleman's garden—I did not see it found—the person who found it pointed out the spot to me, but could not state the day on which he found it—it was about half a mile from where I took the prisoners into custody—Ponman was with his barrow when I met him—he had it nearly filled with iron ware, shovels, and hoops, and things—the other boys were with him—I did not see that they had anything.
   Witnesses for the Defence. JOSEPH JOHN HUSSEY. I am a blacksmith, of No. 3, Charlotte-street, Turville-street, Bethnal-green—I have known Ponman two or three years—he has been working for me for the last five months—I always considered him honest and industrious—I never knew anything against him.
   Cross-examined by MR. LILLEY . Q.  How came he to have his head cropped so? A.  He has been ill since his imprisonment, and has had his head shared—I live near him—he is married—I know all the other prisoners perfectly well, and have done so for two or three years—Ponman worked for me up to the time of his being taken into custody—I sent him out on that day to dispose of goods—he had with him about 3 cwt of goods, worth between 5 l . and 6 l .—I got the goods back from the policeman at the station—about 1 l . worth had been sold—he has been in the habit of travelling for me with goods in that way, daily, for five months, to every part within twenty miles of London, and he used to account to me daily—I have known the Reynolds from their infancy, and never knew any harm of them—they are employed by their father, who is a hawker of hardware—Gore was employed by Ponman to help push his barrow—he lives in the same street. JAMES REYNOLDS. I am a hawker in Charlotte-street, Church-street, Shoreditch—the two prisoners are my sons—they go out hawking for me—they have always been well conducted—I sent them out on this occasion with 8 s . or 9 s . worth of cinder shovels, funnels, and other things, and they sold as many as came to 7 s . 6 d . in one place, Mr. Charles's, who keeps the Coal club in Woodbine-grove, Anerley, ana here is his writing to prove it ( pro  ducing a paper)
 (  Several other witnessed deposed to the prisoner's good characters. ) JOHN ROBERT REYNOLDS. re-examined.  I have been on duty at Sydenham nearly two years—that is not far from Anerly—I do not know Anerley at all—I cannot tell whether there is a Mr. Charles keeping the Coal club there. NOT GUILTY.
