GEORGE WILLIAM BINGHAM. I am a Lieutenant in the first battalion
of the Royal Artillery. I knew the deceased-he was Sergeant-major of
the second battalion--he was not under me-on Sunday morning, the
26th May, about half past ten o'clock, I got up from my breakfast table in
my own room on the ground floor, and looked out of window-I saw the
Sergeant-major moving along towards the private parade, where his guard
was falling-in-I saw the prisoner with his musket to his shoulder levelled
towards the deceased-he was about twenty-five yards from me at the
time, and about eight or nine yards from the Sergeant-major-before I
could think, I heard the explosion of the musket which was pointed at
the Sergeant-major, and I saw the Sergeant-major fall flat on his face on
the ground-his back was towards the prisoner and likewise towards me-
when I saw him fall, I had not the least doubt in the world he was a dead
man-the prisoner stood still and seemed to poise his musket, to hold it to
his shoulder to see if he had perpetrated the act-I rushed from my room
and cried out, “Seize that man," and before I got up to him he was secured
by two non-commissioned officers-he was standing between Sergeants
Polson and Patton when I got up the moment I arrived I put my hand
on his arm and said, "What could induce you to commit such an act ?"-be
said, "I have meant it some time, or I have thought of it some time"-I
cannot be certain which--one or the other I am perfectly sure of-I have
been in Court while Patton was examined--the account he has given of the
manner in which the ball cartridges are given out for convict service, and the
duty of returning them, is perfectly correct-when a man mounts duty during
divine service, it is not his duty to have his musket charged at all--he should
have nothing in it-there is a difference between ball cartridges and blank car
tridges-ball cartridges are made up in whitey-brown paper, and blank car-
tridges in blue paper--it was not his duty to have any cartridge in his
musket, but ten in his pouch, made up in packets of five-there is never
any convict guard on Sunday-the prisoner never was on convict guard-
he was once, as I have heard, warned for it, but was never on it-Her Ma-
jesty's birth-day was celebrated on the 23rd of May-the men fired the
usual feu-de-joie on that occasion, which is three rounds of blank cartridges
- they are delivered out for that express purpose-no more and no less
than three to each individual.
Q. Did you examine the body of the unhappy man who fell ? A. As
soon as I left the prisoner I went towards the deceased, and made way for
him to be carried into one of the barrack-rooms-he was laid on his back,
and was stone dead.
Cross-examined. Q. The ten cartridges you speak of are ball cartridges?
A. Yes-according to the regulations every man carries ten rounds in his
pouch at all times--that is all they are to carry except when they go out
to fen-de-joie-they are left at home in case of accident-the ten are for
them if called suddenly on any duty-I do not know myself when the
prisoner had his ten served out to him--they are put back into his pouch,
know.
