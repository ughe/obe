I was called to the deceased's home about nine o'clock on Saturday evening, the 22nd of Aug.—I found her up stairs—she complained of great thirst, constant vomiting, violent pain of the bowels, and involuntary diarrhoea—she did not complain of her throat—I felt her pulse, and found that the power of life was rapidly sinking—I asked her what she had taken, and I understood, I think, from Mrs. Bywater that she had taken some jalap—when I arrived at the house the prisoner sent the boy into the next-door neighbour, Mrs. Bywater—she acme in immediately, and went up stairs first, and I followed her—I did not learn from the deceased that she had taken anything—it was Mrs. Bywater, in her presence—I immediately gave her some brandy and water, and also requested some one to go to my house for some medicine, to lose no time; and I gave her some astringent medicine, as the diarrhoea was so severe—I considered from the symptoms, that it was an attack of cholera—I remained there some few minutes—I did not see her again—I was not sent for again—understand she died about one that morning—I saw the prisoner on the Sunday evening—I told him his wife was seriously ill, dangerously ill; and I would do all I possibly could for her—he did not make any reply—he said he would send for the medicine directly, and Mrs. Bywater said she would come for it.
   Q.  Did he give you any reason to believe that his wife had taken arsenie or any poison whatever? A.  None whatever—he heard what I said—he said nothing at all to me about the situation of his wife—he was down stairs when I saw him—I only saw him once—he opened the door to me, I think, when I went in—I waited while he sent next door for Mrs. Bywater—I should think I was alone with him, altogether, seven or eight minutes—I then west up stairs with Mrs. Bywater—when I came down again I found him down stairs, and then I said that his wife was seriously ill, in great danger—he said nothing to me about her, on either occasion—I examined the body after wards—I took out the stomach, and gave it to Dr. Taylor—Mrs. Bywater applied to me on the following morning for a certificate, which I refused—I was the first person that opened the body—I had no doubt then from the appearances that presented themselves, that her death was caused by poison—I have no doubt of it now—the stomach was highly inflamed, and also the duo denum—I did not examine the throat—there were appearances which justi fied me in taking out the stomach, and submitting it to a chemical analysis.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you ask the prisoner any question as to what his wife had taken in the course of the day? A.  No, nor did I ask Mrs. By water—the woman herself was rapidly sinking, she was hardly able to articu late—I remained there seven or eight minutes, altogether—not less than that—I think it was seven minutes—I did not inquire what food she had taken that day.
