 Have you got that letter? - No, I went to the lady, and she told me she was sorry to inform me that she could not have the phaeton, for she had information by her friends, that none but ladies of easy virtue rode in phaetons; I told the lady I had served a number of ladies of family and character with phaetons; the lady told me she would call, and bring a gentleman with her in the course of that day, or the next; I believe it was the same day she called with Mr. Martin; he asked me where the chariot was; I told him it was at the painter's; he asked me if he could see it; I told him yes; I went with him to the painter's to see it; Mrs. Breedon then said, I had made a great alteration in it, and asked me at the same time what would be the expences, if she was to take her chariot again, with the money I had laid out upon it; I told her, I thought it would be about twenty, or twenty-four pounds, the way I meant to finish it; she then said, she should have an objection to riding in the chariot, as it was her wedding chariot, and she should be affected with the thoughts of her husband; she asked if I could not get her another chariot; I told her at some expence I could, I knew a lady at Bath, that was going to throw up her carriage; but she came to town, and kept her carriage on; she said, in the course of time you may meet with one; I said I would endeavour to meet with one; I received another letter from her by this  Elizabeth Harris, and I waited on the lady; and she said, I have considered of it, and let my friends say what they will, I insist on having the phaeton; this was about a fortnight after Mr. Martin had been with her to my house; then Mrs. Breedon ordered a servant to fetch a silver-waiter, for there was some difference she thought in her arms or crest, and a candlestick was brought to examine it; I told her I could not find out any, without I was to examine at the Herald's-office; she then desired a candle, and she would give me the impression of her arms and crest in wax, and the name of her husband she gave me in writing, to examine the family arms; and sent the servant to fetch the livery, to see if some lace she had seen in my shop was near the livery; she then told me to go on with it as I thought proper, till just before Christmas, which I did; and a few weeks before Christmas, I received of her a note to wait on her, and she told me, she had put by all thoughts of having a phaeton, and I must give her thirty pounds for her chariot; I told the lady I was not indebted to her thirty pounds, nor no such sum, but when the phaeton was finished and delivered, she was indebted to me fifteen guineas;
