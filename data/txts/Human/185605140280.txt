   COURT . Q.  If the prisoner was at the Post Office that morning would a Gloucester letter for Hackney pass through the department where he works? A.  Yes—he was one of the sorters in that room—there are a great many sorters there, perhaps fifty—there were some other fragments of paper given me besides these, but not with writing on them—I may have thrown them away supposing they did not belong to the envelope. THOMAS GAPES. I am an inspector of letter carriers of the London district at the General Post Office. I was there on the morning of 15th April—I saw the prisoner in the Sorting Office—I did not notice him until my atten tion was called to him by the officer—that was, I think, about half-past 8 o'clock, or shortly after—I do not exactly recollect the time—I have the signing book here—the men sign this book when they arrive—it is kept in the Signing Office—it is no part of my duty to see to the signatures—Mr. Cook does that.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Is there any one here who can tell me whether the prisoner was in the sorters' room about half past 7 o'clock that morning? A.  I saw him there about that time—I cannot say to a few minutes—that was before the officer called my attention to him—it was about that time—where they sit depends upon the nature of their duty—Mr. Cook sees to that—there is no fixed time for breakfast—they get it as they can. THOMAS BURRELL COOK. I am superintendent of the Sorting Department—I saw the prisoner in the Sorting Office on the morning of 15th April; he was there at 5 o'clock.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Was that the only time you saw him there that morning? A. No ; I saw him at 25 minutes past 7 o'clock in the morning—he left then—he was there from 5 o'clock till 25 minutes past 7 o'clock—he was then taken out of the room by the officers—I only remember Peak coming to the room once, that was when he pointed him out—he might have come in without my seeing him, if I was engaged—I know I am accurate about the time—I know how the duty progresses every minute—the prisoner could not have been absent without my knowledge—I cannot say he was there every minute, or every five minutes. ELLEN ATTWATER. I am the wife of Thomas Attwater, of Brockworth Gonrt, about four miles from Gloucester. Our letters are left at the house of Mr. Watts, a tailor in the village, where they are called for by the postman—on the 14th of April I wrote a letter to my father, enclosing a sovereign in two cards, one of Mr. G. T. Attwater's and one of Mrs. Frederick Davidge—I put it in an envelope, and addressed it, "Mr. Woolbert, No. 2, Palace-cottages, Wells-street, South Hackney, near London"—the papers on this first piece of tissue paper form part of that envelope—I find here Mr. Attwater's card entire, and a portion of Mrs. Davidge's—I believe these to be the cards in which I enclosed the sovereign—I put two stamps on the letter, because I thought it might be over weight—this other piece of tissue paper contains nearly all the letter I wrote to my father—the sovereign I sent was a George and Dragon—I did not notice the date of it—my father is a great invalid—a person named Lizzy attends on him.
   COURT . Q.  Look at the word above the word "London," on that envelope; there is apparently an "n," can you account for that at all? A.  It is "nr," for near  London—I wrote that in the same line as "South Hackney."
