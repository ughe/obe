I rushed over the arm of the chair, and twisted round to get away from him; when I turned round he was struck over the head - the daughter called out."Oh, father, give me the razor!" and took it out of his hand: I did not see the blow, for he was struck, I suppose as I turned round - he had got his hand to his head, and was walking away; his daughter Maria shut the razor, and put it in her bosom; the deceased went down stairs - he walked down stairs; my son staid in his own room - he was not out of it; the deceased did not fall down at all while he was in the room - they asked me to go down into the deceased's room, as his head was cut, and I went; it was about ten minutes before I got into his room, I observed his head was cut, and he put his hand to it; I saw it was hurt, but it did not bleed much - he was standing up; his daughter was there, and did not know what to put to it - I had a little bottle of oil, which had cured me of a broken arm, and I put some to it; he went to bed, and arose up as the watchman called three o'clock - he appeared ill; he said his head ached very bad - it was bound with his handkerchief; there was no assistance till the afternoon, when I came home - he appeared in the morning to be in a sound sleep, and in the afternoon I went with his daughter to Spital-square; I waited while she went in - she came out, and said the gentleman told her he might never awake again, and advised his being taken to the hospital; he was taken there.
 Q. Had he rushed towards you with the razor in his hand? A. Yes, and said he would do for us all; I never saw any body in such a vehement passion in my life.
 Cross-examined. Q. Did Maria share in the drink at the King and Queen? A. Yes, she drank porter and gin, and was dancing and singing; she was there twice, and drank each time - she had beer and gin at the Fleur-de-lis.
 COURT. Q. When the deceased came up with the razor in his hand, who was nearest to him? A. I was, for I was by the door - it is a smallish room.
 MR.  CHARLES BURNETT. I am a surgeon. I saw the deceased on Wednesday evening. at the Hospital; he had an injury on his head, which might have been given by a piece of wood - he lived till about nine o'clock next morning; I examined the body the following day, and have no doubt the injury on his head occasioned his death.
 Cross-examined. Q. If he had been drinking and dancing all day, might not apoplexy take place? A. It might, but apoplexy did not cause his death - his skull was fractured; I do not recollect seeing the daughter.
   JOSEPH HAMMOND. I apprehended the prisoner, and showed him the wood; he confessed striking the man with it, and was sorry for it - he said he believed that was the piece of wood, and he did not do it with intent to kill him.
   DANIEL HANKS. I am a watchman. I was sent for before the blow was struck; they were quarrelling, and I told them to be quiet - in about twenty minutes I was fetched again; Maria was inside the passage, accusing the prisoner with striking her father, and he replied "Where is the razor, you b-h."
 Prisoner's Defence. I have a witness.
   WILLIAM BARTON. I am the prisoner's brother-in-law, I met Maria Harris at a public-house in White Lion-street - she told me to tell the prisoner not to come home, as her sister might be angry; she said the deceased was going to cut the prisoner's mother's throat.

 859.  CATHERINE SEDDON was charged on the Coroner's Inquisition, with the wilful murder of Eliza Joyce.
   MARY ANN SIMMONS. I live in Turnmill-street, and sell fish. Between eight and nine o'clock in the morning, I saw Eliza Joyce in Cock-court- she was very tipsy, and the prisoner was with her; they were both coming up the court, singing, and very tipsy - they could hardly walk- in five minutes afterwards I saw Joyce lying at the foot of a ladder in Cock-court; she was bleeding at the head very much, and quite insensible - she was taken to Dr. Cooper's, and then to St. Bartholomew's Hospital; I saw her there three days afterwards; she died about a week afterwards - she appeared very ill: I know she and the prisoner were acquainted; I never knew them quarrel.
 Cross-examined. Q. Was not the only entrance to the prisoner's room up this ladder? A. Yes; it is a common ladder, and very difficult to get up.
   ANN WARBOYS. I live in Cock-court, right opposite to where the prisoner lives; I heard a fall in the court, and went to the window - the deceased was then lying quite insensible by the ladder; how it happened I cannot tell: if there had been a struggle between her and the prisoner, I must have heard it - I did not hear them sing.
   THOMAS JOYCE. I am in the coal trade. My sister lived in Rose-court, I believe, but I did not go to see her till she was in the hospital; I visited her four days there, and saw her the day she died - she was perfectly insensible - the last time I saw her when sensible was on Sunday, the 23d; she had been there about a week; I know she expected she should die - she said so, and begged of me to come and see her, as she was sure she should never come out alive; I asked how it had happened - she said she had lost her shawl, and she was going after it - that a woman named Seddon had got it, and the woman struck her - that she went up the ladder after the woman, and the woman struck her when she was on the ladder, she then fell, and could say nothing more; I asked her if she was drunk before - her answer was, "No, I was perfectly sober."
