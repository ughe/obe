I went into the prisoner's service as clerk on the 4th of December, before the bankruptcy—that was in December, 1834—among other employment, I was in the habits of going to the Bank of England for the purpose of taking the monies taken in the shop to be ex changed for notes—I took monies to the bankers occasionally for him—I remember, on the Monday previous to the bankruptcy, the prisoner giving me cheques and cash to the amount of about 250 l .—on the Saturday I had taken to the London and Westminster bank about 215 l . in small notes and cash—my instructions were to get them exchanged for large notes.
   Q.  Was the transaction on the Monday at the Bank of England? A.  yes; he gave me small notes and cheques to the amount of 250 l  —I got partly money and partly notes for the cheques—I then proceeded to the Bank of England with the whole, and got large notes—the largest note I received was a £200 note—I had not put anything on the notes I brought from the bankrupt, and from the bankers in Lombard-street, till I got to the Bank, when I wrote "William Rotherham" on the top of the notes—(looking at some notes) here is the name on every one of these—I received a £200 note, and delivered it to the bankrupt—I do not remem ber what amount of cash I had in gold and silver, but I received a ticket like this (looking at one)for the gold—that was given under the gallery—I never received a £200 note for the bankrupt except on those occasions, on the Saturday before, when I took some money to the London and West minster Bank, and the £200 note which I got at the Bank of England.
   Cross-examined by MR. BODKIN . Q.  I see you wrote the name on the top; was it always your customs to do that with the notes you took? A.  Certainly, when I went on similar occasions, which I frequently did. WILLIAM TAYLOR. I am one of the clerks in the Bank of England. I have the book of entries here—at the date of 5th of January, last year, I find an entry of the payment of a £200 note, No, 1562, dated 18th December, 1834, paid to the name of Rotherham—I received twelve notes, amounting to 115 l . and a ticket from the tellers of 85 l . paid in cash—this is the cash ticket, and twelve notes which amount to 115 l .—these I delivered a £200 note of the number and date I mentioned—I posted the note, and William Higham paid out the note—the pay-clerk calls the number and date of the note.
   Cross-examined by MR. LEE . Q.  You do not speak as to the number and date of the notes you have before you? A.  No; the pay-clerk is liable to make a mistake certainly, I depend upon the sound I hear—Mr. Higham is the person who would pass the note from the box to the party applying—I call the number to him, I had the twelve notes and the teller's ticket—I enter them, asking the individual what we wishes—he replies a £200 note—I turn to my two hundreds, and say, "One 200—62, "which is chequed by my partner, and he gives it from my direction.
   Q.  But when you refer to four book do you look at the note to see that is corresponds with the number you call out? A.  No; I do not—I call out the note next in turn to be used, and in this case it was No. 1562.
