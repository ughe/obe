Twelve o'clock in the day—I noticed the two prisoner from among the others, as they were looking my way—the others had their backs toward me—I did not speak to them—I noticed them as I passed—they looked right in my face—I did not notice their dress, or whether they had hats or caps on—I did not see them again till the following Monday. FRANCIS RANDER. I live with a widow lady, who keeps a cheese monger's shop, in Middle-row—I lodge in Broad-street, exactly opposide the prosecutor's house. About one o'clock on Sunday afternoon, the 18th of March, I was at my window, and saw three men go into the passaged of the prosecutor's street door—Callaghan was one of them—be appeareds to me to have something under his arm like a bag, going in—directly after wards he came and stood at the door, and looked—the other two went inside—a few minutes afterwards they all three came out, Callaghen first, with something in a bag, and crossed the way.
   Cross-examined by MR. PAYNE . Q.  What part of your house were you in f A.  In the second-pair front—I was looking out of the window, hav ing a headach from being at work—there were not many people about on that side of the way—there was on the other side, coming from church. PATRICK KENNEDY. I am in the service of Mrs. Apted, a fishmonger and live in Middle-row, Hoi born. On Sunday, the 18th of March, I was walking by the prosecutor's shop, and saw three persons come out of the passage at the side of the shop—Sealey was one of them—I knew his before—I cannot say who the others were—he had something in his pocket, but what it was I could not see—I did not see either of them with a bag—I saw them come out of the passage, and shut the door after them—having suspicion, I went and pushed it open, and found the door leading to the parlour waa open—I beckoned to my fellow-servant—he came down, and I gave information to the police.
   Cross-examined by MR. BALLANTINE . Q.  What were you doing on this occasion? A.  Walking by—I was going to get my hair cut—it was one o'clock on Sunday morning—I was waiting for a man named Baker, who keeps a cab  —I was not in the habit of associating with Sealey—I used to drive a cab . and so did he—I was about six months on the stand—I did not see him every day—perhaps not above twice a month—I had not had any quarrel with him of any kind—I never had a quarrel with him about his getting a fare from me—he never got a fare from me, that I know of—I have not got a very good memory, and very likely I might have had a quarrel with him and forgotten it—I never told him I would do him a turn for what he had done to me—not that I know of—I will not swear I did not—as I told you before, I have got a very bad memory—I have not the least knowledge of telling him he should never drive a cab again, but I have a very bad memory.
