Cross-examined by MR. FULTON . I am giving this information in the interests of public justice—I did not plead guilty to the Old Kent Road burglary—I gave this information after my conviction—I had previously told the detective that if I was convicted I could give them valuable inform ation, and if not convicted I might not have given the information—3 convictions of felony were proved against me at the Surrey Sessions, on 6th May—I was convicted at Slough under the Prevention of Criminals Act, as an habitual criminal, and 2 previous convictions were proved against me—Mrs. Chandler was present at my conversation with Jones, bat I do not know whether she heard it—I heard her examined at the Southwark Police Court—she said she did not hear the conversation—I am not aware that any one was present at the conversation between Patterson, Jones, and myself, at the Elephant and Castle—it was at the public refreshment bar.
   Cross-examined by Emma Workman.  I saw you at your house in con versation with Patterson and your husband—I did not tell the police of this because you gave evidence against me.
   By the COURT . Mrs. Workman was a witness against me in the Old Kent Road burglary case. WILLIAM CHANDLER. I am undergoing penal servitude in Pentonville Prison—I was sentenced at the Surrey Sessions, April 9th, 1879, and had been in Wandsworth Prison from 5th November, 1877, to 3rd August, 1878—I knew Patterson seven or eight years ago—I did not know him personally, but a man named Charles Wells, of Camberwell, showed him to me—I have known Workman and his wife for some years—the witness Harding lodged in the name of Baker at 14, Cork Street, and he was lodging there in August, 1878—I have not seen Baker at my house in company with either of the other prisoners except Workman—Workman has been there hundreds of times, and visited Harding (or Baker) there a good many times—Baker and I were out housebreaking together almost daily—about the middle of August, 1878, I saw Patterson and Workman talking together at the Station Tavern, Beresford Street, Walworth Road, but I did not hear what they said—when we came away Workman said, "Do you know that party?" I said, "I have seen him before;" he said, "That's Pat, my brother-in-law. That's the party that's putting up the place in Westminster Bridge Road"—"putting up "a place means to commit a burglary Or robbery—Baker, Workman, and Dowthwaite, alias Rogers, were all out housebreaking together last year—Baker is the cleverest man—I have five or six times heard Baker and Workman, at my place, talking about Maudsley's place, Westminster Bridge Road, and concocting how to do it—I met Workman by the Crown, London Road, coming from Water loo, about the middle of August—I said, "Hallo! Arthur. What's yer game?" he said, "I have just been down to see Pat."
