appearance of the lungs was very unhealthy—the upper part of each lung was very congested, very loaded with extravasated blood, the bronchial vessels also very loaded with blood and the aesophagus—the blood in the lungs was partly fluid and partly coagulated—the liver was very unhealthy, of a very dark colour instead of its natural colour—there was a flaxidity about it, which showed a great deal of unnatural character—the gall-bladder was almost empty—there was scarcely any bile in it—it contained a little air apparently—the stomach appeared partially inflamed about the upper part, and there was minute, in all probability the blood might have come in partially from the veins of the stomach—that would not account for the whole—a large quantity must have come from the lungs, and got down the throat—the spleen and other organs were not quite healthy—there did not appear any inflammation about the bowels in general—the principal seat of disorder appeared the lungs and liver, and the irregular state of the bladder—in my opinion the flow of blood from the lungs arose from violence by blows or falls—the lungs being in a weak state they were susceptible—I can undertake to say that violence must have produced those effects—they could not have been produced to that extent without violence.
   Cross-examined by MR. BALLANTINE. Q.  How long had you attended this poor woman? A.  Several times in the course of the summer—she had taken medicine—she had stated to me before this that she had spit small quantities of blood, not to any extent—she told me that she occasionally expectorated blood—I mean by that, thrown it from her lungs—that might probably be occasioned by ulceration—there must have been some lesion of ves sels to give rise to it—there may be a lesion without a rupture, or the evacuation of blood, but it generally happens—I am able to say that the blood evacuated was venous blood, and not arterial—I did not ascertain what vessel was broken—there were a variety of vessels, towards the upper part of the lungs, not any of the main vessels—there is no difficulty in ascertaining whether blood is venous or arterial—the colour of venous blood is dark, and arterial blood is red or florid—at the upper part of the lungs, the veins and arteries mix and inter sect—I can hardly say that there were no arterial vessels ruptured, probably there might have been, but the greater amount was venous blood—I cannot say whether that was from the diseased part of the lung, as the lungs alto gether were very weak, and participated in point of texture to a similarity of construction—there had been ulceration—it occasionally happens with the lungs in such a state, that the cough may rupture the vessels and produce great extravasation of blood—it is almost universally the mode in which such patients die—I know that the deceased was subject to a frequent cough—I do not consider, from the appearances of the lungs, that a violent fit of coughing would have produced the great effusion that I witnessed; the lungs were apparently not in that very tender state as to produce it—they were in a weakened state—external violence must be a considerably greater strain
