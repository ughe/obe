you come for my shoes?"—he said, "No, I came to call you," and that he was ordered to call me at five o'clock—I said, "I gave no orders"—I saw him distinctly, and think it impossible I can be mistaken—I knew his voice, and saw his side face—he walked out of the room, saying, "I have made a mistake then, for I had to call somebody"—being one of the servants I did not at first think anything of it, but in a few minutes I examined my trow  sers, which I found just where I had put them—the pocket was not turned out, but unbuttoned and drawn up towards the top, and my bag and money were gone—I got up, went down stairs—I sat in the coffee-room, and gave him my shoes to bpush, which he did—he put them down, and turned to go out—I then asked him why he came to call me—he said he had not been in my room at all—I told him I had lost a parcel—he said he knew nothing of it—I then told him what it was—he said there was more in the house than him, there was sweeps sweeping the chimney, and people had got up and left about five o'clock, he did not know who they were, and there was another servant—I said, was he up?—he said "No"—he asked if he should call him—I said, "Do as you please"—he said, "It might be another person"—I said, "No, I am satisfied it is you"—I then considered I might as well see the other, though I was certain of him—I said I should go and stop payment of the notes—when I returned the waiter and prisoner were both there—when I saw the waiter I was positive it was not him—when I charged the prisoner with it about six o'clock he said, "Have you searched? you may have dropped it in the room; you was out in the evening, perhaps it might be picked out of your pocket"—I said, "No, I am quite certain it was safe when I went to bed"—the chambermaid afterwards produced my bag, containing three 5 l . notes—the gold was gone—I had been at the Nag's Head last June, but should not have known the prisoner if I had met him in the street before that, as a servant of the house—I am satisfied he is the man who was in my bed-room.
   Cross-examined by MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  When he said there was another servant, did you say you could tell better if you saw the ser vant? A.  I said "If I saw you together, I should be more positive"—I should know whether it was the other or not—it was broad day light when he came into my room, and I had drawn the window curtain at four o'clock—the prisoner said it was not him—I said, "You had a short jacket when you was in the room"—he said he had had no other dress on that day—I said if he chose to put on a jacket and come into the room he might, but he did not—I said at six o'clock I should go and stop payment of the notes, but I did not, it was too early—I went to the barber's, and got shaved—I re turned in about an hour—I did not tell the barber about this, nor meet a policeman—when I saw the other servant I said, "I am satisfied it was not you"—Mrs. Harris was ill—I did not like to disturb the house—about nine o'clock I said I must go out, I would leave it in their hands till I returned to take what steps were right—I had several things to do—I came back about three o'clock, and then they spoke to the policeman, and I gave him in charge—I found my breeches not moved from under the pillow, but heaved up so that they could get at the pocket—the pillow was more than two feet long,
