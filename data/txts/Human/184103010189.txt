   Cross-examined. Q.  Where? A.  At Gillingham, near Chatham—I did not know her after she was married—I saw her in town, and she said she was married. GEORGE MORPHET. I am a painter. I know the prisoner, and I know William Huggins, who sits here—I know he and the prisoner lived together as man and wife, at the latter end of 1837, in New-square, Minories—they were not living at my house—they left New-square, and went to Richard-son-street, Long-lane, Bermondsey, to Mr. Southerby's, the hatter.
   Cross-examined. Q.  What time was it they left? A.  I think about March, 1838, to the best of my recollection—they lived together exactly on the same terms all the time they were there, which was from about October. FRANCIS SOUTHERBY. I am a hatter, and live at Bermondsey. On the 26th of March, 1838, the prisoner and William Huggins came to live in my apartments—they lived as man and wife till the middle of August in the same year—I very often heard the prisoner speak of Huggins as her husband, and sometimes as "William."
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you see Clothier? A.  No, he has not been to me, the policeman has—the prisoner has often said of a night, "Has my husband come in?" or "Has William come home?" MARIA DUFFEL. I am the wife of William Duffel, we live in Union-street East-lane, Walworth. I have known the prisoner and William Huggins for rather better than two years—I knew them all in 1839 and 1840—they were living at No. 8, Wellington-place, East-lane—I was in the habit of going to work and char for them—I breakfasted there the first twelve months—I remember the prisoner leaving home rather better than twelve months ago—she told me she was going to Gillingham to her mother—she staid away from the Sunday till the Wednesday—on her return she sent for me, and I found her sitting by the parlour-fire crying—she said, "Oh! Duffel"—I said, "What is the matter?"—"Oh! Duffel," said she, "I have done it"—I said, "In the name of God, what have you done t"—she said, "I have got married"—"To who, "I said—she said, "To Mr. Clothier"—I said, "Dear me, I should be very sorry to be as you are, for do you know if they come forward perhaps they may transport you"—she said she was not afraid of that, for they were too fond of her—I afterwards assisted her to move her goods out of the house where she was living with Mr. Huggins, to Mr. Clothier's.
   Q.  When she lived with Huggins, and before she went away, as she told you, to Gillingham, did she and Huggins live as man and wife? A.  Yes—she used to call him Bill , and he her Sarah—she one day showed me a certificate—she opened it, and I saw "William Huggins" and "Sarah Johnson" on it—she then put it into the copper-hole fire, as I was washing; that was the year before she was married—it is about thirteen months ago—when she put it into the fire I said, "Oh, what are you about?"—she said, "Never mind, money will get another"—I saw enough of it to know that it was a certificate of a marriage—I saw the name of a parish on it, but I could not see what—that was on the Thursday before.
