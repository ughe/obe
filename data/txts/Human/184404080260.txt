   Cross-examined. Q.  Have you on any previous occasion examined the head? A.  Constantly—to a certain extent it is uncertain, from the appear ance of the head, whether a person has died from apoplexy or external injury, but finding the woman in perfect health in other particulars, I do not consider it could be produced but from injury—she was not of a full habit of body—I considered her a well-formed woman, and in good condition—I examined the liver and all other parts—there was no disease of any kind—I did not smell any spirits at her mouth—I should imagine if a person had taken much spirits within an hour of death, I should find it by smelling the mouth—if a person died from drinking spirits, I consider I could perceive it all over the viscera—there were many marks of apoplexy about the head—I think I could take on myself to say she did not die from apoplexy—there was considerable congestion of the brain, but there was not apoplexy from the escape of blood—congestion of the brain would not produce death, considered alone, at that age and state of health—if she were in a great passion and under the influence of liquor, I should think it just possible—the bone was not injured, but death would be caused by concussion of the brain by a violent shaking of the brain, without anything else—I should say repeated blows would produce concussion of the brain—I was led to think she might have struck against a sharp edge of a partition—if a pail had been on the stairs, I think the injury would have been more extensive—the appearance of the brain led me to be lieve the whole I saw was from excitement.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  You found considerable extravasation of blood corre sponding with the external marks? A.  Yes, and from that I infer there was repeated blows, which induces me to say that concussion was the imme diate cause of death, and having heard she was sick, I infer it was a case of concussion; that is a usual symptom—in my judgment the blows would produce the effects I witnessed, and those effects would produce concussion of the brain—I consider she died from that—the congestion of the brain must have assisted; but that I consider was produced by excitement of the mind.
   COURT . Q.  The clotted blood had nothing to do with the death? A.  No. that only indicated that there had been external violence—I should judge from the appearances, she must have received blows of great importance—I dare not say she died entirely from external violence—it was the chief cause—sickness does occur in apoplexy; but it is more especially a corroborative symptom of concussion—I consider that a person of her age and health would scarcely die of apoplexy—I consider external violence the chief cause of death—congestion of the brain taking place at the same time, would acce lerate the death, chiefly caused by concussion—if no blow had been struck, and I had found none of the symptoms consequent upon a blow, I should not have seen cause enough to produce death.
