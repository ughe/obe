JOHN CONOLLY, Esq., M.D. I am chief physician to the Hanwell Lunatie
Asylum-I am the author of a work on " Diseases of the Mind"--my attention
has been for many years confined to insanity and nervous affections--I have
present about 1,000 patients under my care at Hanwell, besides my own pri
vate patients--I have also a private establishment of my own- I have had an
interview with the prisoner, and have heard the whole of the evidence that
has been given to-day-previous to my interview with the prisoner I had read
the depositions--I have endeavoured to apply my attention and experience to
the circumstances of this case-the impression made upon my mind is, that a
the time of committing this act the prisoner was not in a sound state of mind
- have known numerous cases where pecuniary losses and domestic afflic
tions have so operated upon the mind as to deprive it of reason-I believes
great many cases of moral insanity arise from pecuniary losses or domestie
afflictions- I have found that in my experience -- I believe cases of insanity
are very much more frequent from those causes than from physical reason
- the impression upon my mind is, from all the circumstances of this case,
that there has been a gradual progressive disease of the mind, that it had
arrived at a very extreme state, a kind of crisis had occurred, and that his
present state of mind, which is entirely that of a rational person, is not incon
sistent with that view, or an uncommon sequel to violent action ensuing upon
a long course of morbid irritation-I have known several cases anolagous to
it-the opinion I have expressed, is strengthened by the interview I have had
with the prisoner.
MR. RYLAND. Q. Your statement generally is, that you consider at the
time the act was committed that he could not be in a sound state of mind!
A. Yes I do not believe him to have been unconscious of the act he com
mitted, but I believe him to have been under an impulse which he was not in
a state of mind to resist-he bad lost control--that it was a sudden impuls
supervening upon a long state of agitation-it appears to have been a gradual
progressive disease of the mind, ending in a violent impulse, which he could
not control- I do not think that he was unconscious of the act he did
should imagine that he could not draw the distinction between right and wrong
-I think he was incapable of consciousness of the wickedness of the act,
Q. In a diseased state of inind, which is arrived at after a gradual progress,
does soundness of mind return speedily or suddenly? A. I think sometimes
it does, having had several examples in which persons have been sent to be

been a long course of conduct showing alteration of character, irritation

immediately after that, the conduct of the patient has become comparative
calm, or even entirely so, and from that time recovery has commenced, au
eventually taken place --that is sometimes attributable to the shock, sowe
times to physical effects, such as loss of blood, and sometimes to an alterado
in the mode of living--a number of circumstances combine to produce un
effect, but at the time the patient is quite sound.
