   Q.  Did he not say that he had found out why his horses did not look so well as the other men's? A.  He said his horses would look as well as others if they had the same victuals—Mr. Black spoke to me about it, and he told me to go about my business—I went home, but I did not leave the service—I went again—Black had not given me any money to go off—he was in a passion. WILLIAM RICHARDSON. I am a police inspector. I was on duty at Ilford station about half past seven o'clock in the morning on the 12th of February, and received information from Mr. Black—I went up the road, and saw a cart driven by the prisoner—I passed it at Stratford, and got a small distance in front of it—I desired the sergeant to keep in the rear of it—I proceeded to the Bell-and-Mackerel public-house—I saw the cart driven to the door—a man came to the prisoner, and he stopped about a quarter of an hour—the prisoner then drove on, accompanied by the other man, who had fed the horses, into Ducket's field, which is out of the main road—I then saw the man who had fed the horses get into the cart—I saw him take two empty sacks out, and place them on the hind ladder—the sacks were the wrong side outwards, and had oats hanging to them—I then desired the officer to take the prisoner, and to take care of the sacks—the sacks were the prosecutor's—the oats have never been found—the prisoner had got three miles and a half from his master's before I overtook him.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did he draw up at the Three Pigeons? A.  No, he did not stop there—from what I had heard I had reason to believe the property would be left at the Bell and Mackerel—I kept in front of him about two miles—when I first saw the sacks they were empty, and they were making preparations to fill the cart with dung—the sacks were merely moved to prepare to load the dung. NOT GUILTY.
