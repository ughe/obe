 How should you know that Harvey and the soldier were searching for Newberry? - Because I heard the soldier call out he wanted Peter; somebody came down and opened the door, but who it was I cannot say, then they went in and had a candle of some of the neighbours, but who it was I cannot say; then they went up stairs and searched the garret, and all this while my eyes were upon the house, and coming down the stairs again Peter Newberry came into the house.
 Court. Was the door of the house open or shut? - It was open, to my appearance.
 Did you see them come out again? - Yes, they came out all together, after Peter went in, and went up the street; I believe they came out in two or three minutes, I cannot say for a minute or two.
 Do you recollect who came out first? - No, Sir, I cannot recollect, but I saw Mr.  Harvey the officer have Peter by the shoulder.
 Where was Lockell then? - He came out of the door along with them, they all came as near as could be together: I kept my eye on the door till they did come out.
 Did Lockell come out dancing, with a broomstick in his hand? - He had a broomstick in his hand when they came all out together.
 Was  Peter Anderson there at the time? - No, he was not.
   PETER ANDERSON sworn.
 Do you know anything about Newberry's being taken up? - I was not there at the taking of him, I was there at the first searching for him.
 Mr. Fielding. You remember the night that Lockell was robbed? - Very well I do.
 Do you remember the calling out to Harvey, constable, constable? - No, Sir.
 Mr. Fielding. Gentlemen, I am now to solicit your attention at this late time of the day, to the case of the defendant that stands before you, and I am sure if it were to strike me as necessary to offer any apology to you for the time I must necessarily take, you would think that apology would be impertinent; for I am sure you will be heartily inclined to give a fair investigation to this case. Gentlemen, although I stand here an advocate for Harvey, and he has a right to expect the exert on of my feeble abilities; yet I go with the learned Judge who tried this cause, in all the observations I have heard him make on the crime of perjury: I perfectly agree that there is nor a crime more deserving punishment; that if committed in the extent suggested here, punishment should fall on the wretch without the accompanying commisseration of any one soul; for if it be true that the man standing before you has committed a wilful and corrupt perjury, on the trial of two men for their lives, my desire is that he should be punished with the utmost severity; at the same time you will be cautious of letting that indignation set aside the weight of the evidence necessary to criminate the person that you have before you: Gentlemen, I cannot say I feel the task upon me to-day of vindicating the defendant an arduous one; because added to the consciousness that I persuaded myself of before I came into the Court, of Mr. Harvey's innocence, I now am more and more confirmed of the propriety of that persuasion from the evidence that has been laid before you: Gentlemen, you know very well that to constitute the crime of perjury, the will must go with the action, and there must be a corrupt motive all through; and whereever therefore that crime is imputed to a man, you will necessarily enquire into the motive: Now what can be the motive of Mr. Harvey for the commission of this crime?
