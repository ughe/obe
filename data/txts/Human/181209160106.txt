I heard my name mentioned between them; then Nugent mentioned a bankruptcy of John Folkard; that he had spent a good deal of money; they wished for friends to get him through. Nugent wished me to take a bill, to go to Guildhall with, to prove under the bankruptcy, to get him through. He said he would pay me for my trouble. At last I agreed to do it. He appointed to meet me on the Sunday. I went out to avoid it, and when I came home I saw the two Folkards and Nugent in the room with Bond.
 Q. Were any stamps produced, and bills drawn - A. Yes; William Folkard produced them; some  William Folkard wrote, and some Nugent wrote. I wrote one myself; John Folkard accepted it, and he accepted Bond's, and he accepted one in black ink; he gave that to the Baroness to get another person.
 Q. Do you remember any thing being said about these stamps - A. Nugent asked  William Folkard if the stamps were all right. He answered, yes, they were all right; they were old stamps. Nugent said, it was not proper that bills so far back should be upon new stamps. On the next day I went to the Butler's Head; William Folkard came out and told us there was no room there. He took us to the White Bear. I saw John Folkard there. Nugent came afterwards. Bond, Shehey, and Kelly were there, and before we went to Guildhall the bills were arrayed and looked to by William Folkard, to see that they were all right.
 Q. Did you go to Guildhall - A. I did, and proved the debt. After that I went back to the White Bear with Bond. I then saw Nugent and the other parties there, John and  William Folkard. Nugent took the bills of me and Bond. He gave me a one-pound note.
 Q. Afterwards did Mr. Powis, the assignee, come and make enquiry about your debt - A. I saw Mr. Powis at Bond's.
 Q. After Mr. Powis came and questioned you about your debt, do you remember John Folkard came and wrote any papers for you - A. He wrote that paper for me in case Mr. Powis came there again. I asked John Folkard what I was to do about the business. He said, he would give me an account, and at the same time he wrote Mr. Bond's.

 Q. to Grick. Had you ever sold him any bullion - A. No, I was as poor as a man could be. Two or three days after we had been to Guildhall,  John Folkard came to the Baroness, and enquired after names of persons that the Baroness knew; they wished to have some names of the Baroness's friends to book debts down to. They enquired after Signor Moranda, Count Holmar, and another Baron. They wished to have debts booked to them to make the books up. They said, they were to be persons that had gone out of the country. Nugent and Folkard said so. They called some one after upon the Baroness, and produced a necklace. They asked the Baroness, where they could pledge them. She recommended them to Parker's, in Fleet-street.
 Q. Last night did you come to the house of Mr. Harmer for the purpose of giving information - A. I did. I met  William Folkard in Fetter-lane. He asked me how I did. I told him the time was coming on. He asked me whether I was subpoened. I said, yes; I was very sorry for it. He asked me to go to Mr. Morley, the attorney, in Fleet-market.
 Mr. Andrews. Who was your master when you were a clerk - A. Messrs. Hoffman and Co. of the Crescent, in the Minories.
 Q. When you lived in Bond's house how many rooms did you and the Baroness occupy - A. There were two bed rooms between us.
 Q. When the Baroness walked Fleet-street did you accompany her - A. I do not understand you.
 Q.How long did you live together - A. Not quite a year.
 Q. Are you friends now - A. We are not. I intend to go abroad of my own will. I certainly dislike her.
   HANNAH BALDWIN. Q. You keep the White Bear, London-wall - A. Yes.
 Q. Do you remember on Monday, in February last, any persons being at your house - A. I do. I recollect my husband being ill at that time. Bond came and asked me if I had any room to accommodate them for refreshment. There were there four or five men. I noticed none.
   SARAH WARNECK. Q. You lived as servant with the Baroness in February last - A. Yes.
 Q. Do you recollect on the Sunday, shortly after you were there, your mistress and Mrs. Bond going out - A. Yes, and they came home in the evening about eleven o'clock. Before they came home, Mr. Nugent and the two Folkards called and left a note. They said, they should call again. Mr. Nugent gave me the note. I gave it to my mistress. After my mistress came, Nugent and the two Folkards came. They went into my mistres's room. Some liquor was sent for. One of the Folkards was writing on paper. There was something like a stamp upon it. I do not know one of the Folkards from the other.
 JOB POPE. I keep a green-grocer's shop, in Greek-street, Red-lion-square.
 Q. Did the prisoner Nugent live in your house - A. He did, about a twelvemonth ago last  August. At  the time he was with me he was very poor. He has borrowed one, two, and three shillings of me. He had a wife and one child.
   JONATHAN JOHNSON. I am a victualler.
 Q. Do you know Nugent - A. Yes, for about a twelvemonth. He got in my debt for porter about thirteen shillings. He paid me five shillings off. He said, he would call and pay the remainder. He never did.
 JOHN NAARY. I am a dealer in coals. Nugent lived opposite of me. He is in debt five shillings and sixpence to me now for a sack of coals.
