   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Have there been any instances in which a customer, after things have been cut off, has declined to buy them? A.  Never in my house—I have known it in other houses—I am not always at home—I raised his salary because he suited me, and I wished to give him encouragement—I did not raise it on. account of his good conduct, certainly—I never swore that I did, to my knowledge.
   Q.  Is this true or false, "I raised his salary in consequence of good conduct?" A.  It is not true, sir, neither did I ever state that I did, to my knowledge—I do not remember it—I decline saying positively whether I did or not—I took him on suspicion of taking money from my till, which I found deposited, as I have stated, in my window—Mr. Fowler afterwards came to me on the Sunday morning, and asked me if I had missed any handkerchiefs—I will swear I never charged the prisoner with embezzle ment—I never swore so—I merely stated that my suspicions were that be must have put the handkerchiefs where I found them—I never said, after charging him with embezzlement, that I found I was wrong.
   Q.  Now attend to this Question, for it behoves you—did you ever say this to any body, that you would get the prisoner sent to Newgate to pre vent an action being brought, and then at the Old Bailey you would try to get him off? A.  I said to Mr. Ludlow, "The parents have threatened me with an action, I shall be compelled to go on now"—I did not wish to prosecute the boy—I never said I found I was wrong in the charge—I never said I first charged him with embezzlement, and found I was wrong in the charge—I went to Mr. Ludlow to ask the prisoner's previous character after I had been told about the handkerchiefs, but I did not men tion about the handkerchiefs to him—the prisoner was not held to bail on a charge of embezzlement, that I swear.
   MR. DOANE . Q.  Did you charge him on suspicion of robbing your till? A.  Yes—that was the charge before the Justice—that was after I found the pocket-book with the increase of the money—he was remanded on bail on the Monday—I was before the Justice on the Friday, and he was remanded till the Monday—and on the Sunday prior to that Monday Mr. Fowler came to me, and in consequence of what he said to me I made this charge about the handkerchiefs when I went again on Monday. SAMUEL HALE FOWLER. I live at No. 5, Felix-terrace, Liverpool-road. In consequence of a communication which my son made to me, I went on the Sunday to Mr. Burchmore's, and in consequence of the com munication of my son, Mr. Burchmore searched a particular place in his shop which I described—I saw him find the two handkerchiefs. JAMES FOWLER. I shall be fifteen years old next Friday—I am the son of the last witness—I know the prisoner, and I know a boy named Jones. On Friday, the 22nd of March, about six o'clock in the evening, Jones and I were walking by Westminster-bridge—the prisoner joined us, and told us to keep out of the way till after Monday, as he said he should get clear—he said he had had two silk handkerchiefs in his jacket, and he had placed them under the shop window—I told my father of it on Saturday, the next day.
