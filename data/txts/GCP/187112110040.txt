- GIBBONS, Mayor.
Mr. Justice Willes had ruled, in “Reg. v. Dosset," that other acts which go
to explain the prisoner's motive, if they are germane to the question, are
admissible in evidence. In “Reg. v. Gearing" (18 Law Journal, p. 215), it
was held that evidence of the administration of poison to other persons than the
deceased, was admissible, even though their death occurred after the death which
was the subject of the investigation, in order to show the prisoner's knowledge of
the noxious effects of the drug he administered; that was exceedingly like the
present case, because the prisoner, in taking these sums from time to time, must
have done so with the same motive as he took the sum in this case, and
there was, therefore, very cogent evidence that the prisoner set fire to the
premises at Wapping. Had the prisoner been defended by counsel, it might
have been suggested that he had caused the fire accidentally; but they were in a
condition to show that the prisoner, on multitudes of occasions before, had been
present when fires broke out, and went to the station and got 28. 6d. for doing
80; that supplied a motive, and was on that ground admissible. It also appeared
upon the depositions, and therefore the prisoner had had the fullest notice of it.
THE COURT enquired whether there was any case where the principal act for
which the prisoner was indicted, was only sought to be proved by circumstantial
evidence, tending to fix him with the act, as that appeared to be the case here 1
MR. DENMAN again referred to “Reg. v. Dossett," where, as in this case, it was
a matterof circumstantial evidence ; also to " Reg. v. Regan" (4 Cox's Criminal
Cases, p. 315), where the prisoner was indicted for setting fire to a building
for no other purpose than that of obtaining a reward, and where similar
evidence to that proposed to be given was admitted, although there was no more
evidence than existed in the present case. The Prosecution only proposed to
give evidence as to the prisoner claiming rewards for giving information of
firu, to show that he had a motive for the act. MR. POLAND referred to the
evidence in “Reg. v. Bond" (Central Criminal Court Sessions' Papers, vol. 66,
p. 299, and contended that evidence of the receipt of money by the prisoner in
other cases showed a motive, as the prisoner did the thing systematically,
knowing that he could thus obtain sums of money. The Prosecution were also
entitled to put it thus, namely, that the two questions to be determined were, “Was
the fire wilful ?" and "Did the prisoner cause it ?" The fact that he was the
first person to give information of a number of other fires in different parts of
London away from his own residence, was evidence to show that this was not an
accidental fire, as in a case of unlawfully uttering counterfeit coin, other utter-
ings were allowed to be given in evidence.
MR. JUSTICE GROVE had no doubt abort the admissibility; it was a mere
question of the degree of evidence. If there was evidence to go to the Jury of the
handwriting, then, assuming that other fires had occurred in connexion with which
the writing of the receipt appeared to be the same, it would be admissible within the
limits mentioned by Mr. Denman, the ground being that there was some evidence,
it was not necessary to say how much, to go to the Jury, of the prisoner being the
man who, whether intentionally or accidentally, did set fire to the premises; it
then became a reasonable ground of enquiry as to what was the prisoner's intention
in committing the act, provided the Jury were satisfied that he had done 80;
and the Court would not shut out evidence, if that intent could only be proved
by acts showing what was in the prisoner's mind; evidence could not be given
of other acts of a similar nature, and the Jury be then called upon to assume
that the prisoner, having committed similar guilty acts, was therefore guilty of
this act; but on the question of whether the fire was intentional or not, the
evidence seemed to be admissible.
