Cross-examined. Q.  You were not certain at first that he was the man? A.  No, I said I thought he was the man, but would not say positively—I am positive I did not say that I did not think he was the man—the police-man said, "You cannot say positively that he is the man, and I cannot take him"—I went round the building to see if I could see anybody else—I had a strong suspicion of him, and when he put his jacket on I knew him by his face; it put a different appearance on his features—I saw that he had a cord jacket when he came into the shop, and if it was shown that I was mistaken, I have not the least doubt that I could swear to his face—after the sovereign was found to be bad, Golding sent for me and asked me if I should know the man—I said that I should know him at once if I saw him. WILLIAM HARKER. I am waiter and potman at the King's Arms, Kew. On Tuesday, 22nd Sept., about 8 o'clock in the morning, Welch came with a lad, who ordered three basins of coffee or tea—Seers came about a quarter of an hour afterwards, and they breakfasted together at one table—Seers had one of the three basins—Welch asked me to cook a mutton chop for him, which he brought with him, and I did so; he pulled his money out, and said that he had got half a sovereign more than he thought he had, besides several half crowns and shillings—I saw the half sovereign—he said that he had been out on the drink, and he should put it in a pocket by itself—I did not see that he was any the worse for liquor—Seers seemed as if he had been drinking over night, but did not seem tipsy then.
   Cross-examined. Q.  How soon after the 22nd were you spoken to about this matter? A.  About 12 o'clock on the same day—a policeman came and asked me if they had been there to breakfast—I had only those three to breakfast that morning. DANIEL LANGFORD. ( Policeman, N  137). On 22nd Sept., about a quarter to 11 o'clock, I received from Mr. Huntley a counterfeit sovereign, marked with a cross on the head—I also received another counterfeit sovereign from John Golding, in Miss Warden's presence—I went with Fletcher to look for Welch; he was on the roof of the building, and I did not take him at first—I afterwards went to the King's Arms and made inquiry—I went round the building, and Welch was then on the ground—I asked him what he had had for breakfast; he said, "Some meat and pudding, the same as the other men"—I asked him where he had had breakfast; he said, "At the King's Arms"—I asked him if he had had a mutton chop for breakfast—he said, "No"—when he was at his dinner I took him into custody—I found Seers at the station at Kensington, and brought him from there on an omnibus—I searched him at the station and found on him 15 s . 6 d . in silver, and 4 d . in copper—I searched Welch and found on him 8 s . 6 d . in silver, and 8 d . in copper—when I saw Welch at the building, he had no jacket or waistcoat on; when I saw him at dinner he had a jacket on, but no waist coat—I gave him into the custody of another constable.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you say that you took him for passing a coun terfeit sovereign? A.  Yes; he said, "It is not me, you are mistaken"—there were four or five workmen at the building; there may have been one near his height, but nothing like him—the witnesses were not quite certain at first, and I would not take him for an hour or an hour and a half.
   COURT . Q.  Did you go with Golding and Smith round the building? A.  Yes; neither of them picked out any other man—the other men were carpenters and painters, and Welch is a bricklayer's labourer.
