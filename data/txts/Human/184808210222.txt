Gentlemen, I hope better things of you; are: are men, I presume, who are moving in what are called the middle circles of society, some of whom, I doubt not, have sons who, when brought to years of maturity, will have to grapple and struggle with the affairs of business; think seriously of the ruin and misery which the atrocious policy of the Government is entailing upon you by seeking to destroy thac spirit of justice and growing intelligence which the working classes are determined to enjoy, and to remove the fruits of those who have so long laboured for the working classes, just as they are beginning to make their appearance. But the working classes are beginning to understand that they are the wealth-pro ducers, and are entitled to receive a proportionate share of enjoyment, and they are determined to meet from time to time to consider what steps are likely to promote their cause, and to discuss and promote tbeir principles and if the noble Lord at the head of the Government supposes that by employing a few thousand policemen to break the heads of honest and hard-working men, and by employing spies to procure convictions, through the means of prejudiced juries, he will ultimately find himself mistaken. The avidity with which the great majority of the people of this country have adopted the principles of the people's charter, seems greatly to have frightened the noble Lord at the head of the Government; and no wonder, when the eyes of the people being to open, and they begin to inquire how the taxes have become so enormously increased, and when they see nothing but bankruptcy and ruin staring then in the face. This has led a great many men to desire a speedy change in public affairs, and to inquire how this state of things has been brought about, The noble lord has said that the disaffection of the Chartists has been to such an unparalleled extent, as has obliged him to increase the rates in order to support the men who had come forward in order to put down that disaffec tion; but this lame story has only led the people to make further inquiries, and to adopt such measures as to bring about a better state of things, and the means they had decided upon are the principles of the people's charter. Do you know those principles, Gentlemen?—if not, allow me to refer you to that excellent little pamphlet written by Dr. M'Douall, wherein be states what they are, and what they want. I believe that the principles of the people's charter contain one of the best political creeds that was ever submitted. Its points are easy of conception, and its principles are just. It does not, as some assert, wish to destroy one class of society to raise up another, nothing of the sort; but it does wish to give to all a just political equality. It seeks to give to every man of sound mind, untainted by crime, a voice in the election of the representatives of the people. It seeks to destroy a pro perty qualification, for the purpose of giving men of talent, without pecuniary resources, an opportunity of obtaining a seat in Parliament. It seeks to pre vent that disgraceful intimidation and bribery which is carried on under the present system. It seeks to shorten the duration of Parliaments, to keep the representatives of the people honest. It seeks, as nearly as possible, equal electoral districts, so as to give as fair a representation of the country at large as can possibly be obtained. These are some of the leading principles of the charter; and for calling upon my fellow-countrymen to organise, never to cease their agitation, but to avail themselves of every opportunity to forward their cause, I have been charged with all the foul and malignant falsehoods contained in the indictment; and for this the learned counsel wishes to obtain conviction.
