Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Were you always quite certain of the quantity you put on the wagon—is it not possible you might make a mistake? A.  We were very busy talking, and it is very possible I might have made a mistake in the counting—I said so to a lady named Wright—I counted them twice—I first counted fifty-three, and then fifty-four—lever made it so little as fifty-two.
   COURT . Q.  Did the fifty-two you found at Brentford correspond with the one that is here now? A.  Some of them did—there were two sorts hay, and one sort corresponds exactly in binding and hay with this. MARY ANN WOOD. My husband is a policeman. We live at East-bridge, Putney—Penstone keeps a beer-shop there—on the 22nd of this month I saw a cart-load of hay before his door, the prisoner Blake was with it—I saw his son roll a truss of hay from the front of the wagon on to the shaft—Blake helped him to ease it on to the shaft—about twenty minutes after, I saw Penstone come out, take it off the shaft, and carry it in to his door—I told my husband.
   Cross-examined. Q.  About what o'clock was it? A.  About two in the day—I was at my window, within about twenty yards of the wagon—it is a public-road—I did not notice many people passing.
   COURT . Q.  How long did Blake remain in the public-house? A.  about an hour and a quarter, and about an hour after the hay was taken in. GEORGE WOOD( police-constable V  12.) I am husband of last wit ness. I saw the wagon, drawn by three horses—the prisoner Blake was driving—it stopped opposite the beer-shop—the lad on the wagon, who appeared to be his son, lowered the hay down from the top of the wagon, and Blake came and eased it on to the shaft—he then went into the beer-shop and the lad likewise—the hay remained there about twenty minutes—I had my eye on it—I turned my head round, and it was gone—I asked my wife who had taken it—I gave information to my sergeant—Blake then came out, and the boy took charge of the horses and drove off—we fol lowed, and took Blake and the boy—I came back to Penstone—he was sitting in the tap-room smoking his pipe—I said to him "What have you done with that truss of hay?"—he said, The bit of hay is in the back-place"—it is a place used as a wash-house, and for cleaning—he had no stable—I went there, and found the truss of hay now produced—it was weighed, and there was a truss all but half a pound—it might I have lost that in being thrown down—there was fifty-five pounds and a half—I then told him he was my prisoner—he conducted himself with great violence, and as we had no more constables, I got two more, and we got him out without further violence. JAMES FINLAYSON( police-sergeant V  18.) I took Blake into custody—I asked him who authorised him to leave the hay at Penstone's—he said he had not left a morsel, and so did the boy in his hearing—Sir Felix Booth was there, and directed me to take him into custody—I was taking Blake into my room, and he asked me what I was going to do with him—I said what his master ordered—he said 'I have done nothing, there is no harm in giving Mr. Penstone a bit of hay. "
   Cross-examined. Q.  Did you say a word before the magistrate about Blake denying having left the hay? A.  I did, but not that the boy did—I cannot recollect whether it was read over to me.
