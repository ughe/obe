ROBERT SAMUEL BINGHAM. I am a bricklayer and oven-builder, and live in Harriet-street, Lambeth. I have known the prisoner twenty-five years—I saw him in November last—he was making inquiries for me—he said there was money left for me in the unclaimed dividends; that he had seen my name on the list; that he should have passed it over; as he had done many more; but knowing me so well, and so many years, he thought it must be me, at he knew of no other Robert Bingham, of Lambeth Marsh, but me—on the 24th of Jan. I wrote to him—on the morning of the 25th he came, showed me some pieces of parchment and papers that he was doing business for other people; and he said they were similar to what I was to receive—he said, he was going to the Court of Chancery, where he would file an application, that it would cost him 12 s . 6 d .; and on his return he produced a piece of parch ment similar to the one he produced in the morning—it had my name and address on it, and some other writing besides—I scarcely looked at it—he said he had paid 12 s . 6 d . for it, for applying to the Court of Chancery—I had told him before that if he brought me the paper I would pay him the money—I gave him a half-sovereign and half-a-crown, believing his statement, that he had paid it for my benefit—I asked him for a receipt, and this is the one he gave—I saw him again a few days after—he said he was going to present my petition, which would cost 1 l . 3 s . 6 d .—I said, it was not in my power to pay, it must rest a little while—I had but 13 s . 6 d ., which I gave him—he said, "Your business shall not be at a stand-still for the want of 10 s ., I will lend you 10 s . for a fortnight"—he said the petition must be signed by some person that knew me for many years—I hesitated, to thiok of some old neighbour, and he said, "I can do it myself, I have known you many years," and he signed it for me—he went away, at I thought, to present this petition—he called again, and said my business was getting on very well; that I had Sir William Home, one of the oldest Masters in Chancery, to do my business for me—because I should not forget the name, I wrote it down on a card which the prisoner had given me of his address—this is it—he likewise informed me, one day when he came, that the property was near Oakingham, near Reading, that it was three times advertised, and was left in the unclaimed dividends—he took me to the Writ-office in Lincoln's Inn-square, to search the books for my name, and I felt surprised it was not there—he said, it was all the better, for if any one had put in a claim against me, my name would have been there—I have since discovered that that office has nothing to do with the Court of Chancery.
