DAVID TAYLOR. I am a surgeon. Collins was brought to my surgery, and I attended him—he had an ordinary contused wound of the head of a very trifling character—I have no doubt it is well now, or nearly so—I have not seen it, because I have been engaged here during the last week.
   JURY . Q.  Was the wound of that magnitude as to cover the prisoner with blood? A.  Trifling wounds will sometimes produce a large quantity of blood without any serious importance—there was nothing in the nature of the wound to lead me to suppose it impossible that it should cause a good deal of blood.
   MR. PAYNE called JOSEPH HOLDSWORTH. I am a plasterer, and live in Dove-row, Hack ney-road. I know the prisoner—on Monday, the 22nd of August, we were working for Mr. Tully at some houses in Lambeth-road, about a quarter of an hour's walk from Kennington-Common—the prisoner left work at six o'clock that evening—I heard him say in the course of the day that he had some tools at some other job, and he should go and fetch them in the evening, and he left with that intention—he only worked with me a week—he seemed a sober, peaceable man.
   COURT . Q.  Were you at the meeting? A.  No. ROBERT COLLINS re-examined . I saw the prisoner throw eight or nine different stones before the one that struck me—other people were throw ing stones after that and before that, but not at the time the stone struck me—the prisoner was standing among the crowd, outside the rails, on the footpath, when he threw the last stone that hit me, and after that he was hiding behind the people—he was at the head of the footpath when he threw four or five, and then he came to the edge of the rails, and threw the last two or three—there were a good many people on the path—I was ten or twelve-yards from the rails—I could see him, being much the taller man there, and having a white frock on—I saw him come forward and throw the stone, and then run back among the crowd—I thought it was time to take him when he struck me—I did not take him before, because I saw the danger of going over the rails singly—I might have got killed, and should, no doubt, but for the assistance of Lutsford. HENRY LUTSFORD re-examined . The prisoner must have been by the curb of the footpath when he threw the stone—I saw the place on Collins's hand afterwards—I did not see the prisoner standing near the rails at all—I saw Mr. Brown with his cane after the prisoner was in custody—he kept back the crowd in the best manner he could—the crowd pushed us down.
