By cases decided in the High Court and in the Court of Appeal, it had been well settled for six or seven years that if at the house of which the person accused is occupier material parts of the transaction of betting are carried on, it is immaterial that the money paid in regard to these betting transac tions is not received from the customers or clients of the betting man at the house of which he is occupier, and, therefore, the expedient which the defendant had resorted to was nothing more nor less than wasted ingenuity and the law was too strong for him. Up to some years ago it used to be thought by many people, and not unlikely by those who are members of the legal profession, that the handing in of the betting slip or the letter containing the bet at the house occu pied by the person accused must, in order to bring that transaction within the provisions of this Act, be accompanied by money or money's worth, and that the proprietor of the betting business carried on there must sit at the receipt of custom. But the decisions of the Courts had changed all that, and now it was quite immaterial where the money was received if the evidence satisfies the jury and the tribunal that at the premises mentioned in the indictment or accusa tion material parts of the transaction of betting are in fact carried on. The learned counsel cited the cases of Stoddart v. Hawkes (1 L. R., K. B., 1902) and Lennox v. Stoddart (2 L. R., K. B., 1902, p. 21).
