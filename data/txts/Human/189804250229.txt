   Cross-examined by the Prisoner.  Your letters were in the name of Wilson—you always gave that name—you had the child vaccinated in my name—I did not have the vaccination paper, you said that you burnt it—you asked whether any of my little boys had fits, because Charlie was so strange about the eyes—he never had a fit—he was attended once by a doctor after I had him—he had diarrhoea, and you were sent for—the doctor said nothing about fits, or that if he did not get better in twenty-four hours he would not get better at all—he was a beautiful boy—he used not to shake his head about in bed, only when he had the ear-ache—I never told Tester that he did. CAROLINE DARVILLE. I am the wife of Frederick Darville, a carpenter, of 19, Collin's Street, Tooting—the prisoner lived there with a man named Tester—they occupied three rooms and a scullery—they had six children—the four elder ones slept in one room and the prisoner and Tester occupied another room with the child that is dead and a younger child—the other room was occupied by the grandfather and the other children—and the lodger slept in the kitchen—the rooms were all on the ground floor—there was a bed in the front room, and a kitchen with a scullery off it—I remember this child coming in November—he was in good condition—I have 10 children—he could walk and was bright and very intelligent and not emaciated or thin; but after six months I noticed that he was getting very thin, and there were sores all over him and I noticed bruises once or twice—I noticed a bruise on his lips and a large one on his cheek and another on his eye at the same time—that was a month or six weeks after—I remember his being taken to a doctor—I had never seen him taken out of the house into the air from the time he came till then—my room was up-stairs, and their bedroom down stairs—when the child has been put to bed I have heard the prisoner slap him, and heard him cry—the prisoner told me that he was a nurse child, and had no mother, and that she was paid 5s. a week for him—I sometimes fed him—he took his food, and kept it down, but I once fed him with milk, and he vomited it back, and the prisoner rammed the spoon in his mouth and he cried—after that he became worse, and after Christmas I advised her to take him to a doctor—she said that she would, and asked me to ask him who hit him—I did so, and he said that his mother did—he had bruises at that time on his lip—when he said "Mother," in answer to my question, he pointed to the prisoner, and she swore at him and said, "Don't call me mother, you bleeding little perished"  —her son William was there, he is sixteen—he said, "What is the good of hitting the child?"—at that time, therefore, the child appeared to be frightened of his mother—she put him in a wash-house with a stone floor, and kept him there half an hour or longer—he had no extra clothes on, only an old frock—he cried while he was shut up there—I remember when he died, but did not see him that day—I saw him since he died, and saw a bruise on his forehead—the prisoner said that he knocked himself—after Christmas I heard her smack him and throw him. down in the front room, and she said, "Lie there and die."
