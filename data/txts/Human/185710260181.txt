   MR. M'MAHON . apprehended that the Court would consider the case upon the record only, abstractedly of the facts. The plea stated that the prisoner was, upon a former occasion, indicted for cutting and wounding, with intent to murder, and that he was acquitted of that offence; the plea did not allude to any second Count, nor did the Crown reply that Count; he was therefore clearly acquitted of the wounding which caused the death, and the Court could take no notice of the grounds, of that acquittal; the acquittal was of the whole offence, of everything essential to be proved against him; for if it had been possible, on the facts, to convict of an unlawful wounding, the Statute enabled the Jury so to do. As to the general rule, that a party cannot be tried twice for the same offence, the learned Counsel referred to Blackstone , 2 Hale's Pleas, ch.  35 and  36; 4 th Cooke, p.  40, ( Darley's case ), Hudson and Lee; Yaux's case, Holcroft's case Sparrow's case ; 5 Cooke, p.  6 l . As to case of pardon, he referred to Hale's Pleas , 426; Plowden , 401, ( Cole's case ); Cooper v the Hundred of Basingstoke ; 7 Modern Reports; also , 2 Salkeld , 614; Holt's Reports , 638; and  2 nd Raymond , 826, Hales and Pettit; Plowden , 262; Cole's case, Nicol's case; Foster's Reports , 64; and Rex v. Jenning, Russell and Ryan , 388. From these cases he urged that, if the Crown, instead of prematurely proceeding to trial before the death, had granted a general pardon of all felonies and offences, it was quite clear that the prisoner could not have been afterwards tried for the murder, and the case was exactly the same upon pardon, punish  ment, or acquittal. The case of Rex v. Vandercourt and Abbott, 2 East , 519, and Leech's Crown Law , 316, was very much in point; to an indictment for burglary, with intent to steal, was pleaded a former acquittal of burglary and stealing; but as, upon the first indictment, there was no proof of stealing it was held to be no bar to the second. This established the principle that unless the first indictment were such as the prisoner might have been convicted upon by proof of the facts contained in the second, an acquittal on the first indictment could be no bar to the second; and this principle was a very striking test to apply to the present case. He therefore submitted, as well upon the general principle as upon the authorities referred to, that, first, upon the ground of the pleadings, the prisoner had substantially been acquitted of this offence; and, secondly, if the pleadings were to be amended, and the Crown permitted to reply the conviction on the second Count, that, then the prisoner would be punished twice for the same offence.
   The Counsel for the Crown were not called upon to reply. THE LORD CHIEF BARON. " I believe we are all of opinion that this is not a good plea. The prisoner is, on the present occasion, indicted for murder, and murder may be committed without any intent to kill. If a man intends to maim, and causes death, and it can be made out most distinctly that he did not mean to kill, yet if he does those acts and use those means for the purpose of accomplishing that limited object, and they are calculated to produce death and death ensues, by the law of England that is murder, although the man did not mean to kill. On the former occasion the prisoner was charged with wounding, with intent to kill. The Jury found that he did not intend to kill, and there the intention was of the essence of the crime; that is not so on the present occasion; it is not necessary here to prove any intention to kill; it is only necessary to prove an intention to inflict an injury that might be dangerous to life, and that it resulted in death; that is quite sufficient to sustain the present charge.
