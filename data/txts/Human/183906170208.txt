I am in partnership with my brother as goldsmiths and dealers in bullion, in Cheapside. Before this occurrence, we had been in the habit of purchasing bullion and dealing with Solomon, a re finer, in the Strand, to the amount of from about 7000 l . to 10, 000 l . a year, for the last five or six years—I was present when young Selim Solomon re ceived a cheque of 1200 l . from my brother, on Messrs. Robarts, on account of two bars of gold, which we had bought of him on the 2nd of April—we had bought the gold of Solomon, but his eldest son Selim brought it to us, as he had been in the habit of doing—the weight was 408oz. gross—we have a correspondent named Emerique, of Paris—in purchasing bul lion the object of the party is to turn it into money directly—we seldom keep it above a few hours if we can dispose of it—it depends on the state of foreign exchange—we shipped these bars of gold to Emerique, of Paris, I think on the 5th of April—I had in the meantime made a com munication to Mr. De Mole, in consequence of what. I had heard of the loss of the gold-dust—I made the communication the same day I received the gold, before I sent it away—when gold is once melted, if it is amalga mated with copper or any thing, it will entirely alter the colour, which would prevent it being identified—the alloy will alter the colour—the colour depends on what it is alloyed with—I have two assay papers—( marked  12 and  13)—they came from Mr. Johnson, the assayer, in the ordinary course of business—it is the custom to take a little piece off to assay gold—I did not pay the balance for the gold on the same day the 1200 l . cheque was paid—we had an order not to pay the balance—Edward Clark, our clerk, had paid some money to Solomon on the 2nd of April on account, but not in my presence—there were two payments made on the 2nd of April to Solomon—I do not exactly know whether it was on the 2nd without my books, which my clerk can produce to-morrow—the payments were all made by my clerk—he can prove these things to-morrow—he is not here—this is the cheque for 1200 l .—( marked No.  11.)
   Cross-examined by MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  Did not the ingot that was brought to you appear the result of smeltings of different sorts of gold? A.  I am not a smelter, therefore I cannot say—it must have been melted of different materials, because of the alloy in it—I do not understand what you mean by smeltings of different kinds of gold—if it was old jewellery and coins I should call them different—it did appear the result of smeltings of that description of material, as far as my judgment goes—it might be old coins, jewellery, and gold-dust all mixed together—there is so much disguise in gold, it is impossible to tell unless you see it.
   COURT . Q.  Is it so much altered by alloy it is difficult to say what it is? A.  Yes.
   MR. PRENDERGAST . Q.  That was your judgment at the time, that it was different kinds of gold, old coins and jewellery, and the setting of diamonds? A.  No, there would be silver in that—it might be watch-cases, snuff-boxes, and such articles.
