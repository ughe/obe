would make any difference in this injury-by concussion I simply mean
stunning, from which a person recovers shortly, compression is the result of
the slow oozing of blood on the surface of the brain, which gradually causes
that effect-a patient is less likely to recover from that than from concus-
sion-it is a small oozing from the brain or one of its vessels.
MR SHARPE. Q. I believe on the Tuesday evening he was half delirious ?
A. Yes; those symptoms kept on increasing--he had an epileptio fit on
Wednesday evening; those continued to the time of his death I was not
with him at the exact moment of his death, but I was shortly before I
afterwards made a post-mortem examination--I first opened the head-I
found a quantity of extravasated blood on the surface of the brain, and
towards the base-I collected it; there was about three ounces of fluid
blood- I cannot exactly define how much there was at the base of the brain;
the blood there was lying on the right side, at the back of the head, on a
level with the ear-it commenced exactly at the cut which was just at the
apex, and went to the back part; it had gravitated down to the base of the
brain-the blood had all proceeded from an arterý which was wounded; that
would be likely in time to cause death-I saw the extent to which it had
gravitated, and it must have destroyed lifoit would be likely to cause
epileptio fits with other symptoms-the ruptured artery on the brain was
the immediate cause of death-I examined the skull, it was unusually thin-
if he had been tripped up by his legs and fallen, it might have caused both
the extravasated blood and the wound; it is a common cause I attach no
importance to the scalp wound, it was purely external; both the external
and the internal rupture of the artery were, at the same part the proba-
bility of such a rupture would be increased by reason of the heavy weight of
the man, and would be still further increased by the remarkable thinness of
the skull; the thinner the skull is the more the external violence would be
felt-I examined the brain, and found it in an unusually healthy condition;
there was nothing in it to cause death, except the rupture of the vessel and
the blood in the brain-I found a fracture of the skull extending from the
apex, or about an inch below it, to about an inch from the out, extending
through the temporal bone right through the bones of the ear in a diagonal
direction; it was a linear fracture without depression-I should attribute
such a fracture more to a fall than a blow.
Court. Q. You say there was a crack, but no depression of the bone on

increased by the man being heavy and his skull. not being thick-I attri-

recover from simple fracture of the skull; if it had only been & linear
fracture the patient might have recovered— Beeing the blood that oozed
from the rupture, I am of opinion that that was the cause of death.
