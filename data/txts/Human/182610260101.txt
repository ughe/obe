 2006.  JACOB EVANS was indicted for feloniously killing and slaying Camp Collins , by administering to him 6ozs. of decoction of fox-glove, well knowing the same to be a deadly poison.
   JOHN BURBIDGE. I am a cabinet-maker, and live in Bayne's-row, Clerkenwell - Camp Collins was my apprentice; he died last Thursday morning, about half-past ten o'clock; he was seventeen years old; he had been ailing at intervals for some months; he complained for weeks together of a giddiness in his head, which at times seized him suddenly when at work - he was unable to work for three or four weeks - I have known the prisoner about three years, and I believe he was lately been out of employ - he published a book on the use of herbs some months ago - he called on me last Tuesday, about eleven o'clock, and told me Collins' mother had spoken to him, to know if he could prepare anything from herbs, to prevent this disorder, and he had promised to call and see the boy, and asked how he was afficted - the boy replied, "I feel pretty well now;" "He said, how do you feel when the disease attacks you?" he said, "I am taken with a stupid giddiness, and swimming my head, as if something was running all about me;" the prisoner passed, and then said, "If your mother will get two or three pennyworth of fox-glove at the herbalist, and put to that one quart of water, and boil it till it is reduced to something more than half a pint" - I said, "Mr. Evans, is it to be between half a pint and a pint?" he said Yes - this the boy I believe perfectly understood; the prisoner said, "And then, my boy, you will take half that quantity; it will make you very sick, and work you upwards and downwards" - that he would be in that way for for several hours, and probably not able to work all day - I went down-stairs with the prisoner; and the boy followed us, and said, "Sir, if you have no objection, I had rather Mr. Evans get these things himself, as I do not know where to get them, and my mother will satisfy him;" Evans said, "Very well, the expense will be very few pence;" he then went away, and called again next day (Wednesday) about eleven o'clock - he came into the shop, and said, "Now, my boy, I have brought the stuff, I should like you to take it in something large enough to hold half of it" - the boy fetched up a large breakfast cup, which held something less than half a pint - Evans filled it about three parts full, and said, "Now, my boy, you must not sip this, it is bitter and nasty, you must drink it off at once;" the boy took it from him, and drank it off at a draught - he had some bread and cheese, which he was eating for a lunch, and he eat some very hastily to take the laste out - Evans said, "If this does not operate in about half an hour, take about the same quantity again;" and he said,"You will see, Mr. Burbidge, that he takes it, if it does not begin to operate about that time;" I looked at the watch which hung up, and said, "It is now such a time, and at such a time, the other must be administered;" Evans had not left the shop till the half hour expired - the boy then untied his apron, began to rub himself, and then went down to the yard; he returned in a short time - Evans had then left the shop; the boy continued to work for about a quarter of an hour or twenty minutes - I conceived the stuff had taken its effect, and did not give him the second dose - the boy then complained of being very sick, and went into the next room, which was his bed-room, and vomitted a good deal - I went to him in about ten minutes, and said, "Can you come down to dinner?" he said, "I fear, I cannot eat I am so sick;" he came down, saw the dinner, but went up directly, saying he was so sick. When I returned from dinner, I asked how he was? he said, "Very sick" - he had then taken the medicine and hour and a half - he took it exactly at ten minutes to twelve o'clock, and it was twenty minutes past when he went into the yard - he still complained of being very sick, but brought very little up, except some green yellow stuff - I went to him several times, found him very sick, and advised him to walk about, as it would operate better - at four o'clock, I asked him down to tea; he said he had rather have it sent up - it was sent up; he took some, but eat nothing - a little before five o'clock, he came down into the kitchen, drew a chair to the fire, and leaned his head on the table, and at short intervals vomited - my wife advised him to take some warm water, which he declined; I said I thought he had better take the other half, but he did not. I came down about seven o'clock, and thought he was asleep; I said to my wife he had better not sleep there; he said,"I am not asleep, I had rather sit by the fire, as I feel so sick" - I then went out and found his parents at a chapel, told them of it, and said I thought he had not taken sufficient; they advised me to persuade him to take the rest; I went home and found my wife had just put him to bed; she went up to him a little before ten o'clock, he appeared asleep, and she brought the light down - a man named Harris went up about a quarter to eleven, and he thought him asleep; he slept in the next bed - I went up about ten minutes to seven o'clock in the morning, and he said he was very sick every now and then, but in no pain except in his head, putting his hand to his forehead - I sent for Mr. Whitmore, and about half-past ten o'clock the boy died. He was opened next morning, in my presence - there were four surgeons. The prisoner had never said how much the two or three pennyworth of fox-glove would produce - the bottle was about three-parts full; he did not give him quite half; here is the bottle, with the remainder of the stuff in it.
