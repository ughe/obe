   Cross-examined by MR. BALLANTINE . Q.  How long have you been married? A.  About four years—my wife's name was Ann Nye before I married her—she was not a widow—I have never complained of her con duct before with anybody, nor of her absence on any occasion—she has not been living with me since this has happened—she was at my house one night, the night we went down by the night-van late—she remained there with her sister, and next morning she went down to her mother's—I have seen her during the last week—she has been living with her sister, away from me—I have slept in the same house—when Nye came for the box, I asked if she knew what she had got there—she said, "Yes"—I then told her it was my property, and gave her into custody—that was all that took place.
   Cross-examined by MR. PAYNE . Q.  Did you say anything to Pratt, when you found him? A.  I said, "You are a pretty fellow, are you not?"—he said, "What have I done?" and I said, "You have ran away with my wife and all my property"—this was on Friday, the 5th—my wife left home on Wednesday, the 3rd—I had seen Pratt in my house a little before ten o'clock that evening—I was down in the cellar for about ten minutes when my wife left, and I missed her when I came up. ANN ILLMAN. I am the prosecutor's wife. I left my husband's house with Pratt on the 3rd of August—Nye had been at Reigate for some days before—I did not tell her anything about Pratt's attention to me—he asked me if she should go and pack up my things, and she went up stairs and packed them up—I first conversed with Nye about Pratt about three days before that—she asked me to go away—I did not say that I would or would not—she said if I wanted to go away I was to go with Pratt—I had not told her anything about him before that—he had spoken to her—she told me that they had been talking together, and she wished me to go away with him—I did not exactly agree to that—I did at last go away with him, with a great deal of persuasion by both of them—Pratt asked me what I was going to take with me—I told him I did not know—he said I was to take as much as I could with me, and I was to take as much money as I could, and the watch besides, and leave my husband penniless—I was very much shocked at that proposal—he said he could not take any thing more than what he stood upright in, and I was to take as much as I could of my husband's clothes for him—Nye also told me to take as much as I could with me, and she packed the box up herself—Pratt corded it, and wrote the direction on it—it was to go away as her box—she went away about three o'clock that afternoon, with the box, by the coach—Pratt went about two or three hours before her, and met her on the road—that was arranged before they went away—Pratt said he would come and fetch me, and he came a little before ten, and fetched me away—my hus band was then down in the cellar about his beer—Pratt and I went up the hill towards Sutton—Nye was in a fly  at the top of the hill—we got into it, and drove off to London—we got to London very late—we could not find a place where there were separate rooms, and we all three passed the night in one room—next night we went to another house—Pratt and I passed
