made to him by Bishop, they being in the nature of privileged communications;
for although he toas not actually engaged as his attorney at the time they were
made, yet when he afterwards became s0, he availed himself of the information
80 acquired; this toas not the privilege of the attorney, but of the client, and
he contended that if this evidence was admitted, the rule of law would be
defeated. (See Taylor on Evidence, p. 616). MR. BALLANTINE contended
that no privilege existed until the relation of client and attorney was established,

not intend to ask the nature of Bishop's communication, only what the witness
said to Bishop. The COURT was of opinion that such a question might be put.)
