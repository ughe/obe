ELIZABETH BODEMAN. I live at 2, Windmill Street,. New Cut—on Tuesday, 5th August, about 2 o'clock in the day, the deaf and dumb boy was by my door with a considerable number of people—I had seen him once or twice before—my brother made some signs to him to get him out of the crowd, instead of which he thought my brother invited him in, and he came in—we were at dinner with Mary Ann Davis—I made motions to him, trying to convey that we would give him something to eat; and he made motions with his mouth, as if to convey that he would have some thing to eat—I cut him some bread and meat and handed him a knife—he put the knife on one side and shuddered at it—he would not look at it—he ate the bread and meat and went away—about 5 p.m. he came in again when my brother and I and Mrs. Davis were at tea—I offered him, by signs, a cup of tea—he pointed to his mouth again, and I made him two cups and gave him some bread and butter—he pointed to some blood on his trousers; I motioned to ask him how he came by it—he lifted something up from the fire-place and made a motion as if he was lifting something into the bed or sofa. ( MR. SLEIGH objected to this evidence as it teas a conversation, though by dumb show, in the prisoner's absence, equivalent to words; and being so, he was unable to check it. MR. WILLIAMS contended that the evidence was admissible, the signs being acts done. MR. JUSTICE LOPES considersd that the signs were something more than acts done, and equivalent to an ordinary conversation, and therefore excluded the evidence. ) In consequence of what passed my brother gave the deaf and dumb boy an old pair of trousers, and he took off his own trousers, put them into a tub and put a little cold water on them—he wrote something on a piece of paper—he came again on Wednesday and Thursday, and seemed in a way because I had put his trousers in water—the detective subsequently took them away—they had remained up to that time in the water—they had not been washed at all, only put in the water and run out again.
