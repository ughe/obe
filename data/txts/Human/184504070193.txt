GEORGE PRIOR. I am a gardener, in Mr. Tebbutt's employ. On the morning of 12th March I was at work in the garden—I went on the fofest with a policeman about twelve o'clock in the day, and saw him find the ear-case of one sheep about half a mile from Mr. Tebbutt's, on the forest—it was buried under the ground—I took part of the ground off it—on Friday, the 15th, I went with Want and brought that sheep home—we afterwards searched and found another place on the forest, and found another beep's carcase there, which we brought home—the entrails had been taken out and the throats cut—there was a great deal of blood—I do not know the marks on the sheep. EDWARD WANT. On Tuesday night, the 11th of March, I saw Mr. Tebbutt's sheep safe at a quarter before six o'clock—I went into the meadow about half past nine o'clock on Wednesday morning, and missed two—I went on the Friday with Prior to the forest, and brought home the carcases of two sheep—they were the carcases of Mr. Tebbutt's sheep which I missed on the Wednesday—there was a long red mark from the neck nearly down to the tail, by which I know them—there were no letters on them—the skins are here—I took them off—the entrails had been taken oat before they were buried—the meat and skin were buried. EDWIN DIVER( policeman. ) I was stationed at Leytonstone on the night of the 11th of March—I know the lane leading from the forest of Hainault to the high road, by the side of Mr. Tebbutt's house—it is called Davis-lane—I was in that lane about one o'clock in the morning, and saw the prisoner Hack there, close to Mr. Tebbutt's house, about 100 yards from the meadow—he was just moving—he appeared as if moving his feet—I asked why he was loitering about there—he gave me a saucy answer—I do not recollect his words—he after that said, "I am going to Wood ford,"and proceeded on his way towards Woodford—I passed the place again, and at two o'clock saw him in company with Cornish—I was going towards the forest, and they were coming from it—I had my lantern—I believe they saw me, but they directly crossed the road, and quickly passed me—I turned my light on them as they passed—they walked quicker when they saw me—I walked after them, and then they ran—I am quite certain it was both the prisoners—I ran also, and overtook Cornish—Hack wa" forward—I asked Cornish what he was doing thew, and why he ran away—he did not give me a direct answer—I turned my light all round him, and saw his hands were covered with blood—I laid hold of him directly, and asked how he accounted for the blood on his hands—he gave me no direct answer, but struck me with his hand in the stomach, which took my wind away—I got hold of him—we scuffled together—he got away from me, and ran towards a fence of Mr. Davis's—I ran after him, but he leaped over the fence, which has spikes in it, and in my trying to get over, a spike ran into my hand—he got into the forest, and got away—I went on Wednesday to where the sheep was found—I watched on Wednesday night and Thursday, and nobody came for the sheep—on the Friday night I found Cornish in custody, and recognised him at once—I went with another officer in search of Hack, on the Monday following—I recognized him, and took him into custody—when Cornish was
