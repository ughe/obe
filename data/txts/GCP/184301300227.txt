Q. You have spoken in high terms of Stanley; at that time had you any
reason to alter your opinion of Stanley's honour A. The first doubt I had
thrown up on Stanley's honour was by Mr. Newhall, who very much astonished
me in depreciating him in every way-up to that time I had not the least
doubt of Stanley-I had a very high opinion of him, and so had my father
he died with that impression-Stanley had been very much trusted by my
father and myself, we placed the greatest confidence in him he was con-
stantly with us during the time we resided at Lewisham-it was a lone place,
he resided in the house-my father was eighty-seven years of age--we had
known Stanley ever since the year 1810--he was then a rich merchant, in
King's Arms-yard, Coleman-street, in the wool trade-when he afterwards
came to us, he represented that he had been some years in America-he was
trusted and confided in by my father and myself from the moment of his
putting his foot into our house, from the year 1837, and then he represented
that he had just arrived in England-he resided in our house at Lewisham
we went from town to Lewisham-my father died in West-square--Stanley did
not reside in that house-we had been there from the period of leaving our
house in the June preceding-the house, Mount Pleasant, at Lewisham, was
let furnished to a Mr. Freeman, a solicitor, of Coleman-street--I never saw
Newhall till I saw him at the prisoner's house--I saw him after that I have
seen him about three times in the course of my life,
