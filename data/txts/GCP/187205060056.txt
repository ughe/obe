MR. SEYMOUR contended that there was sufficient provocation to reduce the
offence to manslaughter; for although no words would be provocation sufficient,
yet as a soufle was heard before the third shot, and as the prisoner's shoulder
was struck, there was evidently more than verbal provocation, and further,
that though the deceased entered the workroom lawfully, he entered the parlour
unlawfully, and became from that time a trespasser, which would also reduce
the offence to manslaughter. (See "Reg. v. Smith" and “Reg. v. Sherwood,"
Archbold, p. 333; "Reg. v. Harewitz," Hale's Pleas of the Crown, p. 474;
also Archbold, p. 685.) MR. POLAND contended that as the prisoner was not
defending his possessions, even if the deceased insisted on remaining in the room,
he was not justified in using a deadly weapon towards him, and that no words,
threats, or gestures would be sufficient provocation for the use of it. Mr.
