   JOHN ADAMS. I am a headborough. I was present when the prisoner was apprehended - Brown's statement is correct.
 MR. BLOOM. This brown silk is mine - I know it by the selvedge; I generally make white selvedges, but having a remnant of blue to get off, I put this blue to it - the silk was made by my direction; I could tell you how many threads there are in this selvedge, and how many in that; the other piece is also mine - there are eight claret-coloured threads outside the selvedge; I remember manufacturing silk of that sort - I lost three pieces of this description at the time of the robbery; two of them were sixty-eight yards long, and the other thirty-five and three quarters.
 Cross-examined. Q. How many pieces of that description have you manufactured? A. I suppose one hundred of this pattern - the brown is a common pattern, but not a common colour; they are shots - it is brown and black, what we call a cane; I manufactured two of this description - one was a white selvedge, and the other of this colour; I sold the other two years ago - another manufacturer might put on exactly the same selvedge, but that is a hundred chances to one.
 MR. BRODRICK. Q. Had the piece you sold the same selvedge as this? A. No, that was a white selvedge - this is blue and white.
 MR. BARRY. Q. Is it not common to manufacture with a white selvedge? A. Yes; but not blue and white - I will not swear that no other manufacturer ever made with blue and white.
 MR. BRODRICK. Q. Had you ever manufactured any other with that selvedge? A. No, not of this colour; I sold twenty-five yards of this piece to Mr. Maund, of Corn-hill, and have got this piece back to prove it - I asked him to lend it to me; I sold it to him on the 2nd of May, and know it to be the same piece - I went there, and brought it away myself to compare; they are both one piece - they tally exactly in the selvedge; I am positive they are from one piece, and will swear it.
 COURT. Q. Of how many yards did the piece originally consist? A. Eight-four; I sold twenty-five to Maund, and six and three quarters to Kestevens, of Milk-street; about fifty yards were taken out of my warehouse - the piece found is about seven or eight yards.
 Prisoner's Defence. I am totally innocent of the charge, and know no more about it than a child.
 Eleven witnesses gave the prisoner a good character.
     GUILTY- DEATH- Aged 59.
 Of Stealing above the value of 5l. in the Dwelling house, but not of breaking and entering.
 Before Mr. Baron Vaughan.
 1833.  ELIZABETH CARTER& ROBERT CARTER(THE YOUNGER) were indicted for stealing, on the 8th of June , one 40l., one 30l., two 20l., nine 10l., and five 5l. Bank notes, the property of Johanna Keefe , in the dwelling-house of Robert Carter the elder; and ROBERT CARTER(THE ELDER) was indicted for feloniously receiving one 30l., one 20l., and two 5l. Bank notes, part of the said property so feloniously stolen, he well knowing the same to have been stolen.
 2nd COUNT, stating them to be the property of  John Richard Farre.
 3rd and 4th COUNTS, stating them to be the property of  William, by divine providence,  Archhishop of Canterbury , Primate of all England and Metropolitan.
 5th and 6th COUNTS, stating them to be the property of a person or persons unknown.
 MR. ADOLPHUS conducted the prosecution.
   JOHN RICHARD FARRE, ESQ. M.D. I live at No. 4. Charterhouse-square. Mrs. Keefe was my aunt - I saw her alive last on the evening of the 1st of June; she resided at Robert Carter's house, No. 91, Drury-lane- I received information of her death, and went there again on the 8th of July, having been at Brighton: I saw Mrs. Carter, and told her I was administrator to the estate - she replied that she knew it; I told her I had proof that the desk of Mrs. Keefe had been opened, and Bank notes to a considerable amount removed from it - she replied, she could not help it; she had done her duty - and when I pressed her again and again on the subject of the Bank notes, she denied all knowledge of them, not only once but repeatedly; I told her it was the last time I should have it in my power to show her that mercy which I wished, and desired that the whole of Mrs. Keefe's property should be sent to No. 11, Charter house-square, on the following day; and at the same time to send for payment of her bill for lodging, attendance, &c.; she made no answer, except to deny all knowledge of the notes - I left her, and went to the Police-office; she never told me that Mrs. Keefe had made her a present of the notes: I never saw her on any other occasion till she was in custody.
 Cross-examined by MR. BRODRICK. Q. What was the lady's age? A. As near as I can judge, between seventy and eighty - it might be seventy-five; there was that kind of habit in her which I should expect from a lady who had married early in life, and lived much abroad - she lived in lodgings much below her station; I believe that resulted from penurious habits - she went to lodge there on the 2nd of April, and had lodged there previously, between 1822 and 1824; I called on her there several times - I cannot recollect how often: I do not recollect where she went to lodge after that - I saw her repeatedly; I was scarcely a week without seeing her - I saw her when she lodged in Carey-street, in 1829, repeatedly, and she used to come either to my house or to some of my relations, every week; I sent her to the West Indies twice, at my own expense; before she went to Carter's I understood she lived in Portugal-street, after she left Carter's the first time; I did not visit her there, being much occupied in my profession, and she usually called on me or my family - I saw her last on the 1st of June; I left town the next morning for Brighton, for my own health, and left her better than I had seen her from the commencement of her illness in February - I left her dropsical and ill, but expected she would recover; she kept her bed when I left - she was confined to her bed all the time she was at Carter's; her complaint required it - I did not direct any medical friend to call on her; I
