 Q. Then how could you see them whip their horses with the wall on your side? A. It was very light, moonlight. I was between the fore and hind wheel, on the side next the Arcade. I could see the coachmen, for they stood up to whip; the wall is too high to see over; one carriage put back, and the moment there was room for one, he whipped his horses and darted in.
 COURT. Q. Did you see them whipping distinctly? A. I did; I did not think he could have got in, the other backed so little; one backed more than the other, which admitted the other to dart in; there was room for one to back.
 Q. Supposing that both backed a little to get disentangled, was it not the most prudent course for one to go forward? A. I suppose when one found room he should have gone forward, and did so.
 Q. Suppose he had not driven furiously, and gone in without whipping, was it not proper for him to go in? A. Yes, according to my judgment - no foot passenger could pass till he had gone in.
   SAMUEL PLANK. I am an officer of Marlborough-street. I was on duty on this night, at Lord George Cavendish's, to preserve order with the carriages. I heard a noise between twelve and one o'clock, and went down to the centre gate, where Dawkins lay with his back against the wall, and his leg broken - he was on the left side of the gate going out. I went with him to St. George's Hospital, and next morning went to Lady Lambert's stables and apprehended the prisoner. I told him I had a warrant against him for running over a man at Burlington-house last night. He said he recollected driving against a man, but had no idea that the wheel ran over him. I told him the man's leg was broken, and he was in the hospital - he said he was extremely sorry to hear it. The carriages should have been stationed with their horses' heads towards Hyde-park, and not to come in till called - that is always the regulation in that place.
   THOMAS DAWKINS. I am a servant to his Majesty. Nicholas Dawkins was my brother; he was fifty-five years old - he was an officer of Marlborough-street; I visited him daily in St. George's Hospital - I believe he lived nearly twelve weeks - he said, full two months before he died, that he knew he must go, and that he was a murdered man. The night before he died, he said he must go, that it was all dicky with him - that if the coachman had pulled up in a proper manner, he might not have been run over, if he had not whipped on so; he said so a fortnight before he died, and the night before also - he was aware that he should not recover - he said he went down by Lord Westmoreland's order, to avoid the carriages coming in in that manner - that he got them to back a little, and Lady Lambert's carriage whipped on - that he laid hold of the pole and reins, and down he fell, and the fore and hind wheels went over his legs.
 Cross-examined. Q. At one time the surgeons considered him out of danger? A. I understood so - he died of a fever.
 Q. At the time he used these strong expressions was he not in a state of suffering? A. Yes.
   GEORGE SKINNER. I am butler to Lady Lambert - the prisoner was her coachman.
 Cross-examined. Q. Was he not a humane careful coachman? A. Yes; he was bailed in consequence of the deceased being pronounced out of danger - he has always expressed his ignorance of the accident, if it occurred through him; and his regret at it - he has never been out of the way.
 MR.  HENRY PITMAN. I am house-surgeon of St. George's Hospital. In May last, I remember Dawkins being brought to the hospital; he had received a compound fracture in the right leg; he was attended to immediately, and went on favourably till the 3d of June, when he was attacked with erysipelas, caused by the fracture; it extended up the leg and thigh, and terminated in the formation of an abscess, which was opened, as occasion required - there was a great discharge - his constitution suffered very much - he died on the 11th of August. I have no doubt of the fractured leg causing his death.
 Cross-examined. Q. I believe you pronounced him quite out of danger on the 11th of July? A. Yes, I considered him so at that time: he was a robust man, and not likely to be afflicted with erysipelas without injury.
 MR.  HENRY JEFFRIES. I am a surgeon of St. George's Hospital. The deceased was under my particular care; he had a compound fracture of the right leg, both bones were broken, in consequence of which an attack of fever followed: on the 31st of May, or 1st of June, that fever subsided; on the 3d of June he had a relapse - abscesses formed in several parts of his leg and thigh - they were opened several times - I considered him then in considerable danger - fever followed, and he died on the 11th of August - the compound fracture was the cause of his death.
 Cross-examined. Q. In the state he died it is very probable that expressions of a hasty nature would escape him? A. The whole system was in a state of extreme irritation.
 Prisoner's Defence. I am heartily sorry the accident happened, and was not aware of it having happened.
   RICHARD FENNER. I am footman to Lady Lambert. On the 24th of May I was in attendance, and received directions from her Ladyship - the carriage was called, and I went for it; I found it drawn up at the corner of Duke-street, with the horses' heads in a direction to come on - it was not close to the gate-way; I told the prisoner to draw up; he at that time was not in the line to come in, because he could not get up; I went again, a second time, and found him jammed in the gateway, with another carriage - I repeated to him the order I had received for him to drive up as soon as he could - there was a great quantity of carriages behind him, at the time; I said her Ladyship was waiting, and that the carriage had been called for, I supposed, half an hour, and he must come up as soon as he could. I went to the house, and in about a quarter of an hour he came up, her Ladyship got in, and he drove away - I did not hear of the accident till next morning.
