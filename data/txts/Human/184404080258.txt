   Cross-examined by MR. WILDE . Q.  Mrs. Hobbs was tipsy, was she not? A.  She appeared so—I did not know her before. MARY BURNS. I live with my mother in Birdcage-alley, in Carroll's house—on this morning I was in bed when the deceased came home—I saw her about half past nine o'clock—she had been drinking—I saw the prisoner—they had words together—Mrs. Carroll was coming down stairs, and the de ceased coming up—Mrs. Carroll said to her, "Betsey, instead of your making Mrs. Burns tipsy, you had better give her 6 d . to get some breakfast"—my mother was very tipsy that morning and had been out with the deceased—Mrs. Hobbs said it was her own money, she had a right to do what she liked with it—Mrs. Carroll followed her up stairs to her own room—I heard a great deal of quarrelling between them, and crockery smashed—Mr. Carroll ran up stairs—I called out to know what it was all about—I heard him go into the room, and after that I heard a great deal of scuffling—I heard Mrs. Hobbs cry out, if her husband was at home, they would not ill-use her—I went up stairs once, and saw Mrs. Carroll standing on the landing, just by the room-door, with a cap in her hand, which was torn, and her hair hanging down her back, as if she had been in a scuffle—Mr. Carroll took the bed off deceased's bedstead, and brought it down stairs—he said, she should go out of the place—I did not see him put a hand on her—she called Mr. Carroll some very bad name, and threw something at him—at that time the deceased was bleeding behind her neck—I did not hear her say anything to Mr. Carroll about it—after Mr. Carroll went down stairs, the deceased followed him down to his own room door, and they were wrangling together there—I afterwards saw the deceased very sick, and she said, both Mr. and Mrs. Carroll had been beating her—that was the last word she spoke.
   Cross-examined. Q.  You never saw the prisoner strike her? A.  No—I heard Mr. Carroll cry out, "Unloose me; leave go of me." WILLIAM ALLEN. I lodged at this house—I first heard the noise a little after nine o'clock—I heard the deceased cry out "Murder"—my room is above hers—I came down stairs, and saw Mrs. Hobbs—she called Mr. Carroll very bad names several times—I interfered to take Mr. Carroll away, and advised him to go away—he said several times, "She has tried to job  me with the fork several times," and said, "If I had the poker I would knock her head off." I went down stairs, and Mr. Carroll followed me—in about half an hour the deceased came down stairs—I heard a quarrel about the boots and crockery between the deceased and Mr. and Mrs. Carroll—Mr. Carroll told her if she would replace the things she had broken he would give her the boots—they were then on a box in the room—a few minutes after, he com plained that she had run the fork into his lip—I did not see any sign of that—the deceased had a fork in her hand, and was trying to job  it at him—I saw her do  that upon the landing, but did not see it strike him—there was a piece of broken long below stairs in his room where we were at this time—Carroll
