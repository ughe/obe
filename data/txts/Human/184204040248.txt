I remember about six weeks ago receiving a letter from Miss Sarah Ann Biggs to take to the post-office—it was on a Saturday—I do not remember the day of the month—there was something in it—I do not know what—I took it to the post-office kept by Mrs. Weatherhead, nearly opposite—I gave it to the youngest daughter, the prisoner's sister—I asked her how much the postage was—she said 2 d  and I gave her 2 d .—a few days after Miss Cohen called at mistress's, and in consequence of what passed I went over to the post-office and saw the prisoner—I said the lady had come to our house, and she had not had the letter which I brought over two or three days ago, could she tell mistress how to trace it out—she said no, she could not tell her, there was no way of tracing it unless the post-office let them know—I said there was a purse in it, and mistress was very vexed about it—she said if that was ail, it was of no consequence, there were many worse losses than that.
   Cross-examined. Q.  At what time of day did you put the letter in? A.  About four or half-past four o'clock—I am sure it was between four and five. CATHERINE COHEN. I live at Mr. Hawker's, High-street, Kensington. I did not, in February, receive a letter from Miss Biggs, containing a silk purse—I did not know of her intention of sending one—I became apprised of it when I called on her on Wednesday or Thursday, about the 16th or 17th—I am not quite sure—I did not receive any letter from her in February. MATTHEW PEEK. I am a constable employed at the post-office. I went to the receiving-house in East-place, Lambeth on Friday, the 1st of April, with Mr. Bell and Mr. Peacock—the name of Weatherhead was over the door—I was never there before—I had a search warrant, and searched the house—the prisoner was not at home when I commenced—I found in the back-parlour this paper box on the sideboard by the side of the fire, and found this purse in it, wrapped in this blue paper—I am quite certain it is the same—here are my initials and the date on the paper, in my own writing—I laid the purse and box on the table, out of the box—I believe Mr. Bell put it into the box—the prisoner came in about five minutes after—Mr. Peacock, Mr. Bell, the prisoner's mother and sister were present—Mr. Peacock held the purse in his hand and said, "Who does this belong to?" or "Whose is this?"—the prisoner said, "It is mine"—the question was asked, "How long have you had it?"—she said about three months—"Where did you get it from?"—she said, "I had it given to me by a friend"—"Where did he get it?"—she said somewhere in the Strand—he bought it somewhere in the Strand—she was asked where about—she im mediately turned round, addressing her sister and said, "It is yours"—the sister said it was not—another question was put, I do not recollect what, but she made no answer—I finished my search, came down stairs, and the prisoner said she bought the purse at the Bazaar—I asked which, and she said she believed it was the one in Soho, she had been to them both—I was ordered to take her into custody, and took her to the post-office. and then to the Police Court, Bow-street—on our way there she said, "If I was to tell who took the purse, what would they be done to?"—
