 Court. Then you went away without giving a charge? - Mr. Sherwood's runners ordered us over the way till my master should come to us; we waited there from six till seven, and my master never came; my mother and I went both over the way, the justice said he did not want to hear what I had to say, but he heard my mother.
 When your master did not come you went over the way to the justice's without him? - Yes; the next day I was examined at the Rotation-office in Whitechapel.
 Where was this? - I cannot tell, I was a stranger; I did not know the place.
 To what justice was it you went, if it was not to Whitechapel, and where did he live? - I do not know the justice we went to then, but the next day I was examined at Whitechapel Rotation-office.
 Don't you know where it was? - No.
 Counsel. I mean a week before this when you went with Whitway to the Rotation-office, and you and your mother went away and the justice discharged him? - I went with two of Mr. Sherwood's runners; I do not know where to.
 You know Mr. Sherwood very well? - Yes.
 Counsel for the crown. How soon after your master's house was demolished did you first see your master? - I did not see him at all for four or five days after his house was destroyed; I did not know where he lodged or where he was; my mother and I went the next day to find him out, but we could not.
 Where did you first tell this story? - In Whitechapel, at the Rotation-office.
 When was the prisoner taken up? - I do not know.
 To whom did you first give any evidence against the prisoner? - First I went to Mr. Benjamin, then to the Rotation-office in Whitechapel, where Justice Sherwood examined me.
 Was that the first time you was examined? - Yes.
   MARY FRAZER sworn.
 I am the mother of the last witness. I lay at Mr. Lebarty's the night the mob came. I came about ten and went to bed about eleven o'clock; I went to sleep; when the mob came I jumped out of bed and opened the fore window; no sooner had I opened the window but there was a boy at the window; we had put lights in the window. I said I would open the door if they would not break it; before I could get to the top of the stairs the mob were up stairs; we lay in the back room; the mob thrust us into Mr. Lebarty's bed chamber; some boys opened the door of the fore room up one pair of stairs and the prisoner took a gun from the boy, and swore he would blow my heart out unless I would tell him where my master's money and papers were! after that another boy brought a sword or cutlass out of the same closet; the prisoner took that and pulled it out of the sheath; he held it over my head and said, he would split me in two if I did not tell him where my master's money and papers were. I said, gentlemen, don't do any mischief and cause murder, if you will go down stairs with me I will show you; the prisoner said I must stay a little till they had done their business there and then they would go down with me; we staid till they had destroyed what was in that room and thrown the furniture out of the window; I saw the prisoner beat out the frame of the window and throw the bed out I had laid on. He said D - n you, you old Papist bitch! come down stairs and show us where the money is; he several times called me a Popish bitch! I went down stairs; he followed me; I showed him the bar where Mr. Lebarty kept his liquor and money; I saw the prisoner go into the bar, and I came out immediately; that was the first opportunity I had of getting out of the house, and I saw no more of the prisoner; the back part of Mr. Lebarty's house was broke; whether he went that way or not I do not know; I saw him in the street by himself on the Sunday following in the forenoon. I knew him directly, because the chief thing I did in the room was to observe him.
