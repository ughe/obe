   Q.  Was that 30 l . paid in? A.  Yes, and I have it still at my banker's—I have never paid the interest, it has never been settled about that—the 30 l . if paid in at my banker's, as my money—I do not know whether all these entries in this book are in the prisoner's handwriting—whatever cash he re ceived himself he entered, and what other clerks paid him over he entered—it may be that some of the others have entered it, but he was the cashier—on 31st of May, 6th of June, and 28th of July—he had ten or twelve people in a day to call on to receive money—they were all small sums—they were entered every day—they were never in arrears beyond the next morning—I have lived at my present residence about five years—I have been an accountant for twenty years—I was a man of property before—when I first came to London I commenced as commission-agent—I was a blue-manufacturer—I was a bankrupt ten years ago, as a coal-merchant and bill-broker—I got my certificate six or seven years after—I have not been in any bankruptcy or insolvency since—I know nothing of the prisoner's family or connexion—he told me he had a wife—he has never asked me to go over the accounts with him, and settle them—I had a suspicion about Miss Cripps about three weeks before I took the prisoner into custody—I began to suspect him, and then I examined my books, and found he had altered them, and been carrying on a systematic system of robbery—I was horror-struck, because I had treated the man more like a brother than a clerk—I then went round to the different parties, and took a copy of their receipts—I became acquainted with this account of Miss Cripps about two days before the 24th of Dec.—I went down to Miss Cripps, and took a copy of the accounts—I had occasion to be in the City on Christmas-eve, and had dis covered other things of the prisoner, and I thought it right to arrest him—he had asked me in the morning, before I went out, if he could get away on the Monday, as he was going boxing—when I returned I brought an officer, and gave him into custody—I went before Mr. Rawlinson, the Magistrate, about this affair of Miss Cripps, on the 26th of Dec, and then I begged a remand for a week, that I might get ray evidence up—he was remanded, and at the end of that week the Magistrate asked if I had any other parties—I said, "Yes, a great many," and he said he had better remand him—I produced seven or eight of these parties, but only two of them were examined, (Mr. Devaux of Great Distaff-lane, and Miss Cripps)—the Magistrate did not tell me, when he had heard all my accusation, that it was a matter of account—he said, "Could you not arrange it?"—the Magistrate was not certain what it war—the pri soner's wife came to see me on this business. NOT GUILTY.
