Cross-examined by MR. DOANE . Q.  How long have you known the pri soner? A.  Two or three years—he was not employed by me—I have fre quently seen the deceased and other carters in company—I do not know whether it was a common trick to pull out the trap-stick when the cart was empty—I was in the cart while it was being loaded—there were six sacks of potatoes in it before I went into it. DANIEL JEWITT. I saw the cart—when the seventh sack was put in at the tail of the cart, up went the whole cart, and they were both shot out—after it was over, I saw the pin lying in the front of the cart out of its proper place—I saw the deceased at the hospital on the Monday fol lowing.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Were you present while the cart was being loaded? A.  I was present when they put the sack in, and it tripped up—I was not present at the commencement of the loading of the cart—I saw no one near the pin. GEORGE BELL. I am in the employ of Mr. Smith. I saw the prisoner on the Monday after the accident—Wood was then at the hospital in a bad way—I heard the prisoner speadking to, other person in Mill-lane, and went up—he said he took the stick out, but it was not with the intention of doing any harm, as he had seen it done before—he was crying, and said he was very sorry.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Had you observed the cart? A.  No—I saw it empty—it was waiting for a fare some time—any of the carters had an op portunity of taking out the pin while it was empty. JAMES KEOUGH. I am a cowkeeper, and live in Dean-street, Tooley-street. The prisoner has worked for me for some time—I just came by after the accident happened—the prisoner was not there—I saw him on the Monday following—Wood was then dead—the prisoner said there were several persons, he thought, accused of pulling the trap-stick out, and he was the person who actually did do it, but not with intent to do him any harm as he had seen it done several times before; and I have seen it done myself by other people—that least weight will tilt a cart up when the pin is out—I should think the weight of a man getting into the cart would do it—the prisoner seemed very much affected.
   Cross-examined. Q.  He has been two or three years in your service? A.  Yes, off and on—he is a well-conducted, quiet, industrious young man—I believe he was on very good terms with the deceased—this was a silly frolic. WILLIAM ROBERT JONES. The deceased was brought to Guy's Hos pital, one Saturday evening, between six and seven o'clock—I do not re member the day of the month—I believe it was about two months since—he was insensible—on any question being put to him, he would utter some thing quite unintelligible, but he became worse shortly after—he lived till between one and two o'clock on Sunday morning—after death, I examined the state of his head very minutely—I found a fracture in the back part of his head, extending from a point where I suppose a blow to have been re ceived—the brain also had suffered from the violence of the blow, and there was blood effused from small vessels, ruptured—I should say his death was caused by injury on the head—his death was caused by the fracture of the skull which I have described—it was that, the effusion of blood, and concussion also—I have not a doubt of its being the cause of death.
