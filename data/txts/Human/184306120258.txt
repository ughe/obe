   MR. CLARKSON . Q.  Had you any knowledge whether Walders was working for anybody or not? A.  No more than from Edwards telling me that we should be likely to find him there, as he generally portered in the market.
   COURT . Q.  When you applied to Reardon's master for him, did you say what you wanted him for? A.  No, nor who I was. JOSEPH MAHON. I am a stationer, carrying on business in Blackman-street, Borough. I have a warehouse in a stable-yard in Great Suffolk-street, where I mostly deposit waste paper, done up in bundles and bags, a variety of millboards and rags—in the years 1839, 1840, and 1841. I had deposited about thirty tons of large bundles of paper, which I purchased of the Excise—about a week before Wednesday, the 26th of April, I saw that paper in my warehouse all safe, except a portion that I had removed myself—I am not in the habit of selling the paper in the state of that produced—that is the state in which I buy it—in consequence of information from Oliveira, I went to the warehouse on the morning of the 27th, (I was not at home on the 26th)—I missed about seven tons of paper, and very near two tons in bags—I missed my scale-beams and rope, a quantity of linen rags and millboard—I had the paper in stacks according to their size—there was seven large stacks, and one or two dwarf ones—there was about three tons and a half gone out of one stack of one particular size, and others out of different stacks—the value of the whole seven tons I consider to be about 200 l ., the bags about 30 l ., averaging 1 l . a bag, the rags about 3 l ., and the scales and beam about 2 l .—I have since been shown by the officers some paper, said to be found in an unoccupied room in Pump-court—it was part of the paper I lost—my ware-house is in the parish of St. George-the-Martyr, Southwark—it is called St. George.
