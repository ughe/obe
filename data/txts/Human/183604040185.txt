   MR. SCARLETT . Q.  You have been asked about a press of busi ness on this particular night—if there was, would it not save the time of the President to employ a messenger, instead of going himself to demand the postage? A.  Yes, if the messenger was not engaged at the time.
   COURT . Q.  You say it would be the duty of any messenger to take this letter—do you mean any messenger who happened to be going that way? A.  The messengers are continually coming in to carry the empty bags, and we always send letters by them to the tunnel—if he sees a letter lying there, he might take it and the money, without being directed—I do not know that this is his duty—we usually direct them—I called for a messenger to take the letter which I put behind me, with the postage—we do not allow a messenger to take a letter from behind us without being directed to do so—it was Mr. Teasdale's letter that I put behind me—no letter coming to the Post Office in the usual way could arrive at the Inland Office through the Foreign Office, without the directions of the President or my self, as the opener of the bag—a letter written in the office would go by my directions, by putting the postage on the table—the President might have sent either of these letters in any way he thought fit.
   MR. PRICE . Q.  Does not the maker up of the bundle occasionally send a messenger for the money, without the knowledge of the President? A.  I never heard of such a circumstance. RICHARD CRADDOCK. On Wednesday evening, the 2nd of March, I was on duty at the tunnel, on the Inland Office—it is my duty to receive the letters and postage, which come from the Foreign Office to the Inland Office by the tunnel—I went on duty at a quarter before five o'clock, and left at eight o'clock—I was induced to pay particular attention that even ing to the letters and postage which came through—I received one private letter, belonging to a clerk in the office, with the postage of 8 d .—it was addressed to Teasdale, of Bognor, and I think Mrs. Teasdale—there is a clerk in the Foreign Post Office, of the name of Teasdale—it is the custom of the clerks to transmit their letters in that way—I receive them fre quently in that way—I received two newspapers on that evening for the Ship-letter Office—the postage was 2 d  —I did not notice their address, more than that they were for the Ship-letter Office—I did not receive any postage of 6 s .4 s ., nor of 4 d . that evening—I received no other postage than what I have stated—the tunnel closes at eight o'clock—I remained there till the tunnel closed.
