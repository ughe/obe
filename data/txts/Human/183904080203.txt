   COURT . Q.  Can you form any judgment as to what that is really worth? A.  I asked a person in the trade, and he said he could buy it at 1 s . 3 d . a yard.
   MR. BODKIN . Q.  Would you have advanced the money you did, on that piece of flannel, if you had thought it contained no more than it really does? A.  I should not, a shilling a yard is the utmost I should have lent upon it—in the evening of the same day, Baynton came to the shop, and asked me to take in another piece of flannel—he did not bring it—I asked him if it was the same quality—he said, "Yes, just the same"—I asked what it contained—he said the usual quantity, about 138 or 140 yards—he wanted 8 l . on that—I offered him 7 s .—he would not take that—he took 7 l . 10 s .—he went outside, and brought in the flannel—I did not look at the mark on that piece, at the time, but I have measured it since, and there are 80 yards—it appears to me to be the same kind of flannel as the first piece—1 s . a yard is as much as I would have advanced on it—I did not see either of the prisoners again, till I was at the Mansion House—a woman called on me, and from what I heard I measured the flannel.
   COURT . Q.  In what place of custody had the flannels been kept? A.  In our warehouse, I rolled them up myself, and the boy took them up stairs.
   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  How many shopmen have you? A.  Two, besides me and the warehouse boy—I had the flannels about a fortnight before I measured them—only those persons who are in the busi ness have access to them—it is not worth more than 1 s . a yard for a pawn-broker to lend.
