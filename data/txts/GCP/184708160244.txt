Cross-examined by Mr. PAYNE. Q. Did you know the prisoner before 1
A. No, I had never seen him before-when he was roughing up Blake's hair
he said, "A little whiting and blue would make you a good colour"-he
seemed very foolish, and full of frolic and fun-the prisoner is much taller
than Blake-he took hold of him and took him off his feet-when he knocked
his bead against the door, Blake said, "Now, then," and that was every word
he spoke-he appeared to be quite in the prisoner's power when he was
whirling round--they appeared to go found and round as if shifted by some
machine-when he came back, and said what I have stated, he was mumbling
about the boy, and appeared to be saying something about his getting the
best of him-I did not hear him say to Blake, "Oh, it is you that rolled me
out of the shop?"-Blake was not intoxicated, he was sitting as quiet as a
child-the deceased had a bottle in one hand and a jug in the other, I
cannot say whether she was sober-the street had been dug up, to repair the
water-pipe, a place had been left lower than the other part of the road, and
the deceased appeared to fall in that place.
Mr. PLATT. Q. The deceased was near that spot? A. Yes, she must
have had her foot on the edge of the path, for she spilt some of the beer on
the edge of the path, directly opposite-her feet appeared to go just down by
the door, and her head into this place that was not repaired -1 do not think
she stepped from the spot after she was struck.
COURT. Q. You state that the prisoner was drunk, and that what he was
doing was in the nature of fun? 4. Yes, it would appear like it-what he
was doing to the boy was like what a foolish drunken man would intend as fun
- it did not appear like an assault or ill-usage, but more in the nature of play
-the boy was unusually quiet, according to his character in general-he let
him go on, I think, on account of the things in the window, for I called out,
"Mind the boxes, do not go through the glass."
WILLIAM BLAKE. I am twenty years old, and live with Mr. Digweed. I
was in the shop on Saturday night, the 10th of July, when the prisoner came
in-he first spoke to my mistress, then turned to me, pulled my bair, pulled me
off the tub I was sitting on, and pulled me from the door to the counter, and
from the counter to the door; he then let go my hair, put his left-hand round
my neck, and pulled me outside the door, and when he got me outside, be
put both hands on my hair, gave me a pull towards him, and a shove from
him we both went out together round about-he did this against my will
- I could not help myself, but I took it in the light of a joke.
Court. Q. You thought it was the folly of a drunken man ? A. Yes,
and let him have his humour as he did not hurt me much-I did not care
about it, and never intended to complain of it.
Mr. Justice Erle being of opinion that the prisoner was not proved to be
engaged in any unlawful act at the time the accident occurred, the Jury found
