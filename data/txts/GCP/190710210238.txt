Williams and Wilcocks was earlier in date than my investment with

if I dealt with a firm that was not on the Stock Exchange that was
a "bucket" shop. When I entered into this deal with Tyler I did not
think I was making an investment in Industrial shares. I knew it was
a speculation in differences. I looked at the prospectus, which tells
you it is perfectly safe and you never lose anything.
Mr. F. E. Smith. But, you remember, the statement about not
losing anything is qualified by the words, "should the quotation re-
main the same." You took it with a grain of salt, did not you, with
your experience.
Witness. I knew I should not keep the holding long. I know, of
course, that £1 would not buy a £97 share, and I knew I was having,
a deal in differences which might turn out well or might turn ou:
badly; at the same time, I saw on the papers it was very likely to be
a good thing. I thought from the prospectus it would turn out well.
I would not, of course, have given any money for nothing. Assuming
that I would be paid, I was perfectly prepared to have a gamble.
I suppose that would be the object everybody bets on the Stock
Exchange. I did not think I was only gambling in differences be-
cause I was to have a dividend on my money as well. I supposed
that stock dealers would have the stock if they wanted to sell it. If
they were going to pay a dividend they must have it. I understood
from the circular that "should the quotation remain the same" I
should make this profit, and that if, on the other hand, the quotation
fell I was exposed to risk. As to whether I understood the prin.
ciple on which this speculation was based, I understood that as soon
as the dividend was paid I would claim it, and I knew it was only &
few days before I could claim it. I expeoted Tylers to settle when
it would be paid ; they did not do anything at all.
