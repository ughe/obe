 745. SARAH PERRY was indicted for stealing 2 boots, value 2 s  . the goods of George Underwood . GEORGE UNDERWOOD. I live with my father, George Underwood, a boot and shoe maker, in Chapel-street, Somers-town. About nine o'clock in the evening of the 6th Feb. the prisoner came and asked the price of a pair of shoes in the window—I told her, and she went away—she came again in custody of the constable, who had these two odd boots, which are my father's—I had seen them safe on the window about ten minutes before. THOMAS MORTON( police-constable S  1.) About nine o'clock on this evening I saw the prisoner walking very fast in Chapel-street—a person was in pursuit of her—I stopped her—the person was accusing her of stealing a kettle—I found these boots under her arm—she said, "They are mine"—I took her down to the prosecutor's shop, and he identified them.
   Prisoner's Defence.  I was coming through Chapel-street, and these two odd shoes laid apart, I picked them up. GUILTY. Aged 44.— Confined Two Months .

 746. THOMAS SARGEANT was indicted for stealing 1 sovereign, 1 half-sovereign, 3 crowns, 6 half-crowns, 14 shillings, 2 sixpences, 30 four penny pieces, 30 pence, and 16 halfpence, the monies of Francis Tucker and others, his masters.
   MESSRS. CLARKSON and BALLANTINE conducted the Prosecution. JOHN MURRELL. I am porter to Francis Tucker and two others, of South Moulton-street. In consequence of observations I made of the shop cash, during Aug. and Sept., I made a communication to my employers—I after wards observed the entries made in the cash-book—I took particular observa tion of the dripping and other stuff that was purchased during the 9th of Sept., 1843—on that day John Tarrant, who is usually in the shop, went to Gravesend—I remained there besides the prisoner—we were the only persons in the shop—at two o'clock the prisoner went to dinner—I looked at the drip ping and the change—the dripping is kept in a large tab in the cellar, and the change on a board about three feet by two and a half—the tub would contain about three hundredweight of dripping or a little more—I took the trowel which is usually kept in the cellar and smoothed the surface of the dripping in the tub, and I made a cross on it all the way across the tub—I then examined the changes on the board—there was about two stone of it, just sufficient to cover the board—I returned to the shop and looked into the drawer which is used for the reception of dripping in the shop, before it is taken down to the tub in the cellar—I found the drawer quite empty—I have the shop cash-book here—it was the prisoner's duty to purchase dripping and articles of that description in the shop, and enter them in the book—while he was out that day I examined this cash-book—I found the last entry was two stone of changes, by which it appears that they had been delivered before he went to dinner, and there was about two stone of changes on the board or dresser—on the prisoner's return about three, I left the shop—I returned a little after four—I
