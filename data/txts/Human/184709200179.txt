MR. BALLANTINE. Q.  Do you that you extracted the whole that there was in the stomach at the time? A.  Yes—if anything is allowed for loss it would be but a few hundredths of a grain—the taking of arsenic produce a burning sensation in the throat—that comes on some little time afterwards—in some cases it will take half an hour or more, it is very uncer tain—the operation of arsenic is extremely uncertain in point of time, vary ing from half an hour to four or five—the sickness usually begins with pains in the stomach, but that is also uncertain—sickness frequently comes on a very short time afterwards, but sometimes we find cases where sickness does not occur at all—the pain is principally in the stomach and back part of the throat—that would render a person indisposed to eat or drink—sometimes it causes considerable thirst—they feel disposed to take cold liquids, but indis posed to take their food—very probably a little eaten would bring on retching and sickness.
   COURT. Q.  In what period of time would the indisposition to eat com monly arise? A.  It would be very difficult to say, it would depend so much upon the period at which the person began to eat before taking the arsenic—if it is taken upon a full stomach it does not act so speedily as if it is taken upon an empty stomach—if it was taken about an hour before dinner, probably at dinner time the person would not feel disposed to eat—that is the ordinary consequence of an exhibition of arsenic.
   MR. BALLANTINE. Q.  You have some powders there? A.  Yes, they were brought to me by the beadle as having been found in the house—amongst them there is an aloetic pill—it is what is usually called bitter aloes—it contains bitter aloes—it is an aperient medicine—here is also some jalap—jalap is about as innocent a medicine as anybody can take—it some times slightly irritates the stomach—it not unfrequently causes a little nausea—it is a mild aperient—there is no direction upon these powders as to the amount to be taken—I could not tell whether was any jalap in the stomach—jalap is a vegetable—I did not apply any test to ascertain the existence of any vegetable matter in the stomach, and am not able to state whether there was any vegetable poison or medicine in the stomach at that time—if she had taken any large quantity of jalap in the course of the day upon which she died, I might possibly have found some traces of it in the stomach, but after so much vomiting it would be unlikely.
   MR. BODKIN. Q.  Would it be at all unlikely, a person's stomach being deranged, that jalap should cause vomiting? A.  It would be extremely likely, it is a nauseous medicine—that vomiting, if caused by taking jalap or an aloetic medicine, would indispose the party to eat—a deranged stomach alone would indispose a party to eat—the immediate action of arsenic is by eating into the coats of the stomach—if the stomach is empty the arsenic is more quick in its operation—if it was taken an hour before dinner, upon an empty stomach, and caused nausea and vomiting, I should expect those symptoms to be accompanied by violent heat and pain in the region of the stomach—that would probably precede the vomiting.
