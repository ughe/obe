   COURT . Q.  You saw him pass your door at half-past ten o'clock—that is all you know about it? A.  Yes; I did not see him in bed.
   JURY . Q.  What was to prevent him going out? A.  I did not say he did not go out, but his father would not allow him. GEORGE BAKER. I am the prisoner's father. On Sunday, the 13th of March, I solemnly declare he was in bed at half-past ten o'clock—I went to bed at a quarter before eleven o'clock—I saw him in bed then.
   COURT . Q.  What are you? A.  A linen-weaver by trade, but I do not work at it—I am a porter to an upholsterer now—I have worked for Edward Stolworthy twelve years, and I am in his employment now.
   JURY . Q.  Did you and your son sleep in the same bed? A.  No—I was not asleep at twelve o'clock, nor half-past twelve—I must solemnly swear he did not get up, nor did any person in my house—I was awake till half-past twelve o'clock—I was not at one o'clock—my house is situated at No. 21, Lewes-buildings, Liquorpond-street—I have a clock close to my ear—I burn a rush-light, and at half-past twelve o'clock, I looked at clock.
   COURT . Q.  How do you happen to remember all this so particularly? A.  In consequence of his being taken up the Tuesday night after.
   JURY . Q.  Are you certain you were in the room at half-past twelve o'clock? A.  Yes; he could not have been out without my knowing it—I went to bed at a quarter before eleven o'clock, and about half-past twelve o'clock I dropped off to sleep—when the prisoner was brought up to Marlborough-street, the policeman brought a woman, who was in the tobacconist's shop, and she looked at him, and said that was not the man. RICHARD MARKAM re-examined . It is false—there was a woman at the back of me when we stood in the tobacconist's, as I saw there was evidence enough—I did not wish her to speak—she said he was very much like the man, but she could not swear to him. GEORGE BAKER. When the woman not swear according to his wishes, he would not let her in—I endeavoured to find where she lived—they could find her, but I could not, and they would not let her in. NOT GUILTY.

PHILIPPA GRINDELL. I live in Great Portland-street, my husband is an upholsterer. The prisoner came to me to be employed as a needle-woman, on the 18th of March, about nine o'clock in the morning—I em ployed her till about half-past ten o'clock, when I missed her out of the room—when the work-people were going, we found she had taken every shawl and bonnet out of the place—I never saw her again till the Monday following, when I saw her in Regent-street, looking into a window—I fol lowed her till I sent for an officer, and had her taken.
