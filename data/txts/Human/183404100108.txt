 MR. BODKIN. Q. During your acquaintance with the prisoner, did you ever know him as Captain Wainwright? A. No.
 JURY. Q. Did you expect such an order as that from Captain Gordon? A. None - I do not live at the British Hotel, nor are my letters directed there.

 Prisoner's Defence. My Lord, and Gentlemen of the Jury, although a gentleman by birth and education, I am so paralyzed by the disgrace and ignominy of my present situation, that I have not power to address you verbally, although my life or liberty might be the forfeit of my silence. I must therefore trust to this less effective mode of expressing to you the truth of my case. My father was a man of high respectability, nearly related to the banker and the baronet of the same name; he was an East India director, and I need not add therefore of good fortune. The wants of a man are more or less in proportion to his station in life. My life was that of a gentleman moving in the best society, and to keep a footing with those I associated with, required pecuniary means of considerable extent, and those means were supplied by the hand of a kind and affectionate parent, while my father lived; unfortunately he died, in 1832, and then, for the first time, it was found that his affairs were insolvent, and I, of course, destitute and pennyless. I was of no profession, for I had quitted the army in my father's lifetime, and of course had great difficulty to live and support myself and wife. I had some trifling assistance from friends, but that of course could not last, and I was unable to extricate myself from my embarrassments, and was obliged to keep myself secret and change my name; and, for fear of being arrested, took the name of Wainwright, and went to Ireland, where a friend of mine was with his regiment; this friend was Captain Duncan Campbell, of the 47th: he knew me both by my own name of Prescott, and by the assumed name of Wainwright; and, as I had held a commission in a dragoon regiment, I had (although retired from the service) been generally since styled Captain, which accounts for my being called Captain Wainwright, as I had been called Captain Prescott; and so friendly was Captain Campbell with me, that he advised me to continue the same name, and I corresponded with him by that name when I left Ireland and came to London. At length, my distress and difficulty increasing, I wrote to him for the loan of 50l., and an order came for the amount from a Captain Gordon; this order was addressed to me as "Captain Wainwright, British Hotel, Cockspur-street, London." I there received it, as I had received other letters, and then, as has been stated, presented it for payment on the one day, and received the amount on a subsequent day. Now, gentlemen, where is the forgery? Was I not the person who wrote to Captain Campbell? If I were not the person intended to have the money, who was? My brother-in-law's name, 'tis true, is Wainwright; his name is not however Charles Wainwright, but Henry Maxwell Wainwright; he lives at Aske, near Farnham, in Surrey, forty miles from London. Did he write to Captain Campbell or Captain Gordon for the loan of money? I humbly submit that it is the duty of the prosecutors, before they ask for a verdict of guilty, to convince you that I am not the person who was intended to have this money, by producing the person who was. Captain Gordon intended it for the person who wrote to Captain Campbell; and I am that person, otherwise I should not have received the letter addressed to me. Again, when I presented the order the amount was not paid, but I was directed to return some days after. Would a guilty man have returned? No; he would have sent a messenger, or done any act rather than ventured again to the same spot. I had no such fear; I was doing nothing wrong; and I returned and signed the draft which the clerk placed before me. If any other person had been in expectation of such order for 50l., that person would have gone to the British Hotel, for the letter, and to Cox and Greenwood's to inquire. Is there any proof that any, even the least, inquiry was made there for the letter? and if any other person but myself had expected it, and had been disappointed, would not that person have been a witness here to-day, and complained that he had been defrauded of the 50l. which had been sent him? Gentlemen, the facts resolve themselves into this - I was distressed, and in fear of my creditors, and to avoid arrest, assumed the name of Captain Charles Wainwright: my friend, Captain Campbell, knew it, and corresponded with me accordingly. I wrote to him for a loan, which was sent me by his friend; and I signed the name I had assumed, and by which I was known in the transaction. What forgery is this? What was my conduct when apprehended? - The officer said he had a warrant against me for fraud: I said I knew nothing of it. It was true; I knew of no fraud. He said it was from Cox and Greenwood's. Knowing I had committed no fraud, I offered to go there with him, but he refused to allow me: and then he mentioned the name of Wainwright; on which I instantly named the transaction, and the sum received. Did this look like guilt, gentlemen? No; I had nothing to conceal. I had received the money intended for me, and supposed, because I had not repaid it, that I was to be arrested for the amount, and, of course, was anxious to avoid a prison, not merely on account of the 50l., but for fear of other creditors.
