It was not his way home to break through the ranks, because his way home was either by the Archbishop of York's house, or he might have gone down a little street which comes from Southampton-row, and King-street.
 Could he have gone by the archbishop's house? - Yes, that was left open. After I had done that I went to the Secretary of State's office, and saw Mr. Frazier; I told Mr. Frazier I was very sorry that there were so many servants concerned in that riot, amongst which I mentioned that I had seen a servant of Sir Thomas Sewell's.
 What was the part which the prisoner seemed to take? - He seemed to be animating the mob.
 During the time you saw the prisoner there, what were the rest of the mob doing? - Behaving very violent, ballooing, crying No Popery! throwing stones and glass bottles. One of the privates of the guards was wounded exceedingly, and Mr. Lowton was wounded with a glass bottle; the mob were then pulling down Lord Mansfield's house and burning the furniture.
 Cross Examination.
 The prisoner was in liquor? - Yes.
 He had no arms nor any weapon with him? - I did not see him have any.
 Captain  BARNET sworn.
 I was detached to Bloomsbury-square about ten o'clock; I remained there till five in the morning.
 Do you remember at any time in the course of that night or morning seeing the prisoner,  David Evans? - I do; I first saw him at half past one.
 Did you observe him doing or saying any thing? - Not doing any thing, but talking much with other servants. There was a servant with him who was extremely insolent to me, and was abusing the guard.
 How long do you think you saw the prisoner there? - I saw him there from half past one till five.
 Had you occasion to observe his conduct at any other time? - Not particularly till the morning, when he attempted to pass the guard, and was told by them that he could not pass that way, he remained some time in front of the guard, then he forced his way through. Mr. Parker told him He knew his master and desired him to go about his business. Colonel  Woodford, who was commanding officer at that time, and came with a detachment to assist me, said if he did not go on immediately about his business, he would take him into custody. I did not see him afterwards.
 Cross Examination.
 It was another person who was so abusive? - Yes.
 He was outside the guard the whole time? - He was continually passing and re-passing my guard during the whole time, and he once came up and spoke to the serjeant of the guard; I told him to go away from the guard.
 - ESCOURT sworn.
 I was at Lord Mansfield's at about two o'clock; and I was there at three o'clock, and from then till between five and six in the morning. I was there when Mr. Parker came with a party of guards. Mr. Parker appointed me a constable; I remained there till between five and six in the morning, when the whole business was over. After the soldiers had fired between five and six o'clock, I think it was past five, I was called to by some of the people as a peace officer; they said, Sir, here is a servant making a great riot, I wish you would come and speak to him. The prisoner was then near the rails which surround the square on that side of the line of soldiers which came from the Duke of Bedford's wall to the rails; I went and told him I knew him very well, and I knew his master Sir  Thomas Sewell. I advised him to go home, or I must take him into custody. He recollected me; he made no answer nor did I stay there any longer; I turned about and never saw him after.
 At what time was that? - After the soldiers had fired; it was about five o'clock, I believe.
 Court. Had you observed him before the people told you there was a servant very troublesome? - No, I had not seen him.
