CHARLES COLE. I am a job-master, and live in Little Guildford-street. I was at the Three Compasses—I did not see the prisoner come in—I saw him come out of the tap-room—no one interrupted or said anything to him—he asked for a pint of beer—he had got a little bit of tin in his hand, which the landlord refused to take—he then turned out with a little tiff  —in about ten minutes he came in again, took a pint pot up, and cut three holes in the counter—the landlord was sent for—he came out of the parlour, and the prisoner was put out of the door—in about ten minutes or a quarter of as hour he came back, and beat the door three or four times, apparently with a weapon—the prosecutor went to carry a pot of beer home for his supper, and he caught the weapon on his head—I heard a loud cry for me—I stepped out immediately—I saw Butterworth lying on the ground, and the prisoner beating him with the tongs—he struck him in my presence—I went and took him by the collar, put my foot behind him, and he fell in the gutter—I imme diately took the tongs and pushed them into the street—a man took them into the public-house—they were very heavy—he had gone home for them to his own house—when I went up to Butterworth the blood was streaming down on the flag-stone—if I had not rescued him he would have been seriously injured—he was insensible for about an hour and twenty minutes—he was wounded all about his face—we took him on a stretcher to the station—a surgeon was sent for, who washed and dressed the wound—it bled a great deal—the blood lay next morning on the stones—this is the weapon—( produced. )
   Cross-examined. Q.  The prisoner lives within a door or two of this house? A.  I understand so—I do not think he was very far gone in liquor—I would not swear he was not—the landlord had not spoken cross to him, or any one else in the house—if he had not put him out, we should all have been cut to pieces with this pint pot—I was sober—I had not been in the house more than twenty minutes—I thought it right to assist the man—I did not strike the prisoner—I do not think he had his head tied up when he came into the Compasses—I saw him next day at Union-hall—he appeared as if he had got a bruise at the side of his face—I did not notice that it was very much swollen—I did not give it him—I cannot say whether the prosecutor did—I do not know that his jaw was broken on this occasion—I have heard strange tales about it—his face was not tied up at Union-hall, that I saw, nor bloody, or his shirt, that I noticed—I did not strike or kick him. JOHN MENHINICK( police-constable M  197.) On the 15th of July I saw the prisoner and prosecutor both lying on the ground—the prosecutor was bleeding profusely from a cut on the temple—I took the prisoner to the station—I saw this pair of tongs—the prisoner told me next morning that they were his.
   Cross-examined. Q.  When you went up it was all over? A.  Yes—the prisoner did not appear to be in pain—he said, "Do you want me?"—I did not see any blood—I cannot say how he came in the state he is in now—I saw him at the Magistrate's office—he had a blow in the face, bat it was not tied up at all—I do not know that he has had a broken jaw—I have heard that he has been under the care of Mr. Maynard, in the gaol, ever since he has been there. GUILTY of an Assault.  Aged 30.— Confined Six Weeks .
