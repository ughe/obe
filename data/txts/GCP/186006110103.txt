Cross-examined by Mr. Ribron. . Were you lying down ! A. No; I
was in a convenient posture; sometimes on my knees and sometimes stoop-
ing-I do not know whether the hole was an inch wide ; it was not three-
I looked down from very nearly as high as this Court; twenty feet-it is a
sloping roof-my feet were against the parapet wall, in the gutter, and my
body resting on the slates--I had some chaise cushions put there—the cart
and horse were brought into the house they were both under me, and the
scale was under me-I had a very extended view—the three prisoners and
Payne were moving about--I said nothing to Eddington about it; on 5th
April I let his cart go away—I took a note of the five baskets on that day in
& memorandum book-I chalked down one on a card each time I saw a rob-
bery committed—the bones were only re-weighed on one occasion ; the last
it is about the length of this Court from the scale to where the bones are
put-they were thrown, on the 27th, into a bin prepared for them by tiers
of casks—there were only two compartments, one where Eddington put his,
and the other was occupied by the customer whose bones were being taken in,
into which marrow-bones had been shot before the re-weighing took place
they were separated by a tier of large casks, bigger than beer barrels
Eddington was our bone collector; I am not aware that we solicited him,
it is quite possible--he goes about collecting bones and delivers them to us in
cart-loads—there is also what is called waste there were some baskets of waste
delivered on the 27th, but I do not know how many—I did not pay atten-
tion, because marrow-bones were those which the fraud was committed with-
there was nothing improper done with the waste-it was called out in the
same way with the other baskets, and was shot in a different part altogether
-it was not re-weighed—it is not possible that a basket of waste was shot
and called out as a basket of marrow-bones; you might as well say that
a basket of soot would be taken for a basket of salt-it is not possible for
the waste to be entered as marrow-bones, because the marrow-bones were
weighed on the 28th, and washed singly—the marrow-bones and the waste
were being weighed at the same time the waste consists of the bones of
smaller animals-marrow-bones are from the legs of larger animals, oxen,
and small nutton bones, and blade-bones—it has not often occurred that
what ought to be entered as waste has been entered as marrow-bones-I
never heard of it, nor bave we been obliged to refer to the men and ask
them about it-I swear that marrow-bones are not entered as waste I was
in the same place on the 27th-there was nobody with me--I think it was
after six.
Mr. ORRIDGE. Q. Are you always present when bones are sold to you?
4. No-a basket of waste is known by its appearance, and it is called waste,
and is booked and carried to a different part-the marrow-bones are nearly
three times the price of waste.
MR. Ribron. Q. Have you paid him for these bones! A. Yes ; we paid
him for what were delivered on the 27th-since this case was sent by the
Magistrate for trial we have not paid him for the bones that we were over-
charged for on the 27th, but on previous occasions—we have paid him for
these since this case has been sent for trial.
Mr. ORRIDGE. Q. Were you applied to for payment? 4. Yes ; by an
attorney, and under advice we paid it.
