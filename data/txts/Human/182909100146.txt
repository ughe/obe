   SAMUEL HOUGH. I was in Mr. Edward's employ, and received this property to take to Mr. Moore's, in St. Martin's-lane, but I took it to my father's house, as I have done before - I double locked the street door, and the next morning the box of the lock was off, the lock double-shot, and the door broken open; I ran out into the street very much alarmed, and a young woman said she saw two men, one in a fustian dress, a tall thin man, and the other a short stout man, with a blue frock coat and black apron.
 Cross-examined. Q. What kind of house is your father's? A. A very large house - I was quite surprised at finding the door open; I had locked it - the windows are nailed up all but one; there are not above two or three panes broken - my master has said before, that if I felt myself tired I might leave my work at my father's; I was taken up, and so was my father; we have served a writ on Mr. Edwards - Smith came to examine the door; he did not examine it very attentively; he came tipsy with another man - I do not know whether he examined the door - he was standing on the steps; I suppose his eyes were three quarters of a yard from the door.
 COURT. Q. You thought, from your master's practice, you might leave these at your father's? A. Yes: I was to have received some more in the wood at Mr. Moore's, and he said if I was tired I might leave them at my father's.
 MR. EDWARDS. I went out about dinner time, and told him I wished him to leave work at seven o'clock, and take them.
   RACHAEL WILLIAMS. I was near Mr. Hough's house about half-past five o'clock that morning, and saw two young men come out of the house without anything; they went towards Gough-square - the prisoner has the features of one of them; I observed he was a stout man, but I cannot say it was the prisoner - before six o'clock I saw a man go and push the door open, go to the corner, then go into the house, and bring something out in a green cloth on his shoulder, and go towards Gough-square; I did not know they were mouldings - I afterwards saw the boy crying, and said, "What have you lost?" he told me, and I said, "It was gone out of your house before six o'clock."
   GEORGE FAIRBROTHER. I am a picture-frame maker. The prisoner came to me about the 10th of August - he called me down stairs, and said a person at the end of the court had something to sell in my way; he did not know exactly what; I went to the end of the court - a person there pulled out a piece of moulding, and said he had some hundreds of yards of it for sale; I said I did not want any of it - the prisoner was at the opposite side of the court; the mouldings were similar to these - I told Mr. Hough of it, and he asked if I could tell him where the person lived who offered them; I took him to the prisoner's house, in Star-court - he is a very respectable man; he is lame - no one can see him walk without knowing it; he goes very much on one side.
 Cross-examined. Q. How long have you known him? A. About four years: I was not at the public-house when he was apprehended - I came in afterwards, and went to the watch-house; I did not hear him say he would rather be hanged than split - I do not think he did.
   SAMUEL HOUGH. The prisoner said he thought he was very ill-used; the officer said, "You had better tell who was with you" - he said, "I will rather have my b-y neck scragged than split."
   JOHN DUNCAN. I received the prisoner on the 11th of August. In taking him to the watch-house, I said he should tell where he got them - he said he would suffer his neck to be twisted, or something, before he would let any one into it; they were found at the Spotted Dog, in Clement's-lane.
 Cross-examined. Q. Was not the word he used, that he would sooner have his neck twisted than peach? A. That might be the word - I understood sooner than implicate another person.
 COURT. Q. What was it? A. I should surmise that there was some other person connected with the robbery, and he would sooner take the whole weight upon himself than tell of any other person - Fairbrother was in custody at the time.
 Cross-examined. Q. He spoke rather angrily, I believe? A. Yes; I cannot charge my memory as to whether I spoke to him about my being in difficulties, and had got over them - I might have said it, or I might not; I have been tried, but I really do not know whether I said so to him or no - it was an attempt at a rape charged against me - you pleaded the cause for me, and I was honourably acquitted.
 Prisoner's Defence. I am totally innocent; it is necessary to state that I wear a cork shoe four inches high - this girl said she saw some person come out like my features; this prosecution has been got up for the money - this man got bills stuck up; a person came to me, and asked if I knew a person in the gilding line - I said, Yes, and directed him to Fairbrother; Hough then came to me with my wife, and made me drink; as he told the Alderman - he made people drunk, and then got any thing out of them; he then said I knew a person who had some gilt mouldings, and if I would bring them, he would give me 2l., and no questions should be asked - he then sent for a constable, and had us all taken.    NOT GUILTY.

 SECOND COUNT, stating them to be the goods of  Jane Clarke.
   GEORGE CLARKE. I am a shoemaker and Manchester warehouseman: my dwelling-house is at No. 29, Stonecutter-street, where my wife keeps a shop. The prisoner and another man came there with each a basket, on the 6th of August - the prisoner asked for some half-boots to look at, and then said he would come again; the other man then said he would come again - my wife said to the prisoner."Where are those I showed you?" he said, "You put them into the glass case:" I said to him, "Let me look into your basket;" he set off and ran - I pursued; he dropped the basket, and it contained these boots; I took them up, and a neighbour stopped him - I gave him in charge.
