   JOHN BLANDEN sworn.
 I work with  John Edwards, a farrier, in Golden-lane; I worked at Mr. Whitbread's shop a fortnight or better, I cannot say the time: on the Friday before the horse died the prisoner and I were working together; he said to me, John, do you know what will poison a horse? I said, no, I cannot tell what will poison a horse, I had like to have poisoned myself once by taking mercury; Scott made answer, if I had taken arsenic instead of mercury it would have poisoned me or a horse either; soon after he asked Scott if he would buy him some arsenic: Scott said, if he wanted any he would. On the Saturday afternoon a horse was brought to have a shoe put on; I put it on; I had not nails enough, I went into the shop to point some nails, and saw the prisoner with a ladle before him on the forge; there was nobody at all in the shop; in the ladle there seemed to me to be a ball such as we give to horses that have colds; out of the drawer near the forge he took a paper with brown or yellow powder, and mixed it up together and made a ball, and put the ball he had been mixing in the ladle into a piece of paper, and laid it down upon the vice-bench: I went out to finish putting the shoe on; when I had finished the horse, he told me to go into the stable and put two old shoes on that horse that was lame; I went over with him; he got into the stable; I saw him go up to the horse's head, he had the ball in his hand; I do not know where he took it from, it was the very same ball he had in the ladle; he gave it to the horse; the horse made no resistance at all; then I turned him round to put the shoes on him; I went into the stable and laid hold of the ladle he had made the ball in, it was very nasty; I put it on the fire and wiped it, and put the paper in the fire; I said the ladle was nasty and smelt very strong; he said never mind it: on Monday morning when the horse was dead, he came into the shop where I was at work, and put his hands together and said, I would not have it known I gave the horse any thing for that full of gold; and held his two hands together in this manner (describing it): I never saw him give this horse any medicines in my life before.
   JOHN BUCKLE sworn.
 I had a horse of Mr. Whitbread's under my care with a fore leg: Mr. Edwards had him in hand, but could not cure him; he gave him up; I took him in hand and got him so well, that he could walk about; Edwards begged to have him again; he had him about a month; in which time he grew so bad that he flung him up again, and in nine days time I got him so well as to be able to go to grass; I was ordered to take some grass at Islington, and he was to come on Monday: upon Saturday morning I dressed the horse, and he was in full health; on Monday morning between four and five o'clock the horse-keeper called me up and told me the horse was dead; I went to the stable and found the horse dead; I clapped my hands on the flanks of the horse and said he was poisoned; then I looked behind at his burden, and he began to purge, and a great deal of stuff came from him: I would not go away till I saw the horse opened; they cut a gut and the stuff ran out; I desired to have the paunch out whole; I took the paunch out whole and opened it with my knife, and upon the point of my knife I saw this yellow arsenic, and the paper; it had been done up in the ball with the paper; it was digested, and there was the paper.
 Do you undertake to say the horse died of that and nothing else? - Nothing else at all; I think the man that bought it and pounded it for him was as deep in the mud as he was in the mire; they gave the horse two ounces, an ounce would have done.
 Was the horse taking any balls at this time? - None at all; the horse was in good health; this is the stuff (producing it) that I took from the horse; there is yellow arsenic among it: there was a dispute between Edwards and me about the horse; I do not believe the prisoner would have done it if he had not been bid.
