 Court. Gentlemen of the Jury, this trial has taken up a great deal of your time and and attention; but not one moment longer than justice requires; for you have been called upon this indictment to decide upon as important an enquiry as can well come before a Jury and Court of Justice: the prisoner is indicted for perjury; a crime in itself, and under all the circumstances, where it is wilfully and intentionally committed, with a corrupt intention, of very great magnitude; a crime that cuts up by the roots the only bond of confidence that can subsist between man and man, and the only security that we can have for the due administration of justice both between party and party, and between the public and those that are accused of the crimes; but if a perjury of itself, is a crime of such magnitude, when we consider the circumstances under which the present defendant stands charged with that crime it raises it to a height still greater; for the perjury that is charged against the defendant, is stated to have been by him committed upon the trial of two men for a capital felony, and the evidence given against these men, is charged to be false, and to have been knowingly and corruptly given; and therefore if that is true, the whole catalogue of crimes does not contain one of greater enormity, for though, in the denomination of law, such an evidence, if the event of it should be to take away the life of an innocent person, does not come under the legal description of murder: yet, in point of malignity, it seems to be a murder of the foulest die. The question therefore for you to decide this day is, whether the defendant has in defiance of the laws of God and man, committed in the face of his country, knowingly and corruptly, a perjury, with a view to take away the lives of two innocent persons; or whether an innocent man having appeared for the sake of public justice on a former occasion, stands charged with a crime of so much enormity: you therefore will not grudge your time and attention in this enquiry; it has been stated to you in the opening of this business, that from persuasion of the falsehood of the evidence given on the trial of two men, Newberry and Iverson, they have since, upon great deliberation, and with the intervention of those concerned in a judicial capacity, obtained the King's pardon for the offence: the first part of my duty therefore is to tell you that you ought to lay that out of the question; and not to let it make the smallest impression on your minds, for there might be just and wise reasons for that pardon, without the smallest imputation on the truth perhaps of all the witnesses, but certainly of Harvey's testimony; for though his evidence was necessary, yet the truth of that evidence is perfectly consistent with the innocence of both the prisoners; and that that circumstance may be wholly erased from your minds, I think it right to let you know that a conviction of truth or falsehood of Harvey's evidence never came under consideration, when that pardon was the subject of deliberation, but it arises from circumstances in which the account given by Lockell, with respect to himself and his own situation at the time, threw such a suspicion on his testimony, that it was thought unsafe to run the risque of executing two men on the evidence of a single witness, who stood impeached and
