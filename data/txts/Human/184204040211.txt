I have the charge of the vaules under the New Church at Chelsea, and keep the keys—there are three public vaults—a person named Hillyer is the constant grave-digger—the prisoner assisted him occasionally—Hillyer was often intrusted with the keys to get matting out, to cover the graves over, when he was going to dig a deep grave—he had only to go to a place just at the entry of the vaults, which was separated by an iron railing from the vaults—on the 23rd of March, there was to be a funeral in one of the public vaults at two o'clock—all the arrangements were made for it—the place had been previously selected where the coffin was to be placed—there was nothing to do, as to looking for the coffins of the relatives or anything of that sort—I gave the key to John  son, the gate-keeper, that day—he went down to sweep the place up, about an hour or so before the funeral took place—the prisoner had no business in the vault at all—he was not employed by me—there was nothing for him to do there—after the funeral, in consequence of something that transpired a search was made in the vaults, and we missed some handles and plates from the coffins—there was a brass plate gone from Mrs. Lucy Goddard's coffin. DANIEL JAMES JOHNSON. I am son of the sexton. About one o'clock on the 23rd of March, my father sent me to the vault where the funeral was to take place—I did not find any one in the vault the first time—the second time I went, about half-past one, I found the prisoner there in among the coffins, with a candle out of a candlestick—I asked what busi ness he had there—he said he was looking at the names on the coffins—he did not say what for—the candle then touched something, and went out—I do not know whether it was done by accident or for the purpose—the place was then dark, and I could not see what was going on—I went to fetch a light, and was gone five or ten minutes—he got out, and went to a flue to warm himself—he was not in the vault when I came sack. JOHN CLARKSON. I am a dealer in marine stores, and live in Col lege-street, Chelsea. On the 23rd of March, the prisoner came to my shop about the middle of the day, and brought 141bs. of lead, and 2lbs. and a half of brass—I paid him 10 d . for the plates—I did not examine it in his presence—when I first saw it had the appearance of brass bent and beat up in a roll, and doubled again—I opened it after he was gone, and found an inscription on it, with the name of "Lucy Goddard"—I should say it was a coffin-plate—I sold it again to Gatfield—I asked him his name and address—I had no conversation with him on that occasion—on Monday, the 28th March, after I had seen a bill, I stated what I had seen to the churchwardens.
   Cross-examined by MR. PAYNE . Q.  You saw the bill offering the re ward of 20 l .? A.  Yes—I expected the bill implied that the person who gave information was not to be punished—I do not know whether I should have stirred in the matter but for that—as far as I am concerned the re ward is but 5 l .—they have not promised me 5 l .—I do not expect to get the whole 20 l .—I sell Gatfield all my brass—he was at my place all that day—I keep the brass by me as long as it suits me to do so, sometimes a week, and sometimes not five minutes—it depends on when I am going to sell it—I did not keep this long—I did not deny to any one that I had received any coffin-plates, before the bill came out—I swear that—no one came to make any inquiries about coffin-plates before the bill came out—
