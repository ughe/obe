   COURT . Q.  How old are you? A.  Turned fourteen—this was my first employment on a railway. JESSE RUPP. I am a porter at Blackheath station—on the Friday after noon that that accident happened, Bartholomew told me to go to the prisoner at the signal-box and say that there was a train waiting at Charlton—I went to the prisoner's box door and told him so—he said, "Very well, I thought I heard three beats some little time ago"—he said nothing more—I left the box and came away—I did not see him use the instrument.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Was he perfectly sober? A.  Yes; I have never seen him the worse for liquor—when he said that he heard three beats, I said that I did not know anything about it—I am not paid by the company—I am not in their employ—I only take parcels. EDWARD DUNCAN CHAPMAN. I am station-master at Blackheath—I have been nearly eighteen years in the company's employ, and am perfectly acquainted with the working of the line—it is a rule that when a train is started a message should be sent from the starting station—in starting from Dart ford the station-master would send a message along the line which would be immediately conveyed to the signalman, and that train would be added to their list of expected trains, which was not done in this instance, and the ballast train became entirely dependant on signals from station to station—the prisoner had been porter for two years and four months—he had been working the signals altogether for about eight or nine days—he had been well taught before he was put into the signal box—every person in the com pany's employ is required to make himself acquainted with the rules—he had taken the duty of assistant signalman to help the day man when he went to dinner, and from 6 to 8 in the evening on several occasions when I had given one of the staff a holiday—he was my reserve man, and was par ticularly steady and sober, and one of the best men I ever had under my charge—his hours were from 7 in the morning to 6 in the evening with one hour for dinner—he had no other duties but signaling—this message which was sent from Charlton station by the porter was decidedly irregular; it should have been copied in writing and sent to the signalman so as to impress it more on his mind—they are not justified in attending to merely verbal messages, and if the prisoner had attended to this message he would have done wrong—the regulations are strict you must have a written message to act upon—I cannot understand how the man made a mistake—orders to the signalman may be given verbally by me but by no one else except the head porter when he is acting as my deputy—trains have been stopped in the tunnel and divided before—that arises from the wet dripping through and making the rails greasy and very often from atmospheric causes—it is often not so bad as at other times—it has happened many times since I have been at Blackheath—it has always been reported, and all precautions are supposed to be taken and rules are laid down which were considered sufficient—not taking so many trucks would decidedly be the remedy for it.
