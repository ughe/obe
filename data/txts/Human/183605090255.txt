THOMAS WHITEHEAD. I am a policeman. I went to the prisoner's house, on Monday, the 2nd of May, between two and three o'clock, at the request of Mr. Bedford—it is No.1, Mason-street, Lambeth—I did not take him into custody, at that time—Mr. Bedford and Mr. Barton went to get the magistrate's advice, and on their return, they gave the prisoner into custody, and about nine o'clock, I found this carpet in an upper room. WILLIAM CHAMBERS BARTON. I am the natural son of the late Mr. William Surman Chambers—I was in the habit of visiting him—I lived in one of his house—he had ten houses in Whitehorse-street, Lambeth—he lived at No. 20—I was acquainted with his articles of furniture—I was at the house two or three times every day, for about twelve or eighteen months, till he went into the country, which was in July, 1834, and he died on the 17th of September following—this carpet was down in his frosst room tip-stairs, which was the room he occupied—about a month before he left, he directed me to take this carpet up, beat it, and fold it up, and put it is the black-room, on the same floor, and I did so before he left—the prisoner lodged in the lower part of the house, and paid the rent to my father—my father generally slept in the front room, and made use of the back-room as a sitting-room—he had no servant—the prisoner occupied two rooms on the ground-floor, and the kitchen—I saw him on the 6th of October following—I went into the back-room, and saw this curpet is the same place as I had placed it in before my father went into the country—I picked the key up in the front room, and the prisoner went in and unlocked the door, and I then saw the carpet there—on the 2nd of May, last, I went with Mr. Bedford to the prisoner's house, in Mason-street, and saw the carpet in an empty top room, wrapped up—we asked the prisoner to allow us permission to see what furniture he had, as we believed he had some belonging to the late Mr. Chambers—he said he had not any belonging to him—I afterwards found the carpet, which I am positive was my father's property.
   Cross-examined by MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Had you lived with your father? A.  I lived in a house belonging to him—I never slept in my father's house—I did not visit him for about a fortnight before he left home—before that I was there two or three times a day—I had a quarrel with him about a fortnight before his death—I got into the police and left, by disobeying the orders of my superior officer—I was not discharged—I discharged myself—I would not swear that—I was removed—Mr. and Mrs. Hill were used to my father during his illness, as far as friendship went—Mrs. Hill used to go up and attend to him at times, but I used to nurse him myself.
   Q.  During the last fortnight you could not, for you were not allowed to see him? A.  I saw him the day he went into the country—I came to the house several times, and saw him, but was not friends with him—I do not know that Mrs. Hill was reproved for letting me in by stealth—I never heard the prisoner say that my father had made them a present of the things of their kindness to him—I never heard my father say so—I have heard the prisoner say that my father gave him the things, and he claimed there as his own.
