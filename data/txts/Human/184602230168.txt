Cross-examined. Q.  Was this taken down word for word as the woman made the statement? A.  It is the original statement taken down by me, word for word as it took place—she was not examined on any subsequent occasion. CHARLES COXHEAD. I live in Great Bath-street, Clerkenwell. On Sunday, the 25th of Jan., I lived at No. 2, Halford-terrace, Penton-place—Mrs. Cox is my servant. About twenty minutes or a quarter past seven o'clock I heard in the adjoining house a man and woman quarrelling and fighting in the pas sage—I distinctly heard two voices, male and female, but the parties are strangers to me—I know it came from No. 3—I heard a great deal of most foul and abusive language—I heard the man distinctly say, "D—n you, you will halloo murder again, will you," and I heard the woman immediately afterwards give a kind of shriek—they appeared to me then to retire into the front parlour—they appeared to be in the passage before—I went up stairs, and looked out at the first-floor window, and heard somebody call for assist ance, for the doctor, and police, and call murder, but I did not go in.
   Cross-examined. Q.  You keep the Ben Jonson? A.  Yes—Mrs. Cox was not with me when I heard this—I was in the front parlour—as soon as murder was called, there were five or six people collected—I was not flurried. CHARLES WILLIAM OTWAY. I am a surgeon, and live at No. 11, Canter bury-row. On Sunday, the 25th of Jan., I was called to No. 3, Halford terrace, and saw a woman lying in the front parlour—the prisoner was by her side, with his left hand on her throat, and his right hand in one of her hands—he desired me to come in, and said, "You see what this has come to, Mr. Otway"—(I had been called in two days before on account of a quarrel which had taken place in the family, and I then gave them some advice, and left them)—I had the woman removed on to the bed, and examined her throat—it was a short, clean wound, about an inch and a half long, half sepa rating the windpipe—blood was issuing from it, and also passing down the throat—it was such a wound as would be likely to be caused by such an instrument as this (a razor)—she did not appear quite sensible when I first saw her—I dressed the wound after some time—the prisoner was very much agitated during the whole time when the expressions were made use of by her friends—the wife made signs to me, intended to lead me to the opinion that she did it herself, and she several times shook hands with her husband, and caressed him—after she recovered a little, she spoke to me, but she could only speak in a whisper—I said if she had anything to say, she had better write it down, as she ought not to speak, but she said, "I did it myself"—she repeated it in a low tone—a paper was given to her, and she wrote on it—this is it—she at first wrote—( reads ) "I did it myself through distress of mind; pray, my dear Fred., take care of our dear Arthur, for your poor Ann's sake; will you be father and mother for ever; do not charge my mother and brother with anything, they are innocent"—she signed that in the presence of the po liceman and myself, at our request—I said to her, "Your mother and brother are not charged with anything; you have heard what your mother hat said, have you anything to say about your husband?"—she in great excitement wished to write again—I had the brother and mother, with the policeman, present, and she wrote these words, "Nor my husband," in their presence.
   Q.  What is your opinion as to the mode in which the wound was in flicted? A.  I consider a razor must have been taken, and the head thrown back so as to protrude the throat, and the razor just pushed there—I think if a man had taken the razor, he would take a sweep as if cutting his own throat, but a female would handle a razor in a very different manner.
