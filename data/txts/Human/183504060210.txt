   Cross-examined. Q.  What are you? A.  A master bricklayer—Gar dener is a wharfinger, in the Mile-end-road—the bricks were landed on his wharf, and part of them were sold to him in payment of his account for the wharfage—I do not know when Moore took the ground in Sidney-square, to build the houses—he was about it a long time—I began the buildings at the latter end of November, or beginning of December.
   MR. PAYNE addressed the Court and Jury on behalf of Moore .
   Grobecker's Defence.  I did not acknowledge myself in any way to be the ground-landlord—the word, "ground-landlord," was not mentioned at the time of my interview with the prosecutor, and Sidney-square could not have been mentioned—it was not in contemplation to build houses there then—Moore had no notion of building houses there till after the commencement of November—the agreement for the land there is dated 17th of November, and the building did not take place till the end of November, or the beginning of December—I could not be a party to any representation—I can prove that the word, "ground-landlord," was not used at the conversation—I can not say I have many witnesses, as I did not anticipate that out of so straightforward a transaction any charge of a criminal nature could arise—I might imagine I could not pay the bills, and be arrested, but could not imagine a proceeding of a criminal nature would be brought against me, and therefore I have not evidence as to all the conversation which took place at the interview; however, I have evidence of what did take place, and though it may be received with suspicion, still I will call that evidence. WILLIAM GROBECKER. Jun. I remember Mr. Griange and Mr. Moore coming to my father, in September last. As near as I can recollect, it was about the 26th—I was in the dining parlour, on the ground floor, near the window, which is three or four feet from the ground—the window was open—I heard part of the conversation which took place—I heard a knock at the door—the servant went to the door—I immediately afterwards heard some body passing through the passage—I saw my father approach the door—Moore and Griange went out of the back door, and met my father under the window—Moore introduced Mr. Griange, who said, "Mr. Grobecker, my business is very short with you; Mr. Moore has seen bricks in my field, which he seems to think will suit him to perform a building, which he has contracted to do"—he then said, "Mr. Grobecker, have you any objection to become security for these bricks?"—my father said, "No; I consider myself quite secure"—he then inquired what credit was to be given—Mr. Griange said, "Two months"—my father said, "That is short; cannot you make it three,—or I think it will be better to make it three, and then all will be right?"—Mr. Griange said, "We will see about that, Mr. Grobecker," and they then left.
   COURT . Q.  What are you? A.  Clerk to an estate-agent.
   MR. PHILLIPS . Q.  Have you been living at home with your father long? A.  Yes; about seven months—since that I have been backwards and forwards from home—I was living in his house in 1829—I was absent a few months, about seven months ago—I have heard the name of Luttrell—there was an old gentleman, named Luttrell, used to come backwards and forwards to the house—there was some circumstance between my father and him, I do not know what.
