The boy mentioned nothing of Peter's stopping at the side of the door to listen; Brookes also contradicts the boy; the boy says they were upon the stairs near the bottom of the stairs, when he and Newberry went in, and that he saw them the moment he came to the door; the other witness, Mr. Stones, says, they were in the lower room, and this Mrs. Brooks says they were quite upstairs out of sight when Peter came, and that they did not come down stairs till Harvey was called down; another important contradiction is, that if these men came but once, the whole story told by the boy and the father, which is so contradictory in every other respect, must be a fabrication as to the twice coming; here are seven witnesses on the part of the prosecution, who all positively swear, that the man was taken in the house, but in relating the circumstances under which he was taken, it is hardly possible to conceive greater variety in the examination of witnesses, or in the different parts of the examination of the same witnesses. You are to judge whether you think these contradictions could occur in a story that was absolutely true, whether they arise from witnesses speaking to different facts, or whether they arise from witnesses coming to speak one fact, and not being prepared for questions that they have been forced to answer, you will judge. The general observation is with respect to the propriety of the whole story, whether it is likely that this man Lockell, in search of a man, truly or falsely, that he said had robbed him, should go and knock at a door he knew was open. No observation can arise against the defendant from the evidence of Ann Holt, but if it did make any impression, it must be the other way; there are witnesses who are called to confirm the evidence originally given by Harvey at the Old Bailey; you are to judge whether you think it likely that a man of that character, for the mere hope of sharing in the reward of blood, should have been wilfully guilty of such a crime; you will take the whole into your consideration; you will take the positive evidence and the contradiction, and whether that is to be imputed to the defect of memory or to fabrication; if you are satisfied that this is not a story against the defendant, but that in point of fact the man was really taken in the house notwithstanding all the contradictions of their evidence; in that case it is a crime of an enormous magnitude, and loudly calls for punishment. If on the other hand, you think that the circumstances of the case, together with the evidence that has been called in support of the testimony of the defendant, though not so numerous as the witnesses for the prosecution, yet tell a story more probable and consistent in itself, and without those contradictions which occur in the evidence for the prosecution, in that case you will find a satisfaction in acquitting the defendant, and restoring him to that credit which he has formerly enjoyed, and it is to be sure a most painful consideration, on the one hand, that a man of an extraordinary character should have been for a very small temptation, guilty of a crime of the blackest die; or that on the other hand, a most wicked conspiracy has been framed falsely to accuse him of that offence.
 The Jury retired for about ten minutes and returned with a verdict.
     NOT GUILTY.
 Court. Gentlemen of the Jury, I am to thank you for the very great attention you have paid in the course of a long and intricate inquiry, and I most entirely concur with the verdict you have given, as it is perfectly consistent with my opinion.
