He was financial manager, but he did not assist the directors in making up the 7 per cent. dividends, and as to the £48,200, which was included in the balance-sheet, he did not know who suggested it. The accounts were audited by a Mr. Weller, the directors taking the shares with regard to the item at their "face" value.
   Lambert, in his defence, stated on oath that he first started in business with his father as a hosier at Nottingham; that he became aferwards con nected unth Mr. Number, the cycle manufacturer, and was a partner in that business, into which he put £5,000; that he was introduced to Beall and Wain in 1893, but did not know Singleton; that he was asked to find a responsible person to become a trustee of the bank, and approached Lord Waterpark, whose letter he regarded as a consent to act, and handed Lord Waterpark's letter to Beall; that there was every reason to believe that the com  pany would prove a great success, as there were many persons anxious to borrow money; that he did not know anything about the allotment of the founders' shares; that he regularly attended the meetings of the bank, at which Beall was present as financial manager; that with regard to the resolutions that were passed he acted in good faith; that at the time of the winding up he was a creditor of the bank to the extent of£69; that except as fees as a director, he had not received, either directly or indirectly, a single shilling from the bank, and that the 7 per cent, dividend was suggested by Beall; that the figure £48,200 was not intended to represent the actual value of the shares it referred to, and if he had any reason to doubt Beall's good faith he would certainly not have joined the bank; that beyond asking Beall, he did not take any steps to ascertain whether the statements contained in the prospectus were true, and that he had acted in good faith throughout the whole of his transactions with the bank; that the City of London destroyed its refuse by cremation, but did not make use of the power obtained in the process; and that if the Corporation used this patent they might light half the City for nothing.
   Wain, in his defence, stated that until the Magisterial investigation he did not know anything of Singleton, neither had he any knowledge of the "Financial Gazette," of the signatories to the memorandum of association, or of tht issue of the founders' shares of the bank, or of Lloyd Morgan and Co,; that he did not receive a penny in connection with the promotion of the company; that the failure of the business in Scotland was due in part to the attacks made on the bank and its officials by certain newspapers; that the statements in the prospectus were correct; that he considered the patent acquired by the Refuse Company as a very valuable one; that all their energies were devoted to make it a success; that Beall, Lambert, Mr. Priestley, and himself found the money to keep the company alive; that he did not believe that F.
