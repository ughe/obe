I live in Union-place, Paddington, and keep a stall in Paddington-market. I was at the Adam and Eve on the evening in ques tion, in a room where some persons were changing—Foulkes was in there—I was getting a little too much beer—there were many people round me, but who I do not know—Foulkes had caught hold of me by the arm, and made me sit down by the side of him—I remained with him ten or twelve minutes—we had a pint of half-and-half together, and then I left the place—I left Foulkes behind me. Witness for the Defence . WILLIAM GEORGE. I am a newsvender, and live in Duke-street, Broad way, Westminster. I know Foulkes by sight—I should be very sorry to be acquainted with him—I was at the Adam and Eve on Monday night, the 15th of February—I went about eight o'clock, and staid till a quarter to twelve o'clock—Foulkes came in—Norris was there that night—he goes by the nick-name of Billy Button—the prosecutor asked him to toss for a crown—I am positive of that—Norris would not toss with him, as he had got no money—he gave that reason—Foulkes seemed rather out of temper—he called me a b—thief—I had only asked him to keep quiet, as a gentleman was about to sing—Roberts was sitting in the same box with me, and the prosecutor was in the next box—Roberts offered the prosecutor his pint to drink to keep quiet, and he received a blow in the face for it, and he called him a b—counterfeit—on my oath, I saw him strike him—I was elevated in the room, as I was in the chair—Roberts left his seat, but I went in and kept him in the box to prevent a disturbance—the land lord came in and ordered Foulkes out—he said, "You must leave my room; it is not to be annoyed by you," and he left the room and got to the bar—there were several persons there—I understood they were about to loss—I did not see any thing of that—it was after the prosecutor had been there some time I left the parlour and went to the bar, and he there chal lenged Mr. Cordwent to toss him for a sovereign—he said he would not loss—Foulkes said, "Then I will toss you for 10 s .," and he put his hand in his pocket to see if he could find money to toss with, he pulled out some silver, and Cordwent declined then to toss—he then said to me (I was next to him). "I will bet you half-a-crown Mr. Cordwent has not 5 s . in his possession"—it is a thing I am not in the habit of doing, but I certainly did bet that night—Cordwent was desired to produce 5 s . If he had it, to get the wager—he did produce more than 5 s .—I did not get the money—I have not had it at all—the house was at last cleared by the landlord—he was obliged to expel Foulkes from the bar—he ordered him out—when he was going out he said, "Never mind, I will lay wait"—he was drunk, but knew what he was about—I saw him again that night, about one o'clock, with three women—two of them I knew to he had characters, girls of the town, and one I did not know—he said he would give them some gin if the house was open—I came up just at the time he made use of those words.
