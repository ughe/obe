Cross-examined. Q.  You saw a Mr. Franks there? A.  Yes—he went with me into the coach-house, and pointed out the coke—he said the sack which was three parts full belonged to Brewer, and the rest belonged to him, and he had bought it of Robinson, and paid for it, who said he gave 1 s . 3 d . a sack for it at the Gas-works—I went to the Gas-works, and saw some men who said they recollected his taking one sack—I found that when a man goes there for orders, it is very common for people outside to add to their orders, and they recollected he had had on one occasion thirteen sacks instead of twelve, because they would not sell less than three sacks—I examined the corn-bin, I did not find any other piece of paper in it—there were more oats amongst the chaff in the bin than there were in the bulk at Mr. Grissell's—Brewer came in and claimed the coke in the sack—I saw the prisoner's horses fed from the nose-bags at the Swan in the morning—I did not see what he did with the nose-bags afterwards—I was down the yard at the time he took them from the horses—they generally leave what food remains in the nose-bag, and buckle it on the shafts—I never saw them empty out what remains in the bag, and re-fill it. EDWIN BENJAMIN GAMMON. I am clerk to Mr. Thomas Grissell—the prisoner was in his employ as carman, to take things to the buildings—on Tuesday, 16th Nov., I received from Attride from fifty to a hundred small pieces of paper rolled up—this is one of them—it has T.G., and a cross on it, and is Attride's writing—I threw the pieces of paper in the corn bin with the mixed food of oats, split beans, and clover-chaff—the beans were Egyptian beans, and from that bin I saw the prisoner fill his nose-bags, and then take about a bushel and a half in a sack—that was not regular—he should have gone to the man for that—he would have been allowed to have some for his horses during the day—I have seen the beans produced from the bin at the Swan—they were Egyptian beans—there was clover-chaff in that, and a small quantity of meadow-hay, but none in ours—when the officer brought the prisoner to me, he asked if I would allow him to remain at home that night—I told him I had no authority to do so, as he had been seen to leave his corn two days—he said he had left a little, but it should not occur again—I told him I was very sorry he had been so foolish, after being with us so many years.
   Cross-examined. Q.  Had you been asking him about his leaving coke? A.  I think I also told him he had left coke, and had left his corn two days, and then he said he had left some, or left a little—we do not allow our men to buy and sell coke, it would be contrary to our orders—we allow the prisoner and other men to take home a sack or two in their cart when they have done work—I put the papers in the bin about a quarter past six o'clock in the morning, and about half-past six the corn was taken out—I saw the prisoner return home on Wednesday—nothing had been said to him when he went out on Thursday—he had brought home some feed on Thursday, but none on Tuesday or Wednesday. NOT GUILTY .
