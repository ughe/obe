   COURT . Q.  From October till January he was under no restraint? A.  No; I wished a person to watch him—he was not watched at Mr. Brees's, that I am aware of—I did not know any thing of him from January—at times I do not think he knew right from wrong.
   MR. BREES re-examined . I never thought him insane—except when he was in liquor I did not think he was quite right in his mind; but when he was soiber I thought he was very well.
   MR. NELSON . I am a surgeon, and have been in the Navy. I have known the prisoner since May, or the commencement of June, when he came from Birmingham—I have had opportunities of seeing him from that time, and consider him insane, in the most lucid interval that he had—on the 5th or 6th of October I was called in at the request of his brother, and found him in a state of lunacy, delirious—I have observed that more par ticularly when he has had any thing to drink—from one to two glasses would have that effect on him—I have observed him when not under the influence of liquor, and have observed incoherency, or want of connexion of ideas, and want of memory—I considered him partially unsound in mind at that period.
   Q.  Would it not expose him to be the dupe of any designing person for the purpose of uttering an order, or any thing of that sort? A.  Un doubtedly.
   COURT . Q.  You say he was worse when in liquor? A.  Undoubtedly—he was given to liquor—I do not say he was a drunkard—I should say he liked liquor—I have seen him frequently intoxicated, but one glass would make him so—I believe when under the influence of liquor he did not know right from wrong—he should have been put into some place of restraint—I proposed it to his brother—in fact, I went to St. Luke's to got him in there, and left with his brother the circulars that I got from there—I know when that state, if I had given him a candle he would have eat it instead of bread, or drank soap-suds instead of water—I have not seen him for three or four months. HENRY DUDLEY. I am a policeman. I was on duty in Greenwich-park, on the 10th of October, and observed the prisoner behaving very strangely—I watched him for a considerable time—he would sometimes walk smart, and all on a sudden would stop, open his coat, and button it again—I watched him into Charlton-lane, and asked him if he was out for a walk—he said he was, and that he had been among the farmers all day, walking with them—I asked if he had been walking in the park—he said he had not, but he should like to be there—I took him into the park—he did not seem to know he had been there—he talked wildly, and said there was a dispute in the firm his family had been in—most of them had been shot in consequence of some duels, and he thought he was the only one left—I then took him to the station-house—he did not seem to have the least fear about him—he would not give me his name—I found in his pocket a letter with the address of his lodging, in Kennington-street—Sergeant King took the charge, and agreed with me that he was insane—I went to his lodging, and found where his brother lived—he did not seem alarmed,
